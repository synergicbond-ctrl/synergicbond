10. Kinetic isotope effects as evidence for E2

Replacing the β-hydrogen by deuterium makes the C–D bond stronger (lower zero-point energy), so a reaction in which the C–H bond is broken in the rate-determining step is slower for the deuterated substrate. The ratio kH/kD is the kinetic isotope effect. A primary isotope effect is normally clearly greater than 1 (several units at room temperature), while a value near 1 means the C–H bond is not broken in the slow step.

  • E2: the C–H bond breaks in the single rate-determining step, so kH/kD is clearly greater than 1.
  • E1: the slow step is ionization; the C–H bond breaks only afterwards, so kH/kD is close to 1.
  • E1cB, rate-limiting leaving-group loss: proton transfer is a fast pre-equilibrium, so kH/kD is close to 1 and hydrogen/deuterium exchange of the substrate is faster than elimination.
  • SN2: no C–H bond is broken, so no primary effect.
  1. Prepare the substrate with deuterium on every β-carbon (for example CD3CH(Br)CD3 from 2-bromopropane).
  2. Measure the rate constants with the same base under the same conditions.
  3. Compute kH/kD. A clear primary effect points to C–H cleavage in the rate-determining step (E2).

An intramolecular competition gives the same information within one molecule. In CD3CHBrCH3, the base can remove H from the CH3 group or D from the CD3 group. Both groups have three equivalent hydrogens, so the ratio of the two propenes (CD3CH=CH2 to CD2=CHCH3) equals kH/kD.

Key idea. A large kH/kD proves that the C–H bond is being broken in the slow step; together with second-order kinetics it identifies E2.
