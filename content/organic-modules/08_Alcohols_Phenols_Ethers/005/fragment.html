2. Physical properties and hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding. The O–H bond is strongly polarised; the H of one molecule is attracted to a lone pair on the oxygen of the next. An O–H···O hydrogen bond is worth roughly 20 kJ mol−1, which is small compared with a covalent bond (about 460 kJ mol−1 for O–H) but large compared with van der Waals forces between small molecules (1–5 kJ mol−1). Alcohols associate in chains and rings; this single fact controls boiling point, solubility, viscosity and the infrared O–H band.

Key idea Boiling point order for isomers: 1° alcohol > 2° alcohol > 3° alcohol > ether > alkane of equal mass is lowest. For hydrocarbon isomers the more branched has the lower bp (smaller surface area). Among alcohols of the same formula the straight chain has the largest surface area and hence the highest bp.
Why alcohols boil much higher than ethers and alkanes of the same mass. Ethers cannot donate a hydrogen bond (no O–H); their molecules attract each other only by dipole–dipole and dispersion forces. Alkanes have only dispersion forces. Alcohols must break hydrogen bonds on vaporisation.
Solubility in water. Methanol, ethanol and the propanols are miscible in all proportions because the OH group forms hydrogen bonds with water and the alkyl part is small. Solubility falls as the hydrophobic alkyl chain grows: butan-1-ol dissolves to about 8 g per 100 mL, pentan-1-ol about 2 g, hexan-1-ol about 0.6 g, and higher alcohols are practically insoluble. For isomers, branching increases solubility (2-methylpropan-2-ol is miscible with water in all proportions, whereas butan-1-ol is not) because branching reduces the hydrophobic surface area. Ethers are only slightly soluble (diethyl ether about 7 g per 100 mL) because they can accept hydrogen bonds from water but not donate; ethers of low molar mass such as THF and 1,4-dioxane are fully miscible.
Other facts used in questions
  • Methanol, ethanol, propanol are colourless liquids with a characteristic smell; higher members up to C11 are oily; higher still, solids.
  • Ethanol forms an azeotrope with water (95.6 % ethanol by mass, bp 78.2 °C), so it cannot be dried beyond that by simple distillation. Absolute alcohol is prepared by adding benzene (ternary azeotrope) or by quicklime followed by distillation, or by molecular sieves.
  • “Rectified spirit” is 95 % ethanol; “denatured alcohol” has methanol, pyridine or a dye added to make it unfit to drink and thereby free of excise duty.
  • Methanol is toxic: it is oxidised in the body by alcohol dehydrogenase to methanal and then methanoic acid, which damage the optic nerve (blindness) and cause acidosis; ethanol competes for the enzyme, which is why it is used as an antidote.
  • Alcohols have a broad IR band at 3200–3550 cm−1 (H-bonded O–H) and a sharper band near 3600 cm−1 in dilute solution (free O–H); C–O stretch near 1050–1200 cm−1.
Example 3 L1 Foundation

Boiling point order

Arrange in increasing boiling point: butan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol, diethyl ether, pentane, butan-2-ol.

Show solution
  1. Pentane (72 g mol-1) has only dispersion forces: bp 36 °C. Diethyl ether (74) has a small dipole and cannot H-bond to itself: bp 35 °C. So these two are the lowest; pentane and diethyl ether are practically equal, with ether 34.6 °C and pentane 36.1 °C.
  2. Among the three butanols all H-bond. Branching reduces the surface area and the strength of H-bonding: 2-methylpropan-2-ol (82 °C) < butan-2-ol (100 °C) < butan-1-ol (118 °C).
  3. Order: diethyl ether < pentane < 2-methylpropan-2-ol < butan-2-ol < butan-1-ol (ether and pentane are close; the quoted values are 34.6 and 36.1 °C).
Example 4 L1 Foundation

Solubility

Explain why butan-1-ol is much less soluble in water than methanol, and why 2-methylpropan-2-ol is more soluble than butan-1-ol.

Show solution
  1. Each alcohol molecule dissolves by forming H-bonds with water through its OH group but must also push apart water molecules to make room for its alkyl chain (hydrophobic effect).
  2. Methanol has a one-carbon hydrocarbon part, so the H-bonding gain dominates: miscible. Butan-1-ol has a four-carbon chain; the hydrophobic penalty dominates: only partial solubility (about 8 g/100 mL).
  3. 2-Methylpropan-2-ol has the same four carbons but they are packed in a sphere; its hydrocarbon surface area is smaller than that of the linear chain, so the penalty is smaller, and it is miscible with water.
