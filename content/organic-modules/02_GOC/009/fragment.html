8. The mesomeric (resonance) effect

8.1 What it is

A mesomeric effect (M, also written R) is the permanent polarisation transmitted through a conjugated π-system by the delocalisation of π-electrons or of a lone pair. Unlike the inductive effect it does not fade with every bond: it is carried along the conjugated path and alters the electron density at alternate atoms of the chain or ring.

  • +M (+R): the group donates electrons into the π-system. The atom attached to the π-system has a lone pair (or a negative charge), which is pushed into the π-system. Examples: –NH₂, –OH, –OR, –O⁻, –NHCOCH₃, –X (halogen).
  • −M (−R): the group withdraws electrons from the π-system. The atom attached to the π-system is part of a multiple bond to a more electronegative atom (or carries a positive charge/empty orbital), and the π-electrons are pulled into it. Examples: –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR, –COOH, –COOR, –SO₃H, –CONH₂.

8.2 Positions affected: ortho, para, not meta

The curved-arrow pushing of a donor on benzene places the negative charge at the ortho and para carbons of the ring; an acceptor places the positive charge at ortho and para (the meta carbons are skipped in every contributor). This is the reason ortho/para directors and meta directors exist in electrophilic substitution, and the reason ortho/para substituents change the acidity of phenols and benzoic acids much more than meta substituents.

Exam Trap Halogens are the exception to remember. For F, Cl, Br and I, the −I effect is stronger than the +M effect, so they make the ring less reactive than benzene (deactivating); but the +M effect, which operates only at the ortho and para positions, makes those positions less deactivated than meta, so they still direct o/p. For acidity of substituted benzoic acids and phenols the net effect of the halogen is electron-withdrawing at every position, with para weaker than meta because +M opposes −I most at para (e.g. p-chlorobenzoic acid pKₐ 3.99 vs m-chloro 3.83).

Order of +M donor strength

Arrange the groups in decreasing +M effect: –NH₂, –OH, –F, –NHCOCH₃, –OCH₃, –O⁻.

  1. A lone pair on a less electronegative atom is donated more readily; a charged oxygen is a better donor than a neutral one; a lone pair already tied up with a carbonyl (amide, ester) is a weaker donor.

8.4 Conjugation: three varieties and cross-conjugation

  • π–π conjugation: alternating double and single bonds (1,3-butadiene, benzene, acrolein CH₂=CH–CH=O). The delocalisation involves p-orbitals on adjacent π-bonds.
  • p–π conjugation (lone pair or empty p orbital next to a π-bond): vinyl chloride, aniline, allyl cation CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺, allyl anion, enolates.
  • σ–π conjugation (hyperconjugation): a C–H or C–C σ-bond adjacent to a π-bond, empty p orbital or radical (propene, toluene, ethyl cation).
  • Cross-conjugation: one atom/group is conjugated to two other π-systems that are not conjugated with each other. In divinyl ketone CH₂=CH–CO–CH=CH₂ and in benzophenone the carbonyl is conjugated with each vinyl or aryl group but the two do not communicate through the central carbon as well as through a linear chain; cross-conjugated systems delocalise less than linear systems.

8.5 Consequences of the mesomeric effect

  • Acidity of phenol and carboxylic acids: phenoxide and carboxylate are stabilised by delocalisation of the negative charge.
  • Basicity of amides, anilines: the lone pair on N is delocalised and so not available to bind H⁺.
  • Orientation in aromatic substitution.
  • Bond lengths: C–N in amides (133 pm) shorter than in amines (147 pm); C–O in phenol (136 pm) shorter than in methanol (143 pm); C–Cl in vinyl chloride (172 pm) shorter than in ethyl chloride (177 pm).
  • Dipole moments: the large dipole of p-nitroaniline (6.2 D) is more than the vector sum (5.5 D) because the +M of NH₂ and −M of NO₂ reinforce through the ring (push–pull).
  • Colour: extended conjugation shifts absorption to the visible (azo dyes, indicators like methyl orange, push–pull chromophores).
  • Reactivity of carbonyls: acid chlorides and aldehydes are reactive; amides and carboxylates are unreactive because the donor group conjugates with C=O.
Illustration 8.5 Adv

Push–pull

Explain why the C–N bond in p-nitroaniline has more double-bond character than that in aniline and why p-nitroaniline is a much weaker base than aniline.

  1. NH₂ is a +M donor and NO₂ a −M acceptor, placed at positions that are conjugated with each other (para). The lone pair on N can flow through the ring directly onto the oxygens of the nitro group (the quinoid contributor), giving the C–N bond extra double-bond character.
  2. The N lone pair is therefore delocalised more than in aniline, is less available for H⁺ and p-nitroaniline (pKₐ of conjugate acid 1.0) is a far weaker base than aniline (4.6).
