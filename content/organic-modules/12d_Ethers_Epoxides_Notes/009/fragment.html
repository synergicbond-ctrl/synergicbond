10. Epoxides: preparation

Epoxides arise by oxidation of alkenes or by ring closure of halohydrins. A peroxycarboxylic acid such as meta-chloroperoxybenzoic acid (mCPBA) transfers an oxygen atom to the alkene in a single concerted step ('butterfly' transition state). The reaction is a syn addition and is stereospecific: a cis alkene gives a cis-epoxide and a trans alkene gives a trans-epoxide.

Reactant:

Reagent: mCPBA, CH2Cl2

Product:

Cyclohexene oxide (an achiral meso compound).

The halohydrin route has two steps. Treating an alkene with Br2 or NBS in water gives a bromohydrin by anti addition (water attacks the more substituted carbon of the bromonium ion). A base then deprotonates the OH group and the alkoxide displaces bromide in an intramolecular SN2 reaction, which closes the three-membered ring.

Reactant:

Reagent: Br2, H2O

Product:

1-Bromopropan-2-ol.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOH

Product:

Methyloxirane (propylene oxide).

Key idea. Ring closure needs the alkoxide oxygen and the leaving group anti-periplanar (180°). In a cyclohexane this means both must be axial, which is possible for the trans-2-halocyclohexanol but not for the cis isomer. Only the trans halohydrin gives cyclohexene oxide readily.

Ethylene oxide is made on an industrial scale by direct air oxidation of ethene over a silver catalyst. Nucleophilic epoxidation of electron-poor alkenes such as enones uses alkaline hydrogen peroxide. The Sharpless asymmetric epoxidation, which uses titanium(IV) isopropoxide, tert-butyl hydroperoxide and a chiral tartrate ester, converts allylic alcohols into epoxy alcohols of high enantiomeric purity; the choice of (+)- or (−)-tartrate decides which enantiomer is made. Only this descriptive knowledge is required here.
