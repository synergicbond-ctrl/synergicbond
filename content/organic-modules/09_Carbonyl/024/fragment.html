20. Keto–enol tautomerism

A carbonyl compound with at least one α-hydrogen exists in equilibrium with its enol form. The two are tautomers (constitutional isomers that interconvert by movement of a proton and a double bond, not resonance forms).

⇌ H⁺ or OH⁻⟶
Keto form (left) and enol form (right) of propanone. The equilibrium lies overwhelmingly on the keto side

For simple aldehydes and ketones the keto form is more stable by about 45–60 kJ mol-1, because a C=O double bond (plus a C–H) is stronger than a C=C double bond (plus an O–H). The enol fraction is tiny:

Key idea Factors that raise enol content: (1) conjugation of the enol C=C with another C=O or aryl; (2) intramolecular hydrogen bonding (six-membered chelate, favoured in non-polar solvents, destroyed by water); (3) aromaticity of the enol (phenols); (4) gem-disubstitution effects. Factors that lower it: electron-withdrawing groups on carbon that destabilise the C=C, and ring strain.

20.1 Catalysis: acid and base Enolisation is slow without catalyst, fast with acid or base.

  • Acid-catalysed: protonation of O gives an oxocarbenium-type cation (C=O+H), then a weak base (H2O, solvent) removes the α-H, forming the enol.
  • Base-catalysed: a base removes the α-H directly, giving the enolate ion (resonance hybrid of carbanion and enolate-oxygen forms); protonation on O gives the enol, protonation on C gives back the ketone.
OH⁻, −H₂O⟶ H₂O⟶
Base-catalysed: enolate is the key intermediate, protonation at oxygen gives the enol

20.2 Experimental evidence for enol/enolate

  • Deuterium exchange: ketones with α-H exchange all α-hydrogens with D in D2O/NaOD (or D+): acetone gives CD3COCD3 (6 exchangeable H); butanone 5; acetophenone 3; benzophenone 0 (no α-H).
  • Racemisation: an optically active ketone with a stereocentre at the α-carbon (e.g. (S)-sec-butyl phenyl ketone, PhCOCH(CH3)CH2CH3) racemises in acid or base, because the enol/enolate is planar and achiral at that carbon.
  • Halogenation kinetics: rate of bromination (or iodination) of acetone is independent of halogen concentration and equals the rate of enolisation (rate = k[ketone][H+] in acid): the halogen reacts after the slow step.
Shortcut Bredt exceptions: an α-H on a bridgehead carbon of a bicyclic ketone (such as C-1 of bicyclo[2.2.1]heptan-2-one, norcamphor) cannot be removed, because the enol or enolate would put a C=C at a bridgehead of a small bicycle (anti-Bredt, too strained). Such α-H do not exchange, do not undergo aldol or halogenation.
Example 22 Main

Counting exchangeable hydrogens

How many hydrogens are exchanged when each of these is heated with NaOD/D2O: (a) butanone, (b) acetophenone, (c) cyclohexanone, (d) PhCH2COCH3, (e) 2,2-dimethylpropanal, (f) benzaldehyde?

Show solution
  1. Count the α-hydrogens (those on carbons directly bonded to C=O).
  2. (a) CH3COCH2CH3: 3 + 2 = 5. (b) CH3COPh: 3. (c) Cyclohexanone: 2 + 2 = 4. (d) PhCH2COCH3: 2 + 3 = 5. (e) (CH3)3CCHO: the α-carbon is quaternary: 0 (the CHO hydrogen is not α). (f) 0.
  3. Note: the aldehydic H is never exchanged by this route.
Example 23 Adv

Racemisation

Which of these loses optical activity on treatment with dilute NaOH: (a) (R)-3-methylpentan-2-one, (b) (R)-2-methylbutanal, (c) (R)-2-methylbutan-1-ol, (d) (S)-3-methylcyclohexanone, (e) (R)-4-methylhexan-3-one?

Show solution
  1. A stereocentre racemises only if it is an α-carbon that carries an H. (a) C-3 is α to C=O and bears H: racemises. (b) C-2 is α and bears H: racemises. (c) An alcohol: no acidic H at the stereocentre, no. (d) In 3-methylcyclohexanone the stereocentre is β to C=O (enolisation involves C-2 and C-6): no. (e) C-4 is α and bears H: racemises.
