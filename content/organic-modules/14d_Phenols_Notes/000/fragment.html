1. Structure of phenol and resonance in phenol and phenoxide

Phenol, C6H5OH, has the hydroxyl group bonded directly to an sp2 carbon of a benzene ring. The oxygen is itself close to sp2 in character, and one of its lone pairs sits in a p-type orbital parallel to the ring π system. Overlap of this lone pair with the ring places partial double-bond character on the C–O bond: the C–O distance in phenol (about 136 pm) is shorter than in methanol (about 143 pm), and the O–H bond is correspondingly easier to ionise.

Because the oxygen donates electron density into the ring (+R effect), phenol is drawn as a resonance hybrid of the neutral Kekulé forms and three charge-separated forms in which a negative charge sits on an ortho or para carbon and oxygen carries a positive charge. The positions bearing the extra electron density are exactly the ortho and para carbons, which explains the ortho/para orientation of electrophilic substitution. The meta carbons never carry the charge.

Phenol (left) and the phenoxide ion (right).

In the phenoxide ion the oxygen carries a full negative charge and its lone pair is delocalised even more effectively, because there is no positive oxygen in the delocalised forms. Counting the Kekulé forms with the charge on oxygen (2) and the forms with the charge on one ortho carbon (2 positions) or the para carbon (1 position), the phenoxide ion has 5 contributing structures.

Phenoxide with the negative charge on oxygen, on an ortho carbon, and on the para carbon (the second Kekulé form of each is omitted).

  1. Step 1: the oxygen lone pair is conjugated with the ring π cloud.
  2. Step 2: electron density is pushed to C-2, C-4 and C-6, never to C-3 or C-5.
  3. Step 3: the delocalisation lowers the energy of the phenoxide ion more than of the neutral phenol, so ionisation of O–H is favoured relative to an alcohol.
Key idea. Two consequences follow from one effect. The conjugation makes the ring very electron-rich at o/p positions (strong activation in electrophilic substitution) and stabilises the conjugate base (acidity greater than alcohols). The same conjugation shortens and strengthens the C–O bond, so the OH of a phenol is not replaced by halide with HX.
