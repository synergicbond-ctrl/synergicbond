4. Metamerism

Metamerism is a special case of isomerism found in compounds that have a polyvalent functional group (a heteroatom or carbonyl carbon that joins two alkyl groups): ethers, ketones, esters, secondary and tertiary amines, and thioethers. The molecular formula and the functional group are the same, but the alkyl groups attached on either side of the group differ in the way the total carbon count is shared. Diethyl ether (ethyl, ethyl) and methyl propyl ether (methyl, propyl) are metamers: both are C4H10O ethers.

Metamers of C4H10O: diethyl ether, methyl propyl ether, isopropyl methyl ether.

Pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one have the same five-carbon chain and the carbonyl group sits at a different carbon, so they are position isomers. Many courses also call them metamers, because the carbonyl carbon shares the other four carbons between the two alkyl groups as (1+3) in one and (2+2) in the other; the wording of the question decides which label is wanted. Ethyl ethanoate and methyl propanoate are metamers of each other, differing in the split between the alcohol-derived and acid-derived parts of the ester.

Pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one: position isomers (also called metamers in some courses).

Metamers: ethyl ethanoate and methyl propanoate.

For secondary amines, N-methylpropan-1-amine and diethylamine (both C4H11N) are metamers. Metamerism is often treated as a subtype of chain isomerism, because the change is in how the carbons are distributed in the skeleton about the central atom; it is therefore wise to read the wording of the question.

Key idea. Metamers: same functional group (ether, ketone, ester, 2° or 3° amine), same formula, different alkyl groups on the two sides of the group.
Trap. Propan-1-ol and methoxyethane are functional isomers, not metamers. Metamerism needs the same class on both sides, and it needs a group that sits between two carbon groups.
