3. Structure and bonding of the amino group

Nitrogen in an amine is sp3-hybridised: three bonding sp3 orbitals and one holding the lone pair, so the shape is trigonal pyramidal with C–N–C angles around 108°, slightly less than the tetrahedral 109.5° because the lone pair repels the bond pairs more strongly. This lone pair is responsible for everything that follows – basicity, nucleophilicity, and H-bond acceptance.

Nitrogen inversion. The pyramid flips through a planar sp2 transition state (the lone pair sits in a pure p orbital) with a barrier of only ~25 kJ mol-1 for simple amines. At room temperature this occurs millions of times per second, so the two "enantiomeric" pyramids of an amine with three different groups interconvert at once. Consequence: chiral amines R1R2R3N cannot be resolved. Resolution is possible when inversion is stopped: (i) quaternary ammonium salts with four different groups (no lone pair), (ii) Tröger's base (the bridge locks the geometry), (iii) some strained aziridines and amine oxides.

A quaternary ammonium ion with four different groups can be resolved; a tertiary amine with three different groups cannot (rapid inversion)

In aniline the nitrogen is flatter than in methylamine: the lone pair is conjugated with the ring, so the geometry lies between sp3 (pyramidal) and sp2 (planar). The C–N bond (≈140 pm) is shorter than in methylamine (≈147 pm) because of its partial double-bond character; in p-nitroaniline the effect is stronger. This is the structural reason for the low basicity of aromatic amines.

Key idea Hybridisation of N correlates with basicity: sp3 amine (pKa of conjugate acid ≈ 10–11) > sp2 imine/pyridine N (≈ 5–7) > sp nitrile N (≈ −10). The more s-character in the orbital holding the lone pair, the more tightly it is held and the weaker the base.
