19. Electrophiles, nucleophiles, leaving groups and reaction types

19.1 Electrophiles and nucleophiles

  • An electrophile (E⁺, 'electron-loving') is a species that accepts an electron pair to form a new bond: it is positively charged or has an electron-deficient (δ⁺) atom or an empty orbital. It is a Lewis acid.
  • A nucleophile (Nu⁻, 'nucleus-loving') is a species that donates an electron pair to form a new bond: it carries a lone pair or a π-bond (or is negatively charged). It is a Lewis base.
  • A base attacks a hydrogen (H⁺); a nucleophile attacks a carbon (or another atom other than H). Same species, different role: basicity is a thermodynamic property (equilibrium with H⁺), nucleophilicity is kinetic (rate of attack on carbon).

19.2 Ambident nucleophiles

An ambident nucleophile has two nucleophilic sites. Which site reacts depends on the electrophile (hard/soft), the counter-ion and the solvent.

IMP Rule for ambident anions (Kornblum): the more ionic the salt (or the more SN1-like the reaction), the more the more electronegative (harder, more charged) atom reacts: O of nitrite, N of cyanide in the case of Ag⁺-assisted ionisation. The more covalent the salt and the more SN2-like the reaction, the more the less electronegative, softer atom reacts: N of nitrite (with Ag), C of cyanide (with K⁺).

Exam Trap Basicity and nucleophilicity usually move together but not always. In a protic solvent the order of nucleophilicity of halides is I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ (opposite to the order of basicity F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻), because small F⁻ is tightly solvated. Bulky bases (t-BuOK, LDA, DBU, DIPEA) are strong bases but weak nucleophiles.
Illustration 19.2 Main

Order of nucleophilicity

Arrange in decreasing nucleophilicity in methanol: I⁻, Br⁻, Cl⁻, F⁻.

  1. Methanol is a polar protic solvent: it hydrogen-bonds to small anions. F⁻ is the smallest and most strongly solvated (hindered); I⁻ is large, very polarisable and weakly solvated.
  2. I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻. In DMSO (aprotic) the order reverses to F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻ (the order of basicity).
Illustration 19.3 Main

Nucleophile vs base

Arrange in decreasing nucleophilicity (in water): CH₃S⁻, CH₃O⁻, CH₃COO⁻, H₂O.

  1. RS⁻ is more polarisable than RO⁻ (and in protic solvents sulfur is less strongly solvated). RO⁻ is a stronger nucleophile than the delocalised RCOO⁻; neutral water is the weakest.
  2. CH₃S⁻ > CH₃O⁻ > CH₃COO⁻ > H₂O.
Illustration 19.4 Adv

Same attacking atom: which is the better nucleophile?

(a) NH₃ or H₂O? (b) PH₃ or NH₃? (c) (CH₃)₃N or (C₂H₅)₃N toward CH₃I? (d) CH₃O⁻ or (CH₃)₃CO⁻ toward CH₃I?

  1. (a) NH₃ (N is less electronegative, holds its lone pair less tightly). (b) PH₃ (larger, more polarisable P; the lone pair is more readily distorted). (c) (CH₃)₃N: the three ethyl groups hinder approach of the larger electrophile; for small electrophiles like H⁺ the order of basicity is different. (d) CH₃O⁻: the bulky tert-butoxide attacks the carbon slowly but removes H⁺ easily (good base, poor nucleophile) — which is why t-BuOK is used for E2 elimination.

19.4 Leaving groups

In substitution and elimination reactions the group that departs, taking the bonding pair with it, is the leaving group. A good leaving group is a weak base (the conjugate base of a strong acid), stable as an anion or a neutral molecule, and polarisable.

IMP Leaving-group order for alkyl halides: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl ≫ R–F. This is opposite to the order of C–X bond polarity and follows the C–X bond strength (C–F 485, C–Cl 339, C–Br 285, C–I 213 kJ mol⁻¹) and the pKₐ of HX. Sulfonates (OTs, OMs) rank above halides; to make a good leaving group from an alcohol either protonate it (HX, H₂SO₄) or convert it to a sulfonate or alkyl halide (SOCl₂, PBr₃).

Rank the leaving groups

Arrange in decreasing leaving-group ability: Cl⁻, OH⁻, I⁻, CH₃COO⁻, F⁻, Br⁻.

  1. The weaker the base, the better the leaving group; use the pKₐ of the conjugate acid: HI (−10), HBr (−9), HCl (−7), HF (3.2), CH₃COOH (4.76), H₂O (15.7).
  2. I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ > CH₃COO⁻ > OH⁻.
Illustration 19.6 Adv

Why does an alcohol need acid?

Explain why CH₃CH₂OH reacts with HBr but not with NaBr alone, and why NaBr/H₂SO₄ works.

  1. OH⁻ is a strong base and so a very poor leaving group: Br⁻ cannot displace it. In acid the O is protonated; H₂O is a good, neutral leaving group and Br⁻ attacks the carbon (SN2 for primary alcohols).
  2. NaBr + H₂SO₄ generates HBr in situ (and the acid medium protonates the alcohol), so the reaction proceeds.
+ +H⁺⟶ Br⁻, −H₂O⟶
Protonation makes water the leaving group (pKₐ of H₃O⁺ −1.7) instead of OH⁻ (pKₐ of H₂O 15.7)

IMP Hammond postulate. A transition state resembles the species (reactants, intermediate or products) closest to it in energy. For a highly exothermic step the TS is early and resembles the reactants (low selectivity: chlorination); for an endothermic step the TS is late and resembles the intermediate (high selectivity: bromination; stability of the carbocation or radical directly controls the rate). A more stable intermediate forms faster.
Illustration 19.7 Adv

Kinetic vs thermodynamic control

Addition of HBr to 1,3-butadiene gives at −80 °C mainly 3-bromobut-1-ene (1,2-adduct) and at +40 °C mainly 1-bromobut-2-ene (1,4-adduct). Explain using the allylic cation.

  1. H⁺ adds to C-1 to give the resonance-stabilised allylic cation (charge on C-2 and C-4). Br⁻ can attack either end.
  2. Low temperature (kinetic control): attack at C-2 (the carbon with the greater positive character, nearer the H–Br addition) is faster: 1,2-adduct. High temperature (thermodynamic control): the addition is reversible; the more stable (more substituted, internal) alkene — the 1,4-adduct — accumulates.
+ +H⁺⟶ ↔⟶
The allylic cation: positive charge shared between C-2 and C-4

Summary sheet: reagents

IMP (1) Electrophile = Lewis acid, attacks electron-rich sites; nucleophile = Lewis base, attacks δ⁺ carbon. (2) Basicity (H⁺ affinity) vs nucleophilicity (rate at carbon): I⁻ is a better nucleophile than F⁻ in water, a worse base; t-BuO⁻ is a good base, a poor nucleophile. (3) Ambident ions: KCN → RCN, AgCN → RNC; KNO₂ → RONO, AgNO₂ → RNO₂. (4) Leaving group ability follows the strength of the conjugate acid: TfO⁻, I⁻, Br⁻, TsO⁻, Cl⁻ ≫ F⁻ ≫ OH⁻, NH₂⁻. (5) Solvent: polar protic solvents favour SN1 and hinder small anions; polar aprotic solvents favour SN2.
