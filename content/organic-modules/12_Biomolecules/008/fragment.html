1.6 Reactions of monosaccharides

Monosaccharides do two kinds of chemistry: reactions of the carbonyl (via the small amount of open chain), and reactions of the hydroxyl groups (ester, ether, acetal). The hemiacetal –OH at C-1 is special: it is the only one that can be turned into an acetal (a glycoside) by acid-catalysed exchange.

<h4>Periodate oxidation (Malaprade) — a counting tool</h4>

HIO4 cleaves bonds between carbons that each carry –OH (1,2-diols), and also bonds between C(=O) and C(OH) (α-hydroxy carbonyl); a 1,2,3-triol is cleaved twice with the middle carbon leaving as formic acid. Products: –CH2OH terminal → HCHO; –CHOH– in the middle → HCOOH; C=O (aldehyde) end → HCOOH; ketone carbon → CO2 (via the acid).

Shortcut Use this to decide ring size: a pyranoside has three consecutive free OH (C-2, C-3, C-4): 2 HIO4, 1 HCOOH, no HCHO. A furanoside has a free exocyclic diol (C-5, C-6) and a free ring diol (C-2, C-3): 2 HIO4, 1 HCHO, no HCOOH. This was how the ring size of glucosides was first settled.
<h4>Osazone formation</h4>

Three moles of phenylhydrazine: the first forms the phenylhydrazone at C-1; the second oxidises the adjacent C-2 CHOH to C=O (it is itself reduced to aniline and ammonia); the third condenses with the new C=O to give the bis-phenylhydrazone (osazone). The reaction stops there because the C-2 hydrazone is chelated by intramolecular hydrogen bonding from C-1.

Trap The osazone proves identity of C-3 to C-5, never of C-1 or C-2. "Glucose and galactose give the same osazone" is false (C-4 differs). "Glucose and mannose give the same osazone" is true. "Glucose and fructose give the same osazone" is true.
<h4>Lobry de Bruyn–van Ekenstein transformation</h4>

In dilute base glucose, mannose and fructose interconvert through a common 1,2-enediol (the carbanion/enolate of the α-hydroxy carbonyl). Protonation of C-2 from either face gives glucose or mannose; protonation at C-1 with the C-2 carbonyl re-forming gives fructose. At equilibrium with dilute NaOH ≈ 63 % glucose, 31 % fructose, 3 % mannose. C-3 to C-6 are untouched, so the result is C-2 epimerisation + aldose–ketose isomerisation only.

OH−⟶ H+ at C-1⟶
D-glucose → 1,2-enediol → D-fructose (the same enediol returns D-mannose)
Trap Do not say the stereochemistry at C-3 changes. The enediol (C-1=C-2) has no C-3 stereochemical information lost. Under vigorous alkali the carbonyl can migrate further (to C-3, C-4...), giving other ketoses (psicose, sorbose, tagatose) and eventual fragmentation; JEE problems normally stay at the 1,2-enediol.
<h4>Glycosides</h4>

Acid-catalysed reaction of a hemiacetal with an alcohol gives a glycoside (acetal). The mechanism is SN1-like: protonate the anomeric OH, lose water to give an oxocarbenium ion stabilised by the ring oxygen, then ROH attacks from either face (mixture of α and β; the α-glycoside predominates under thermodynamic control because of the anomeric effect).

CH3OH, HCl (dry)⟶
methyl α-D-glucopyranoside (main product, [α]D +158°); the β-anomer ([α]D −33°) is minor
  • Glycosides are stable to base and to Tollens/Fehling and do not show mutarotation (no hemiacetal), but are hydrolysed by dilute aqueous acid and by specific enzymes (α-glycosidase, e.g. maltase, hydrolyses α-glucosides; emulsin / β-glycosidase hydrolyses β-glucosides).
  • Natural glycosides: amygdalin (β-gentiobiose + mandelonitrile), salicin, digitoxin, anthocyanins, and the N-glycosides in nucleosides.
  • In a glycoside the aglycone (non-sugar part) can be O-linked (O-glycoside), N-linked (nucleosides) or S-linked.
Trap Methyl glucoside has one OMe from the glycoside plus four free OH; it is not methylated at other positions by HCl/CH3OH. Exhaustive methylation (Me2SO4/NaOH, or MeI/Ag2O) afterwards puts OMe at C-2, C-3, C-4, C-6.
<h4>Ethers and esters, and the Haworth methylation sequence</h4>
1. CH3OH/HCl 2. (CH3)2SO4/NaOH⟶ dil. H3O+⟶
methyl glucoside → methyl 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-methyl glucoside → 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-methyl-D-glucose (hydrolysis removes only the glycosidic OMe)

The free OH left after hydrolysis marks the position that was in the ring (C-5, in a pyranose) or was a glycosidic link in a disaccharide (see 1.11). Purdie methylation (MeI/Ag2O) is used when the sugar is sensitive to base; both give no racemisation.

Cyanohydrin and Kiliani–Fischer synthesis (ascending the series)

HCN⟶ +
D-arabinose → two epimeric cyanohydrins (D-glucononitrile and D-mannononitrile)

Steps of the Kiliani–Fischer synthesis:

  • 1. HCN (NaCN/H+) adds to the aldehyde: two C-2 epimeric cyanohydrins (new stereocentre; the others are unchanged).
  • 2. Partial reduction: H2, Pd/BaSO4 (poisoned), reduces C≡N to CH=NH, which hydrolyses to CHO (alternatively: hydrolyse CN to COOH, form the lactone, reduce with Na/Hg).
  • 3. Separate the two aldoses (they are diastereomers): D-arabinose → D-glucose + D-mannose.

Each application adds one carbon and gives two epimeric aldoses (C-2 epimers). A pentose gives two hexoses; a tetrose gives two pentoses; a triose gives two tetroses.

Trap In exam problems the Kiliani–Fischer gives a pair of C-2 epimers. They are not formed in equal amounts, but equal yields are never assumed unless the question says so. The new carbon is C-1 (the nitrile carbon becomes the new aldehyde).
<h4>Ruff and Wohl degradation (descending the series)</h4>
1. Br2/H2O 2. Ca(OH)2 3. H2O2, Fe3+⟶
Ruff: D-glucose → D-gluconic acid → calcium gluconate → D-arabinose + CO₂ (C-1 removed; C-2 becomes the new aldehyde)
  • Ruff: oxidise to the aldonic acid, Ca salt, treat with H2O2/Fe3+ (Fenton): the α-hydroxy acid loses CO2 and the C-2 carbon becomes the new CHO.
  • Wohl: aldose → oxime (NH2OH) → acetylation (Ac2O/NaOAc) gives the acetylated nitrile → NaOMe/MeOH removes the CN (as cyanide) to give the shorter aldose. (Weerman is the amide/NaOCl variant.)
  • In both, C-1 is lost and the old C-2 becomes C-1. Glucose and mannose give D-arabinose (same C-3 to C-5). Galactose gives D-lyxose.
Key idea Glucose ⇌ arabinose bridge. D-Arabinose + HCN → D-glucose + D-mannose; D-glucose or D-mannose by Ruff → D-arabinose. Recognise these two chain-changing pairs: they appear in Fischer-proof type problems.

Example 13 Main

Which sugars react?

Which of glucose, fructose, sucrose and ribose (a) reduce Fehling's solution, (b) decolourise bromine water, (c) give a positive Seliwanoff test within 2 minutes?

Show solution
  1. (a) Glucose, fructose, ribose. Sucrose is non-reducing.
  2. (b) Glucose and ribose only (aldoses). Fructose is a ketose and is not oxidised by bromine water.
  3. (c) Fructose gives the cherry-red colour fastest; sucrose responds more slowly because HCl first hydrolyses it, releasing fructose.
Example 14 Adv

Periodate counting

Methyl β-D-glucopyranoside is oxidised with excess HIO4. (a) How many mol HIO4 per mol, and what small products are formed? (b) Answer again for methyl β-D-glucofuranoside.

Show solution
  1. (a) Pyranoside: free OH at C-2, C-3, C-4 and C-6. Vicinal pairs: C-2/C-3 and C-3/C-4 (C-6 OH is on a CH2 attached to C-5, whose oxygen is part of the ring: no vicinal OH). 2 HIO4; the central carbon (C-3) is released as 1 HCOOH.
  2. (b) Furanoside (ring C-1 to C-4): free OH at C-2, C-3, C-5 and C-6. Pairs C-2/C-3 and C-5/C-6 are both vicinal; C-3/C-4 is not (C-4 carries the ring oxygen). 2 HIO4, giving 1 HCHO (from C-6) and no HCOOH.
Example 15 Adv

Osazone count

How many different osazones are given by the eight D-aldohexoses? Name one set of three sugars (aldoses and ketoses) that share an osazone.

Show solution
  1. Only C-3, C-4 and C-5 survive unchanged. C-5 is R for all D-sugars; the C-3/C-4 pattern has four possibilities (R,R; L,R; R,L; L,L).
  2. So four osazones. Example: D-glucose, D-mannose and D-fructose.
Example 16 Adv

Kiliani–Fischer product mixture

A D-pentose P gives two hexoses A and B on Kiliani–Fischer lengthening. HNO3 oxidation of A gives an optically inactive diacid, that of B an optically active diacid, and P itself gives an optically inactive pentaric acid. Identify P, A and B.

Show solution
  1. The new C-2 centre is R in one product and L in the other: A and B are C-2 epimers with the same C-3 to C-5 pattern as P.
  2. D-Hexoses with meso aldaric acids (palindromic codes): allose RRRR and galactose RLLR. Dropping the first letter gives the parent pentose: RRRR → RRR (D-ribose); RLLR → LLR (D-lyxose).
  3. The pentoses P = ribose RRR or lyxose LLR. The pentaric acid of ribose (RRR, palindrome) is meso; that of lyxose (LLR) is not.
  4. So P = D-ribose; A = D-allose (RRRR, meso acid); B = D-altrose (LRRR, active acid).
Example 17 Main

Reducing power

Which of the following do not give a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent: glucose, methyl α-D-glucoside, sucrose, fructose, glucitol?

Show solution
  1. Methyl α-D-glucoside (no free hemiacetal), sucrose (anomeric carbons both tied up), and glucitol (no carbonyl). Glucose and fructose (via the enediol) do.
Example 18 Adv

Ring size from oxidation

A methyl glucoside is exhaustively methylated and mildly hydrolysed to a tetra-O-methylglucose, which HNO3 oxidises to trimethoxyglutaric acid (C8H14O7). What ring size did the glycoside have? What would the product have been for the other ring size?

Show solution
  1. The free OH of the tetra-O-methylsugar marks the carbon that was bonded to the ring oxygen. HNO3 cleaves the chain beside that carbon.
  2. Trimethoxyglutaric acid has five carbons (C-1 to C-5, three OMe at C-2, C-3, C-4): the cleavage was between C-5 and C-6, so the free OH was at C-5 and the ring was six-membered (pyranoside).
  3. A furanoside would carry the free OH at C-4 and give dimethoxysuccinic acid (C-1 to C-4, OMe at C-2, C-3).
Example 19 Main

Mass check

Glucosazone is formed from glucose and phenylhydrazine. Calculate its molar mass and the mass of osazone from 9.0 g of glucose with 100 % yield.

Show solution
  1. C18H22N4O4: 18(12) + 22(1) + 4(14) + 4(16) = 216 + 22 + 56 + 64 = 358 g mol−1.
  2. 9.0 g glucose = 0.050 mol → 0.050 × 358 = 17.9 g.
