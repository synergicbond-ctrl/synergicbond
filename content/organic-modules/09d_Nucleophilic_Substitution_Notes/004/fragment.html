5. Nucleophilicity, basicity and solvent effects

Basicity is a thermodynamic property (affinity for H+), whereas nucleophilicity is kinetic (rate of attack on carbon). They often parallel each other when the attacking atoms are the same element, so alkoxides are stronger nucleophiles than carboxylates. Nucleophilicity increases down a group in protic solvents (I– &gt; Br– &gt; Cl– &gt; F–; RS– &gt; RO–) because larger atoms are more polarisable and less strongly hydrogen bonded, even though basicity runs in the opposite direction.

Polar protic solvents (water, alcohols, carboxylic acids) hydrogen-bond to anions and stabilise them, lowering their reactivity; small, hard anions such as F– are affected most. Polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF, acetonitrile, acetone) solvate cations strongly but leave anions largely bare, so SN2 reactions of anionic nucleophiles are speeded up by orders of magnitude compared with protic solvents. In aprotic solvents the order of Cl–, Br– and I– reverses and follows basicity and charge density: Cl– &gt; Br– &gt; I–. Fluoride becomes very reactive only when it is essentially free of ion pairing and water, so it is left out of the comparison.

For SN1 the picture is different. Ionisation produces charge from a neutral molecule, so polar protic solvents (which stabilise both the departing anion by hydrogen bonding and the cation by dipole interaction) accelerate it strongly. The rate of solvolysis of t-BuCl rises sharply as the proportion of water in an alcohol–water mixture increases. The strength of the nucleophile does not matter for the rate of SN1.

Summary. SN2 with anionic Nu: aprotic &gt;&gt; protic. SN1: ionising protic solvent (high water content) fastest. Nucleophile concentration affects SN2 rate only.
