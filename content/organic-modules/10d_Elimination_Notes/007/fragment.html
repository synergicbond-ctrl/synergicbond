8. Dehydrohalogenation to alkynes

Two successive E2 eliminations convert a vicinal dihalide (halogens on adjacent carbons) or a geminal dihalide (both halogens on the same carbon) into an alkyne. The first elimination gives a vinylic halide; the second, which removes a vinylic hydrogen and a vinylic halide, is harder and needs a very strong base such as sodium amide (NaNH2) in liquid ammonia, or molten KOH at high temperature.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaNH₂ (2 equiv), liquid NH₃

Product:

A vicinal dibromide loses two HBr to give but-2-yne. Two equivalents of amide are enough because the internal alkyne has no acidic C–H.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaNH₂ (3 equiv), NH₃(l); then H₂O

Product:

2,2-Dichloropropane (geminal) loses two HCl to give propyne; the terminal alkyne is deprotonated by the base to the sodium acetylide, which uses a third equivalent. Water in the work-up regenerates propyne.

  1. Count the halogens: each C–X pair that must be removed consumes one equivalent of base.
  2. If the final alkyne is terminal (≡C–H), add one more equivalent of NaNH2, because the amide deprotonates it (pKa of an alkyne is about 25, of NH3 about 38). A proton source such as water is then added to regenerate the alkyne.
  3. If the final alkyne is internal, no extra equivalent is needed, but under forcing conditions with excess amide an internal alkyne can migrate to a terminal acetylide.

Vicinal dibromides are conveniently made by adding Br2 to an alkene, so the sequence alkene → Br2 → NaNH2 converts an alkene into an alkyne. Geminal dihalides come from the reaction of a ketone or aldehyde with PCl5. A vinylic halide such as the intermediate may eliminate by an anti or a syn pathway; anti is preferred when the geometry allows.

Key idea. Equivalents of NaNH2 = 2 for the double dehydrohalogenation, +1 if the alkyne produced is terminal and is isolated after a proton-source work-up.
