24. Formation and stability of arenediazonium salts

Diazotisation. A primary aromatic amine in cold aqueous mineral acid (HCl, H2SO4, HBF4) is treated with NaNO2 solution at 273–278 K (0–5 °C) to give the arenediazonium salt Ar–N2+X−. At least 2 equiv. of acid per amine are needed: one to generate HNO2, one to form the diazonium salt (the actual stoichiometry is ArNH2 + NaNO2 + 2HX → ArN2+X− + NaX + 2H2O); excess acid also prevents coupling of the diazonium with unreacted amine to give diazoamino compounds.

Why must the temperature stay below ≈ 278 K? Above that, ArN2+ reacts with water to give phenol and N2 (and tars), and HNO2 decomposes. Why are aryl diazonium ions more stable than alkyl ones? Delocalisation of the ring π-electrons onto the N≡N+ group (resonance) strengthens the C–N bond: partial double-bond character. In an alkyl diazonium ion no such resonance exists and loss of N2 gives a carbocation. Aryl cation formation is itself very hard (the empty orbital is in the plane of the ring, sp2, not conjugated), which also contributes to the slow thermal decomposition.

  • Salts: the chloride, sulphate and nitrate are explosive when dry and are always used in solution. The tetrafluoroborate ArN2+BF4− is relatively stable and can be isolated (used in the Balz–Schiemann reaction).
  • Electron-withdrawing groups (p-NO2) make the diazonium ion more electrophilic (a better coupling partner); electron-donating groups (p-OCH3, p-NMe2) stabilise the ion by resonance and reduce its electrophilicity (slower coupling).
Trap The tests of a stable diazonium: the NaNO2 must be added slowly to a cold, acidic solution of the amine. Excess HNO2 is checked with starch–iodide paper (blue-black = excess nitrite present, i.e. diazotisation complete) and destroyed with urea or sulfamic acid before coupling.
