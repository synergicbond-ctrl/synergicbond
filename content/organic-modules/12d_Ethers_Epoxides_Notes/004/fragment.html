5. Williamson synthesis: scope and choice of reagents

The Williamson reaction is an SN2 process, so the usual limits of SN2 apply to the halide component while the alkoxide acts as a strong base and nucleophile. The halide should be methyl, primary, benzylic or allylic. A secondary halide gives a mixture of ether and alkene, and a tertiary halide gives essentially only alkene by E2. Aryl and vinyl halides do not undergo SN2 and do not react unless the aryl ring carries strongly electron-withdrawing groups ortho or para to the halogen (then substitution proceeds by the addition–elimination mechanism).

For an unsymmetrical ether R–O–R′ two disconnections are possible. The correct choice puts the more hindered carbon on the alkoxide and the less hindered carbon on the halide.

  1. Identify the two carbon groups attached to oxygen.
  2. Decide which group can be the halide: it must be methyl, primary, benzylic or allylic. A tertiary group can never be the halide.
  3. If both are primary, either route works; if one is secondary or tertiary, that group comes from the alkoxide.
  4. If one group is aryl, it must come from the phenoxide (Ar–O−); the alkyl group comes from the halide.
  5. Use a bulky alkoxide with a small halide, not the reverse.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaH

Product:

Intramolecular Williamson reaction. Five- and six-membered rings form easily; three-membered rings (epoxides) form fast; four-membered rings close more slowly and seven-membered rings are slower still.

Reactant:

Reagent: 1) NaOH; 2) CH3CH2Br

Product:

Phenoxide is a weaker base than an alkoxide, so elimination is much less of a problem, even with somewhat hindered halides; with a primary halide such as bromoethane, ethoxybenzene forms in high yield.

Trap. Ethoxide plus 2-bromo-2-methylpropane gives isobutene, not tert-butyl ethyl ether. The ether is made from sodium tert-butoxide and bromoethane.
