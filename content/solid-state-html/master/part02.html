2.1 Fractional contribution of boundary particles

A particle exactly on a unit-cell boundary is shared by neighbouring cells. Its contribution to one selected cell equals the fraction of its surrounding space that lies inside that cell.

PositionNumber of equivalent cells sharing the positionContribution to one cell
Corner81/81/81/8
Edge centre41/41/41/4
Face centre21/21/21/2
Body centre1111
Completely internal site1111
Ncell=Nc8+Ne4+Nf2+Nb+NiN_{cell}=\frac{N_c}{8}+\frac{N_e}{4}+\frac{N_f}{2}+N_b+N_iNcell​=8Nc​​+4Ne​​+2Nf​​+Nb​+Ni​

The formula counts any specified species. Apply it separately to each chemically distinct atom or ion when determining the formula of a unit cell.

1
Worked example

Counting a mixed cell

Species A occupies all corners and all face centres; species B occupies all edge centres and one body centre. Determine the formula.

  1. A: 8(1/8)+6(1/2)=1+3=48(1/8)+6(1/2)=1+3=48(1/8)+6(1/2)=1+3=4.
  2. B: 12(1/4)+1=3+1=412(1/4)+1=3+1=412(1/4)+1=3+1=4.
  3. The ratio A:B is 4:4=1:14:4=1:14:4=1:1.
The empirical formula is AB.

2.2 Primitive and centred conventional cells

Centring describes additional lattice points in a conventional cell. The added points must be equivalent to the corner points under lattice translation.

SymbolNameAdditional lattice pointsLattice points per conventional cell
PPrimitiveCorners only1
IBody-centredOne body centre2
FFace-centredCentres of all six faces4
C (or A/B)Base-centredCentres of one pair of opposite faces2
  • Representative I-centring translation: (12,12,12)(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)(21​,21​,21​).
  • Independent F-centring translations: (12,12,0)(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)(21​,21​,0), (12,0,12)(\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12)(21​,0,21​) and (0,12,12)(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)(0,21​,21​).
  • A base-centred cell has only one opposite pair of centred faces; it is not face-centred.
Centring is not a contact rule

SC, BCC and FCC names specify translational equivalence. Sphere contact along an edge, body diagonal or face diagonal is a separate hard-sphere model used later.

2
Worked example

Lattice-point count

How many lattice points are contained in an F-centred conventional cell?

  1. Corners contribute 8×1/8=18\times 1/8=18×1/8=1.
  2. Face centres contribute 6×1/2=36\times 1/2=36×1/2=3.
An F-centred conventional cell contains 4 lattice points.

2.3 From one-dimensional lattices to three dimensions

In one dimension there is only one Bravais lattice: equally spaced points on a line. In two dimensions there are five Bravais lattices. Extending periodicity into three dimensions produces seven metric crystal systems and fourteen distinct Bravais lattices.

The common statement ‘five two-dimensional lattices’ counts primitive rectangular and centred rectangular separately. A centred square net can be redrawn as a smaller primitive square net rotated by 45∘45^\circ45∘, so it is not a new lattice.

2.5 Fourteen Bravais lattices

Not every centring type creates a distinct lattice in every crystal system. Some centred descriptions can be transformed into a smaller primitive cell of another allowed type. After eliminating equivalent descriptions, exactly fourteen three-dimensional Bravais lattices remain.

Crystal systemAllowed Bravais latticesCount
CubicP, I, F3
TetragonalP, I2
OrthorhombicP, C, I, F4
HexagonalP1
TrigonalR1
MonoclinicP, C2
TriclinicP1
3+2+4+1+1+2+1=143+2+4+1+1+2+1=143+2+4+1+1+2+1=14
  • Orthorhombic is the only system that has P, C, I and F forms.
  • There are three body-centred Bravais lattices: cubic I, tetragonal I and orthorhombic I.
  • A structure type such as CsCl, NaCl or perovskite is not a Bravais-lattice name.
3
Worked example

Bravais-lattice integer

How many of the fourteen Bravais lattices are non-primitive?

  1. Cubic contributes I and F: 2.
  2. Tetragonal contributes I: 1.
  3. Orthorhombic contributes C, I and F: 3.
  4. Monoclinic contributes C: 1.
There are 2+1+3+1=72+1+3+1=72+1+3+1=7 non-primitive Bravais lattices.

2.6 Symmetry elements of a cube

The cube belongs to the highest-symmetry common crystal system. A geometrical symmetry element is a line, plane or point about which a symmetry operation can be performed.

ElementNumberDescription
C4C_4C4​ axes3Through centres of opposite faces
C3C_3C3​ axes4Along body diagonals
C2C_2C2​ axes6Through midpoints of opposite edges
Mirror planes93 axial planes and 6 diagonal planes
Centre of inversion1At the cube centre
3+4+6+9+1=233+4+6+9+1=233+4+6+9+1=23

This classroom count refers to geometrical elements, not the total number of symmetry operations.

Axes versus operations

A single C4C_4C4​ axis permits several rotations (90∘90^\circ90∘, 180∘180^\circ180∘, 270∘270^\circ270∘). Do not equate the number of axes with the number of rotational operations.

Coordinates differing by integers describe translationally equivalent positions. For example, (1,0,0)(1,0,0)(1,0,0) is equivalent to (0,0,0)(0,0,0)(0,0,0) in a periodic lattice.

To obtain a direction index, subtract the fractional coordinates of the initial point from those of the final point, then clear fractions to the smallest integers. Direction indices describe a vector; they are not obtained by taking reciprocals.

2.8 Miller indices and interplanar spacing

Miller indices encode the orientation of a family of parallel lattice planes. Find the intercepts of a plane on the a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c axes in lattice units, take reciprocals, and clear fractions. An infinite intercept gives reciprocal zero; a negative intercept is written with a bar.

  • Intercepts (a,∞,∞)(a,\infty,\infty)(a,∞,∞) give (100)(100)(100).
  • Intercepts (a,b,∞)(a,b,\infty)(a,b,∞) give (110)(110)(110).
  • Intercepts (a,b,c)(a,b,c)(a,b,c) give (111)(111)(111).
  • A plane through the origin is replaced by a parallel plane before taking intercepts.

2.9 Bragg diffraction and systematic absences

X-ray wavelengths are comparable with interatomic spacings. Waves scattered from successive parallel planes interfere constructively when their path difference is an integral multiple of the wavelength.

Derivation · Bragg's law
path difference=2dsin⁡θ\text{path difference}=2d\sin\thetapath difference=2dsinθ

One ray travels an additional distance dsin⁡θd\sin\thetadsinθ on entering and another dsin⁡θd\sin\thetadsinθ on leaving.

2dsin⁡θ=nλ2d\sin\theta=n\lambda2dsinθ=nλ

Constructive interference requires an integral number of wavelengths.

Result
nλ=2dhklsin⁡θn\lambda=2d_{hkl}\sin\thetanλ=2dhkl​sinθ

Powder diffraction contains many randomly oriented crystallites, so some crystallites satisfy the Bragg condition for each allowed plane family. Peak positions reveal ddd spacings; peak intensities depend on the basis and atomic scattering factors.

Selection rules assume an identical monatomic basis

Adding different atoms to the basis changes the structure factor. The simple BCC/FCC extinction rules must not be applied blindly to ordered binary structures.

Index a BCC reflection

Which of (100)(100)(100), (110)(110)(110), (111)(111)(111) and (200)(200)(200) are allowed for a monatomic BCC lattice?
