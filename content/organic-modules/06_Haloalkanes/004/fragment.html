3. Preparation of alkyl halides

There are about ten general methods. Learn them in four families: (i) from alcohols (replace OH), (ii) from alkenes/alkynes (add H–X or X–X), (iii) from alkanes/allylic–benzylic C–H (radical halogenation), and (iv) from other halides or acids (halogen exchange, decarboxylative halogenation).

3.1 From alcohols

The OH group is a poor leaving group (HO− is a strong base). All reagents here work by converting –OH into a better leaving group (–OH2+, –OSOCl, –OPBr2, –OPCl4). (a) Hydrogen halides, HX. ROH + HX → RX + H2O. Reactivity of HX: HI > HBr > HCl ≫ HF (the order of acidity and of nucleophilicity of the halide in water). Reactivity of alcohol: 3° > 2° > 1° ≈ CH3OH (allylic, benzylic like 3°). 3° and 2° alcohols react by SN1 through carbocations, so rearrangement is possible; 1° alcohols react by SN2 (protonated alcohol attacked by X−).

HCl, conc.⟶
tert-Butyl alcohol shakes with conc. HCl at room temperature and gives the chloride in minutes (SN1).
NaBr, H2SO4⟶
1-Butanol to 1-bromobutane: HBr generated in situ; primary alcohol reacts by SN2 on the oxonium ion.

(b) Lucas reagent: conc. HCl + anhydrous ZnCl2. ZnCl2 (a Lewis acid) coordinates to the oxygen and makes the OH an even better leaving group. The chloroalkane formed is insoluble in the acid medium, so the solution turns cloudy. This is the classical test to distinguish 1°, 2°, 3° alcohols (works for alcohols up to about C6, which are soluble in the reagent):

Alcohol typeObservation at room temperature
3° (and allylic, benzylic)Turbidity immediately
2°Turbidity in about 5 minutes
1°No turbidity at room temperature (needs heating)
Trap HX and Lucas reagent often give rearranged products with 2° alcohols that can form a more stable cation. 3-Methylbutan-2-ol + HBr gives mainly 2-bromo-2-methylbutane (1,2-hydride shift), not 2-bromo-3-methylbutane. Neopentyl alcohol + HBr gives 2-bromo-2-methylbutane (1,2-methyl shift). To avoid rearrangement use SOCl2, PBr3 or PCl5.
HBr⟶
Secondary alcohol, tertiary product: 3-methylbutan-2-ol, by 1,2-H shift of the initial secondary cation.

(c) Thionyl chloride, SOCl2 (best laboratory method for RCl). ROH + SOCl2 → RCl + SO2↑ + HCl↑. Both by-products are gases, so the product is easily isolated pure, with no rearrangement. Stereochemistry depends on conditions:

  • No base (neat or in dioxane): SNi (substitution nucleophilic internal): the chlorosulfite ROS(O)Cl collapses as a tight ion pair, Cl− is delivered from the same face, retention of configuration.
  • With pyridine (or Et3N): pyridine neutralises HCl, free Cl− is released, and it attacks from the back as SN2: inversion.
SOCl2⟶
Gaseous by-products: SO2 and HCl. Excellent for 1° and 2° alcohols.

(d) Phosphorus halides. PCl5: ROH + PCl5 → RCl + POCl3 + HCl. PCl3: 3 ROH + PCl3 → 3 RCl + H3PO3 (not good for 3° because of rearrangements). PBr3 (or red P + Br2) → RBr, by SN2 with inversion, no carbocation rearrangement (good for 1° and 2°). Red P/I2 gives RI.

PBr3⟶
Inversion at the stereocentre; no rearrangement.

(e) Tosylate route (two-step, stereo-defined). ROH + TsCl/pyridine → ROTs (retention, C–O bond not broken) then NaX → RX by SN2 (inversion). Overall one inversion; useful because the first step never touches the stereocentre. (f) Appel reaction. ROH + PPh3 + CCl4 (or CBr4) → RCl (RBr) + Ph3P=O + CHCl3; SN2, inversion, neutral conditions.

Shortcut Choosing a reagent: want retention of configuration from a chiral alcohol? Use SOCl2 alone (SNi), or two successive inversions (e.g. TsCl then Br−, then Br− again via a different route), or NGP. Want inversion? PBr3, SOCl2/pyridine, TsCl then X−, or Appel.

3.2 From alkenes and alkynes

  • Addition of HX (Markovnikov): CH3CH=CH2 + HBr → CH3CHBrCH3; carbocation intermediate, so rearrangement of 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene + HCl gives 2-chloro-2,3-dimethylbutane as the major product.
  • Peroxide (Kharasch) effect, HBr only: radical chain, anti-Markovnikov: CH3CH=CH2 + HBr (ROOR) → CH3CH2CH2Br. HCl and HI do not show it (for HCl, the H-abstraction by the carbon radical is endothermic; for HI the addition of I• to the alkene is too unfavourable).
  • Addition of X2 (Cl2, Br2 in CCl4): trans (anti) addition through a halonium ion gives vicinal dihalides.
  • Alkynes + 2 HX give gem-dihalides (second addition follows Markovnikov): CH3C≡CH + 2 HBr → CH3CBr2CH3.
HCl⟶
Hydride/methyl shift: 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene gives the rearranged tertiary chloride 2-chloro-2,3-dimethylbutane (major). The secondary chloride is minor.

3.3 From alkanes and from allylic/benzylic C–H: free-radical halogenation

Chain mechanism: initiation X2 → 2 X•; propagation R–H + X• → R• + HX, then R• + X2 → RX + X•; termination by radical coupling. Reactivity of X2: F2 ≫ Cl2 > Br2 ≫ I2 (the H-abstraction step is exothermic for F, Cl; endothermic for Br; very endothermic for I). Selectivity is the reverse: the less reactive the halogen atom, the more selective (late transition state, resembles the radical, so the weakest C–H bond reacts).

Relative reactivity per H1° C–H2° C–H3° C–H
Chlorination (≈ 25 °C, hν)13.85.0
Bromination (≈ 125–127 °C, hν)1821640
Formula % of an isomer = (number of H of that type × relative reactivity) ÷ Σ (number × reactivity) × 100.
Example 4 Main

Product ratio in chlorination

Calculate the percentage of each monochloride when (a) propane and (b) 2-methylpropane are chlorinated with Cl2/hν at 25 °C; (c) 2-methylpropane is brominated at 125 °C.

Show solution
  1. (a) Propane has 6 primary H and 2 secondary H. Relative amounts: 1-chloropropane = 6 × 1 = 6; 2-chloropropane = 2 × 3.8 = 7.6. Total 13.6. 1-chloropropane 44 %, 2-chloropropane 56 %.
  2. (b) 2-Methylpropane: 9 primary H, 1 tertiary H. 9 × 1 = 9; 1 × 5 = 5. Total 14. isobutyl chloride 64 %, tert-butyl chloride 36 %.
  3. (c) Bromination: 9 × 1 = 9 versus 1 × 1640 = 1640: tert-butyl bromide ≈ 99.5 %. This is why bromination is the method of choice for selectively functionalising a tertiary C–H bond.
Trap Chlorination is statistical-plus-slightly-selective: 1° C–H abstraction is not negligible. Bromination is highly selective. Fluorination is nearly unselective and violent; iodination of alkanes does not occur (the reverse reaction RI + HI → RH + I2 dominates).
Allylic and benzylic halogenation. The radical is stabilised by resonance, so these C–H bonds are weakest (allylic C–H ≈ 360, benzylic ≈ 375, vs 3° ≈ 400, 1° ≈ 420 kJ mol−1).
  • NBS (N-bromosuccinimide) with light or peroxide, CCl4: selective allylic/benzylic bromination (Wohl–Ziegler). NBS maintains a very low concentration of Br2 (it reacts with the HBr produced), which prevents electrophilic addition of Br2 to the C=C.
  • Toluene + Cl2/hν (or heat) gives C6H5CH2Cl, then C6H5CHCl2, then C6H5CCl3 (benzotrichloride). In the presence of Fe/FeCl3 in the dark at room temperature, ring (o, p) chlorination occurs instead. Side-chain: light/heat/radical. Ring: Lewis acid.
NBS, hν⟶
Cyclohexene gives 3-bromocyclohexene (allylic substitution); the C=C is untouched.
Cl2, hν or Δ⟶
Side-chain chlorination of toluene (radical); with Fe/FeCl3 the ring is chlorinated instead.
Trap Unsymmetrical alkenes under NBS can give two allylic bromides because the allylic radical is delocalised: but-1-ene → 3-bromobut-1-ene + 1-bromobut-2-ene (mix of cis and trans). Always check whether the allylic radical is symmetrical.

3.4 Halogen exchange

  • Finkelstein reaction: R–Cl/R–Br + NaI (dry acetone) → R–I + NaCl/NaBr↓. The equilibrium is driven to the right by precipitation of NaCl/NaBr in acetone (NaI is soluble in acetone, NaCl/NaBr are not). SN2 – works best for 1° (and allylic, benzylic).
  • Swarts reaction: R–Cl/R–Br + AgF (or Hg2F2, CoF2, SbF3) → R–F. For CCl4 + SbF3 (with SbCl5) → CCl2F2 (Freon-12). Direct fluorination is dangerous; NaF/KF in polar aprotic solvents also works.
NaI, acetone⟶
Finkelstein: NaBr precipitates in dry acetone and drives the reaction.
SbF3, SbCl5⟶
Swarts reaction: tetrachloromethane to dichlorodifluoromethane (Freon-12).

3.5 Decarboxylative halogenation: Hunsdiecker (Borodin–Hunsdiecker) reaction

Silver salt of a carboxylic acid + Br2 (CCl4, reflux) → RBr + CO2 + AgBr. The product has one carbon less than the acid. Yield order 1° > 2° > 3°. Radical chain via the acyl hypobromite RCOOBr → RCOO• → R• + CO2.

Br2, CCl4⟶
Silver butanoate gives 1-bromopropane, CO2 and AgBr. Also works for aromatic acids: silver benzoate → bromobenzene.

3.6 Other useful routes

  • From ethers: R–O–R′ + HI (excess, hot) → 2 RI + H2O (or R′OH then R′I). With HI cold, the smaller/less hindered alkyl gives the iodide by SN2; if one group is 3°, benzylic or allylic, it is that one (via SN1) that goes to the iodide.
  • From carbonyl compounds + PCl5: RCHO or R2C=O + PCl5 → RCHCl2/R2CCl2 + POCl3 (gem-dichlorides).
  • From the α-carbon of acids (Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky): RCH2COOH + Br2/red P → RCHBrCOOH.
  • Chloromethylation (Blanc): ArH + HCHO + HCl (ZnCl2) → ArCH2Cl.
  • From diazonium salts (for aryl halides: section 3.7).

3.7 Preparation of aryl halides

X2, Fe or FeX3⟶
Electrophilic aromatic substitution (X = Cl, Br). Iodination needs an oxidant (HNO3, HIO3) to remove HI; fluorination is too violent.
  • Direct halogenation of the ring: Cl2/FeCl3 (or Fe), Br2/FeBr3. I2 + HNO3 (or HIO3, or Cu2+) for iodobenzene: the oxidant removes HI, which would otherwise reduce ArI back to ArH.
  • Sandmeyer reaction: ArN2+Cl− + CuCl/HCl (or CuBr/HBr) → ArCl (ArBr) + N2. Gattermann reaction: same transformation using Cu powder + HX (lower yields).
  • Iodide: ArN2+ + KI → ArI + N2 (no copper needed).
  • Fluoride (Balz–Schiemann): ArN2+BF4− (from HBF4), dry and heated → ArF + BF3 + N2.
NaNO2/HCl, 0–5 °C⟶ CuBr/HBr⟶
Aniline to bromobenzene by diazotisation and Sandmeyer reaction. Use this when direct bromination would give the wrong isomer.
Trap Phenol does not convert into chlorobenzene on treatment with PCl5 or SOCl2 because the C–O bond has partial double-bond character (lone pair on O delocalised into the ring); one gets mostly triphenyl phosphate or aryl chlorosulfite. Likewise aryl halides cannot be made by an SN reaction on an aryl alcohol or on another aryl halide.
Key idea Summary of reagents and stereochemical outcome (chiral alcohol → halide): HX/ZnCl2 → racemisation (+ rearrangement) for 3°/2°; SOCl2 neat → retention (SNi); SOCl2/pyridine → inversion; PCl5 → inversion (SN2, rearrangement free, though some chloride attack may involve ion pairs); PBr3 → inversion; TsCl then Br− → inversion overall; Appel → inversion.
Example 5 Main

Identify the reagent

Give a reagent that converts (a) propan-2-ol to 2-iodopropane (b) ethanol to chloroethane with no acidic by-product left in the flask (c) 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol to 1-bromo-2,2-dimethylpropane without rearrangement (d) cyclohexene to 3-bromocyclohexene.

Show solution
  1. (a) Red P + I2 (generates PI3) or NaI/H3PO4; HI also works (3° rearranged cations are not involved here).
  2. (b) SOCl2: SO2 and HCl are gases and escape; pyridine can be added to mop up HCl.
  3. (c) PBr3 (or PPh3/Br2). HBr would give the rearranged 2-bromo-2-methylbutane because the neopentyl system ionises with a methyl shift. PBr3 avoids a free carbocation (the C–Br bond forms by displacement on the phosphite-type ester), though the reaction is slow because of neopentyl hindrance.
  4. (d) NBS, hν (or peroxide), CCl4.
Example 6 Adv

Retention or inversion

(S)-Butan-2-ol is converted into 2-chlorobutane by (i) SOCl2 neat, (ii) SOCl2 + pyridine. Give the CIP descriptor of the product in each case and explain.

Show solution
  1. Replacing OH by Cl does not change the CIP priority order: in the alcohol the priorities are OH > C2H5 > CH3 > H; in the chloride Cl > C2H5 > CH3 > H. The order is the same, so retention of spatial arrangement means the same letter.
  2. (i) SOCl2 neat: chlorosulfite ion pair, internal return (SNi): retention, (S)-2-chlorobutane.
  3. (ii) With pyridine the chlorosulfite is attacked from the back by free Cl− (SN2): inversion, (R)-2-chlorobutane.
SOCl2⟶
Retention (SNi). The same SMILES stereo-tag appears on both sides because the priority order of the four groups is unchanged.
SOCl2, pyridine⟶
With pyridine: inversion (SN2).
Example 7 Adv

Radical halogenation count

How many different monochlorinated structural products (excluding stereoisomers) are formed from 2-methylbutane, and how many including stereoisomers?

Show solution
  1. 2-Methylbutane: CH3–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3. Distinct carbon environments: C1 (two equivalent CH3 on C2), C2 (CH), C3 (CH2), C4 (CH3). So four constitutional isomers: 1-chloro-2-methylbutane, 2-chloro-2-methylbutane, 2-chloro-3-methylbutane, 1-chloro-3-methylbutane.
  2. Stereoisomers: 1-chloro-2-methylbutane has a stereocentre at C2 (H, CH3, CH2Cl, C2H5) → 2. 2-chloro-3-methylbutane has a stereocentre at C2 → 2. The other two are achiral.
  3. 4 structural isomers; 6 including stereoisomers.
