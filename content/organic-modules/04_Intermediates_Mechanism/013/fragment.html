12. Gallery of drawn mechanisms

Every mechanism below is built from the intermediates described in sections 5 to 11. Each is shown as a figure (first arrow red, second blue, third green), followed by the steps and an explanation of *why* each step occurs, with the orbital picture where it clarifies the answer. The important points to check in any mechanism are the stereochemistry, rearrangement, regiochemistry and the rate-determining step.

12.1 Nucleophilic substitution at sp3 carbon

SN2: one step, inversion
SN2: backside attack, one step, inversion of configuration
  • The nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group. The transition state is trigonal bipyramidal, with the nucleophile and leaving group at the apexes and the three other substituents in a plane (an sp2-like carbon with a p orbital shared between Nu and X). Bond making and bond breaking are simultaneous.
  • Why backside? The nucleophile HOMO donates into the C–X σ* orbital, which has its larger lobe on the back of carbon, opposite X. Front-side attack would meet the small lobe and the electron density of the leaving group.
  • The substituents flip like an umbrella in the wind, so the configuration is inverted. (R)-2-Bromobutane gives (S)-butan-2-ol: the geometry is inverted, and because O replaces Br at priority rank 1 the CIP descriptor changes from R to S.
  • Rate = k[RX][Nu−]. Reactivity: methyl > primary ≫ secondary, tertiary unreactive, because substituents hinder the approach to the back lobe; polar aprotic solvents accelerate the reaction because they do not solvate the anion strongly.
SN1: two steps, racemisation
SN1: rate-determining ionisation, then capture by solvent and deprotonation
  • Step 1 (slow): the C–Br bond ionises in a polar protic solvent to give the planar tert-butyl cation and Br−. The solvent stabilises both ions, and the cation is stabilised by hyperconjugation.
  • Step 2 (fast): water attacks the empty p orbital (LUMO) from either face; loss of H+ from the oxonium ion gives the alcohol. A chiral tertiary halide gives largely racemic alcohol, with a small excess of inversion because the leaving group shields one face briefly.
  • Rate = k[RX], first order in substrate only. Tertiary and benzylic or allylic substrates react; a cation that can rearrange will do so.

Why It Happens The nucleophile HOMO donates into the C–X σ* orbital, whose large lobe lies behind carbon; the transition state is therefore linear (Nu···C···X ≈ 180°) and the substituents on carbon flip. The steric cost of crowding this trigonal bipyramid is why substitution is fast at methyl and primary carbon and absent at tertiary carbon.
Validity Check Check an SN2 assignment: (i) strong nucleophile, high concentration; (ii) unhindered carbon; (iii) good leaving group; (iv) polar aprotic solvent helps; (v) inversion at a stereocentre. If the substrate is tertiary or if the solvent is a weak, ionising protic solvent with a weak nucleophile, an SN1 (and E1) pathway is expected, with racemisation and possible rearrangement.

12.2 Elimination

E2: one step, anti-periplanar
E2: base removes H, π bond forms, bromide leaves, all in one step (H and Br anti-periplanar)
  • The base removes a β-hydrogen while the leaving group departs from the adjacent carbon, in one step. The C–H and C–X bonds must be anti-periplanar (dihedral angle 180°): the σ(C–H) electrons can then donate into the σ*(C–X) orbital through its back lobe, and the π bond forms from two parallel p orbitals that develop in the same plane.
  • In cyclohexanes the H and the leaving group must both be axial. For example, cis-4-tert-butylcyclohexyl bromide undergoes E2 elimination about 500 times faster than the trans isomer, because only in the cis isomer can the bromine be axial in the favoured chair (the bulky tert-butyl group keeps itself equatorial) and anti to an axial hydrogen.
  • Orientation: a small base favours the more substituted alkene (Zaitsev); a bulky base, or a poor or positively charged leaving group (NR3+, SR2+), favours the less substituted alkene. In the 2-butyl system a trimethylammonium leaving group with hydroxide gives mostly but-1-ene (95 : 5), because the transition state is then more carbanion-like and removes the most acidic, most accessible hydrogen.
  • Rate = k[RX][B]. Some rigid ring systems cannot reach an anti arrangement and eliminate syn (trimethylcyclobutylammonium hydroxide: 90 % syn).
E1 and E1cB
  • E1: the same slow ionisation as in SN1, followed by loss of a β-proton to a weak base. It competes with SN1, is favoured by heat, and the more substituted alkene predominates; rearrangement is possible.
  • E1cB: a base first removes an acidic β-hydrogen (next to C=O, NO2, CN) to give a carbanion, and the leaving group then departs. It requires a poor leaving group and an acidifying group, and it is the dehydration step of the aldol condensation.

Concept check. Why does cis-4-tert-butylcyclohexyl bromide eliminate about 500 times faster than the trans isomer?
Show answer
In the cis isomer the bromine is axial in the chair with an equatorial tert-butyl group, so it is anti-periplanar to axial hydrogens; in the trans isomer it is equatorial and can eliminate only after a ring flip that puts the tert-butyl group axial.

12.3 Electrophilic addition to alkenes

Addition of HBr (Markovnikov addition)
Electrophilic addition of HBr: protonation gives the more stable cation, then bromide capture
  • The π electrons (the alkene HOMO) attack the proton of HBr. The proton bonds to the carbon that gives the more substituted, more stable cation: the less substituted carbon receives the proton (Markovnikov's rule).
  • Bromide captures the cation. The formal sequence has a carbocation intermediate, but for many simple alkenes the kinetics are third order (alkene and two molecules of HX), and the addition is mainly anti, which indicates that proton transfer and halide capture are concerted between an alkene–HX complex and a second HX or halide ion. The regiochemistry is the same in either case because the partial positive charge develops on the more substituted carbon.
  • Rearranged products (3-methylbut-1-ene gives 2-bromo-2-methylbutane) show that a cation, or a species with substantial cationic character, is involved when the structure allows rearrangement.
Addition of bromine: bromonium ion and anti addition
Bromine addition: bridged bromonium ion, then backside attack by bromide (anti addition)
  • The π bond attacks one bromine of Br2 while the Br–Br bond breaks. The bromine donates a lone pair back to the carbon to form a bridged bromonium ion. There is no free carbocation, so no rearrangement occurs.
  • Bromide must open the three-membered ring by backside attack (the same σ* argument as in SN2) at one of the two carbons. The two bromines are therefore anti. Cyclohexene gives trans-1,2-dibromocyclohexane (as a racemic mixture), Z-but-2-ene gives the racemic (2R,3R)/(2S,3S)-dibromide, and E-but-2-ene gives the meso dibromide.
  • In water the solvent opens the bromonium ion, and a halohydrin results, with OH on the carbon that carries more of the positive charge and anti to Br.

12.4 Electrophilic aromatic substitution: nitration

Nitration: attack by the nitronium ion gives the σ complex; loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity
  • Nitric acid is protonated by sulfuric acid and loses water to give the nitronium ion NO2+, which is linear and is the real electrophile.
  • The aromatic π system (HOMO) attacks the nitrogen of NO2+ (LUMO). The result is the arenium ion, in which the positive charge is delocalised over three carbons (the ortho and para positions relative to the sp3 carbon) and aromaticity is lost. This step is rate-determining.
  • A base (HSO4−) removes the proton from the sp3 carbon, and aromaticity is restored: the loss of the proton is fast, so cleavage of the C–H bond does not affect the rate.

12.5 Nucleophilic addition to the carbonyl group

Nucleophilic addition to C=O: nucleophile adds to carbon, π pair moves to oxygen
  • The nucleophile (HOMO) adds to the carbonyl carbon along the Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory, about 107° to the C=O bond. The LUMO is π*(C=O), which is larger on carbon and has a node between C and O, so its lobes are tilted; the angle is a compromise between maximum overlap and minimum repulsion by the filled π bond.
  • The π electrons move to oxygen to give a tetrahedral alkoxide. Protonation gives the addition product. With an irreversible nucleophile (hydride, carbanion) the reaction stops here; with a reversible one (cyanide, alcohols, amines) an equilibrium is established.
  • Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones for steric reasons (the trajectory is less hindered) and electronic ones (one alkyl group instead of two donating into the carbonyl group).

Cyanohydrin formation
  • Acetal formation (acid catalysis): protonate C=O, add ROH (hemiacetal), protonate OH and lose water to give an oxocarbenium ion, add a second ROH, lose H+. All steps are reversible; removal of water drives it, and acetals are stable to bases and nucleophiles, so they are used as protecting groups.
  • Imine formation: addition of RNH2 gives a carbinolamine, and acid-catalysed loss of water gives an iminium ion and then the imine. The pH must be a compromise: too acidic and the amine is protonated, too basic and there is no acid to convert OH into water.
  • Wittig reaction: the ylide carbon attacks C=O to give a betaine or oxaphosphetane; collapse of the four-membered ring gives the alkene and triphenylphosphine oxide. The strong P=O bond is the driving force.

12.6 Nucleophilic acyl substitution

Base hydrolysis of an ester (BAC2): addition, elimination, then irreversible proton transfer
  • Addition–elimination: the nucleophile adds to C=O to give a tetrahedral intermediate, and the best leaving group is then expelled with reformation of C=O. The reactivity of the derivatives follows the ability of the leaving group: acid chloride > anhydride > ester, carboxylic acid > amide.
  • Acid-catalysed esterification (Fischer): protonation of the carbonyl oxygen makes the carbon more electrophilic; the alcohol adds; a proton is transferred; water leaves; the ester is deprotonated. Every step is reversible, so the equilibrium is shifted by an excess of one reagent or removal of water. Labelling with 18O in the alcohol shows that the alkoxy oxygen of the ester comes from the alcohol, so the acyl C–O bond is broken, not the alkyl C–O bond.
  • Base hydrolysis (saponification): hydroxide adds, alkoxide leaves, and the alkoxide removes the proton from the carboxylic acid. This last step is irreversible, which is why saponification goes to completion but esterification does not.

12.7 Enolate chemistry: the aldol reaction

Aldol addition: enolate formation, attack on a second carbonyl, protonation
  • The base removes the α-hydrogen to give the enolate (the HOMO is largest on carbon).
  • The enolate carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule, forming the C–C bond and an alkoxide.
  • Protonation gives the β-hydroxy carbonyl compound. On heating, E1cB loss of water gives the conjugated enone; conjugation helps the elimination.
  • Crossed aldol reactions are efficient when one partner has no α-hydrogen (benzaldehyde, formaldehyde).

12.9 Nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr)

SNAr: addition–elimination through the nitro-stabilised Meisenheimer complex
  • The nucleophile adds to the ring carbon that carries the leaving group (rate-determining) to give the Meisenheimer complex, an anionic cyclohexadienyl intermediate whose negative charge is delocalised onto the ortho and para nitro groups.
  • Loss of chloride restores aromaticity. Each ortho or para nitro group greatly accelerates the reaction because it delocalises the negative charge of the intermediate; a nitro group meta to the leaving group cannot delocalise the charge and has little effect.
  • Because the addition is the slow step, the leaving-group order is F > Cl ≈ Br > I: the electronegative fluorine makes the ipso carbon more electrophilic, and the C–F bond is not broken in the slow step.

12.10 Rearrangement mechanisms

  • Baeyer–Villiger oxidation. A ketone with a peracid gives an ester: an oxygen atom is inserted next to the carbonyl group. The peracid adds to C=O (the intermediate resembles a semipinacol intermediate with one carbon replaced by oxygen), and a group migrates from carbon to oxygen as carboxylate leaves. When the two groups differ, the one better able to stabilise positive charge migrates: roughly tertiary alkyl > secondary alkyl ≈ phenyl > ethyl > methyl; primary groups migrate much less readily than secondary groups or aryl groups. Which group migrates in other rearrangements (pinacol, semipinacol, dienone–phenol) depends on the system and cannot be taken over from this order.

12.11 Pericyclic reactions: the Diels–Alder reaction

The Diels–Alder reaction is a concerted [4+2] cycloaddition: the diene (in the s-cis conformation) and the dienophile react in one cyclic transition state with no intermediate. It is stereospecific (suprafacial on both components) and the dienophile geometry is retained.

  • Why it is allowed. In the frontier-orbital picture the HOMO of the diene (ψ2, Hückel coefficients 0.60, 0.37, −0.37, −0.60) and the LUMO of the dienophile (π*, coefficients 0.71 and −0.71) have opposite signs at their two ends, so both new σ bonds form with in-phase overlap in one step. The reaction is fastest when the diene is electron-rich (high HOMO) and the dienophile electron-poor (low LUMO), because that narrows the HOMO–LUMO gap.
  • Endo preference. With a dienophile bearing an unsaturated substituent (maleic anhydride, acrylates) the kinetically preferred product is the endo isomer, in which the substituent lies under the new double bond in the transition state (secondary orbital interaction); the exo product is more stable and is obtained if the reaction is reversible.

Think Further Explain why the Diels–Alder reaction of butadiene with ethene is slow but that of butadiene with maleic anhydride is fast, using the frontier orbital picture.
Show solution
The reaction is controlled by the interaction of the diene HOMO with the dienophile LUMO. The two carbonyl groups of maleic anhydride lower the energy of the dienophile LUMO, which narrows the HOMO–LUMO gap and strengthens the stabilising interaction; ethene has a high-lying LUMO, so the gap is large and the reaction is slow.
