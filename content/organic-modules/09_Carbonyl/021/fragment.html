17. Addition–elimination with nitrogen nucleophiles

17.1 General mechanism Ammonia and its derivatives Z–NH2 (Z = H, R, OH, NH2, NHPh, NHCONH2) add to C=O to give a tetrahedral carbinolamine (hemiaminal) which loses water to give a C=N compound. The overall result is replacement of the carbonyl oxygen by =N–Z.

  • Step 1: nucleophilic attack by nitrogen lone pair on carbon (the N-nucleophile must be unprotonated).
  • Step 2: proton transfers give the neutral carbinolamine R2C(OH)–NHZ.
  • Step 3: acid protonates the OH (a poor leaving group otherwise), water leaves, assisted by the N lone pair, forming the iminium ion R2C=N+HZ.
  • Step 4: loss of H+ from nitrogen gives the product R2C=NZ.
+ H⁺ (cat.)⟶ −H₂O⟶
Propanone + hydrazine: carbinolamine then hydrazone

17.2 The pH profile: why mild acid (pH about 4–5) The reaction has a bell-shaped rate versus pH curve. Two opposing effects:

  • At low pH, the amine (a base) is protonated to Z–NH3+, which has no lone pair and cannot attack. The free-amine concentration drops by a factor of 10 for each pH unit below its pKa.
  • At high pH, there is too little acid to protonate the carbinolamine OH, so the dehydration step (step 3) becomes rate-determining and slow.

The maximum is near pH 4–5 where there is enough free amine and enough acid. For weakly basic reagents (2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine, pKa of conjugate acid about 1), the optimum shifts to a lower pH (reaction is done in strong acid/alcohol solution, Brady's reagent).

Key idea "Why is a strong acid not used as catalyst for the reaction of RCHO with NH2OH?" Answer: the strongly acidic medium converts NH2OH into NH3OH+, which is not nucleophilic; reaction rate falls. Medium of pH about 4.5 (acetate buffer) is optimum.

Derivatives of carbonyl compounds

The 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazones are coloured because of extended conjugation through the N–N and the nitroaryl ring: yellow for non-conjugated carbonyl compounds, orange to red when the carbonyl is conjugated with C=C or an aromatic ring. This is a standard test for any aldehyde or ketone ("Brady's test"). Esters, acids, amides and acid chlorides do not react because the carbonyl carbon is stabilised by the adjacent heteroatom's lone pair. 17.4 Why semicarbazide reacts through only one of its two nitrogens Semicarbazide H2N–NH–CO–NH2 has two –NH2 groups. The NH2 attached to C=O is an amide-type nitrogen: its lone pair is delocalised into the C=O and is not nucleophilic. The other NH2, attached to the NH of the hydrazine unit, is the nucleophile, so the semicarbazone is R2C=N–NH–CO–NH2.

Trap Semicarbazide is not an amide-nucleophile. If asked "which N of semicarbazide attacks", the answer is the terminal –NH2 of the hydrazine part (N-1), because the amide –NH2 lone pair is delocalised.
17.5 Stereochemistry of C=N: oximes
The C=N bond is planar and rotation about it is restricted, so aldoximes and the oximes of unsymmetrical ketones exist as geometric isomers: syn/anti or E/Z. The OH on nitrogen takes the place of a substituent. Acetone oxime has no isomer (two identical CH3 groups); butanone oxime and benzaldehyde oxime each have two.
(E)-benzaldehyde oxime (Z)-benzaldehyde oxime (E)-butanone oxime (Z)-butanone oxime
Geometrical isomers of oximes

CIP priority at carbon: the substituent of higher priority is compared with OH on nitrogen (the lone pair on N is the lowest priority). In (E)-benzaldehyde oxime the OH and phenyl are on opposite sides.

Shortcut Number of stereoisomers of an oxime R1R2C=N–OH: two if R1≠R2, one if R1=R2. For a dioxime (e.g. butane-2,3-dione dioxime = dimethylglyoxime) with two C=N units, there are up to 3 geometric isomers (EE, ZZ, EZ), because EZ and ZE are the same molecule when both ends are identical.
17.6 Beckmann rearrangement
Ketoximes treated with acid (conc. H2SO4, PCl5, polyphosphoric acid, SOCl2) rearrange to N-substituted amides. The group anti to the leaving OH migrates from carbon to nitrogen, and water adds to the nitrilium ion to give the amide. Cyclohexanone oxime → ε-caprolactam (monomer of nylon-6).

Trap Beckmann rearrangement is stereospecific: the group anti to –OH migrates, not the "better" group. Acetophenone oxime is formed mainly as the E isomer (OH anti to Ph), so Ph migrates; this is an outcome of geometry. A frequent error is to apply the Baeyer–Villiger migratory aptitude table here.
17.7 Imines and enamines
Primary amines give imines (Schiff bases). They are formed best from aromatic aldehydes (stabilised by conjugation) and are hydrolysed by aqueous acid (the reverse reaction). Secondary amines give enamines when the carbonyl compound has an α-hydrogen: the carbinolamine cannot lose a proton from N (there is none), so iminium ion loses an α-proton instead.
+ H⁺, −H₂O (Dean–Stark)⟶
Cyclohexanone + pyrrolidine → enamine

Enamines are C-nucleophiles (the β-carbon is nucleophilic because the N lone pair is conjugated with C=C: N–C=C ↔ N+=C–C−). In the Stork enamine reaction, the enamine is alkylated or acylated at carbon and hydrolysed back to the carbonyl compound: a milder substitute for enolate alkylation without polyalkylation or self-condensation.

+ (i) Michael addition (ii) H₃O⁺⟶
Stork reaction with methyl vinyl ketone: 2-(3-oxobutyl)cyclohexanone

17.8 Ammonia with HCHO and CH3CHO

  • 6 HCHO + 4 NH3 → (CH2)6N4 + 6 H2O. Hexamethylenetetramine (urotropine, hexamine); an adamantane-like cage; used as urinary antiseptic and in the Duff formylation. Nitration gives the explosive RDX.
  • 3 CH3CHO + 3 NH3 gives the cyclic trimer of aldehyde-ammonia, 2,4,6-trimethyl-1,3,5-triazinane trihydrate; hydrolysed back by acid.
+ 6 HCHO + 4 NH₃ → −6 H₂O⟶

Example 16 Adv

Beckmann

Identify the product of: (a) butanone oxime (E) with PCl5; (b) cyclopentanone oxime with conc. H2SO4; (c) benzaldehyde oxime heated with ethanoic anhydride.

Show solution
  1. (a) The group anti to OH migrates. In the (E) isomer the ethyl group is anti to OH (E: higher CIP priority group on C, ethyl, opposite to OH). Ethyl migrates: N-ethylethanamide CH3CONHC2H5. (The Z isomer would give N-methylpropanamide.)
  2. (b) Ring expansion: δ-valerolactam (piperidin-2-one).
  3. (c) Aldoximes whose H is anti to OH are dehydrated to the nitrile: benzonitrile, not an amide.

Example 17 Adv

pH and rate

Why does the rate of oxime formation from acetone and NH2OH pass through a maximum near pH 4.5 and fall at both lower and higher pH?

Show solution
  1. At low pH (say 1) hydroxylamine (conjugate acid pKa about 6) is almost wholly NH3OH+, which has no lone pair, so the attack step (step 1) is starved of nucleophile.
  2. At high pH (say 11) all the hydroxylamine is free, but there is too little acid to protonate the OH of the carbinolamine; dehydration becomes rate-determining and slow.
  3. Near pH 4–5 there is enough free NH2OH and enough acid: the rate is a maximum.
Example 18 Adv

Distinguish

Distinguish between (a) acetone and acetone oxime, (b) benzaldehyde and benzaldehyde phenylhydrazone, (c) HCHO and CH3CHO using ammonia.

Show solution
  1. (a) Acetone gives a yellow-orange 2,4-DNP precipitate; the oxime does not (it has no C=O). Alternatively boil with dilute acid, then test.
  2. (b) Tollens' reagent: benzaldehyde gives a silver mirror, the phenylhydrazone does not (until hydrolysed).
  3. (c) HCHO + NH3 gives crystalline hexamethylenetetramine (white solid, no odour); CH3CHO gives aldehyde-ammonia, an unstable adduct. Simpler: iodoform test (ethanal positive).
