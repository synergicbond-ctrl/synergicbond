23. α-Halogenation

23.1 Acid-catalysed halogenation: monohalogenation In acetic acid (or aqueous acid), an aldehyde or ketone reacts with Br2, Cl2 or I2 to replace one α-H by halogen. The slow step is enol formation; the enol (a nucleophilic alkene) attacks X2.

Br₂, CH₃COOH (H⁺ cat.)⟶ +
Bromoacetone and HBr

The introduction of one halogen makes the carbonyl less basic (the halogen is electron-withdrawing), so the second protonation/enolisation is slower: reaction stops at the monohalo stage. For an unsymmetrical ketone, the halogen goes to the carbon that forms the more stable (more substituted) enol, i.e. the more substituted α-carbon.

Br₂, H⁺⟶
Butanone in acid gives 3-bromobutan-2-one (more substituted α-carbon)

23.2 Base-promoted halogenation: polyhalogenation In aqueous base, the reaction goes by the enolate. Each halogen introduced makes the remaining α-H more acidic (−I effect stabilises the enolate), so the next substitution is faster than the first. All α-hydrogens on the same carbon are replaced, and for a methyl ketone the trihalomethyl ketone eventually undergoes haloform cleavage (Section 24).

Key idea Acid: mono, via enol, more substituted carbon. Base: poly (exhaustive), via enolate, the less substituted (methyl) carbon is attacked first for methyl ketones (more acidic, less hindered).
23.3 Follow-up chemistry of α-halo ketones
  • E2 elimination (LiBr/Li2CO3 in DMF, or pyridine, heat) gives the α,β-unsaturated ketone; a way to introduce C=C next to C=O.
  • SN2 with nucleophiles is very fast (the carbonyl accelerates displacement at the α-carbon): α-halo ketones are potent lachrymators and alkylating agents.
  • Favorskii rearrangement: α-halo ketone with an α′-H and alkoxide gives a rearranged ester (ring contraction for cyclic α-halo ketones) through a cyclopropanone intermediate.
NaOCH₃, CH₃OH⟶
Favorskii: 2-chlorocyclohexanone → methyl cyclopentanecarboxylate
LiBr, Li₂CO₃, DMF, Δ⟶
Dehydrohalogenation: cyclohex-2-en-1-one
