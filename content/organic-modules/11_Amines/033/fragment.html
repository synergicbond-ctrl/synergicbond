33. Nitriles (cyanides) and isocyanides (carbylamines)

Both are C,N isomers, R–C≡N and R–N+≡C−. The cyanide ion is an ambident nucleophile: C-attack gives nitriles; N-attack gives isocyanides.

acetonitrile (methyl cyanide) methyl isocyanide benzonitrile phenyl isocyanide
Cyanides and isocyanides (isomers)
  • In RCN the carbon is sp-hybridised and the N lone pair is in an sp orbital (poorly basic). The dipole moment (CH3CN ≈ 3.9 D) leads to high b.p. (82 °C) and miscibility with water.
  • In RNC, the formal charges (N+, C−) can be written as a carbene form R–N+≡C− ↔ R–N=C: ; the terminal carbon has a lone pair (nucleophilic) and an electrophilic character, behaves like a carbene (adds electrophile and then nucleophile to the same carbon – α-addition). Isocyanides have vile odour, lower b.p. than the nitriles (CH3NC 59 °C vs 82 °C).

33.2 Reactions of nitriles
  • Hydrolysis: RCN + H2O/H+ (or OH−, heat) → RCONH2 → RCOOH + NH4+. Stopping at the amide: conc. H2SO4 (cold), or H2O2/NaOH, or acid (HCl, 40 °C).
  • Reduction: LiAlH4, H2/Ni or Na/C2H5OH → RCH2NH2; SnCl2/HCl (Stephen) or DIBAL-H → RCHO.
  • Grignard reagent: RCN + R'MgX → R–C(=NMgX)R' → H3O+ → ketone RCOR' (the imine salt is not attacked again). Useful for ketone synthesis: CH3CN + C6H5MgBr → acetophenone.
  • Alcoholysis (Pinner) RCN + R'OH/HCl → imidate salt → ester with water.
  • α-Hydrogens are acidic (pKa ≈ 25–31): CH3CN forms carbanions with strong bases.

33.3 Reactions of isocyanides
  • Hydrolysis: stable to alkali, but hydrolysed by dilute mineral acids to primary amine + formic acid: RNC + 2H2O → RNH2 + HCOOH. (This is a proof that N is attached to R.)
  • Reduction: H2/Ni or Na/EtOH → secondary amine R–NH–CH3 (N-methyl amine). The isocyanide carbon becomes a CH3 on N. (Contrast with RCN → RCH2NH2, a primary amine.)
  • Isomerisation: heating at ≈ 523 K converts RNC to the more stable RCN (1,2-shift).
  • Oxidation/addition at C: HgO → isocyanate R–N=C=O; sulphur → isothiocyanate R–N=C=S; Cl2 → RN=CCl2 (carbylamine chloride).

Example 36 Main

Isomeric products

Compound A (C3H5N) on hydrolysis with dilute HCl gives a carboxylic acid and NH4Cl; its isomer B has a very unpleasant smell and gives a secondary amine on reduction. Identify A and B.

Show solution
  1. A: propanenitrile CH3CH2CN (hydrolysis gives propanoic acid + NH4Cl). B: ethyl isocyanide CH3CH2NC; reduction gives N-methylethanamine CH3CH2NHCH3 (secondary).
Example 37 Adv

Carbon counting

Ethyl bromide → X (KCN) → Y (H3O+, heat) → Z (SOCl2) → W (NH3) → V (Br2/KOH). Identify X to V and the carbon count at each stage.

Show solution
  1. X = propanenitrile (3 C: one carbon gained); Y = propanoic acid (3 C); Z = propanoyl chloride (3 C); W = propanamide (3 C); V = ethanamine (2 C: the Hofmann step loses one carbon). The sequence turns ethyl bromide into ethylamine after a detour through a C3 acid.
Example 38 Adv

Ambident reagents

Why does benzyl bromide give PhCH2CN with NaCN in DMSO but a significant amount of PhCH2NC with AgCN?

Show solution
  1. Sodium cyanide is ionic: the cyanide ion is free, and its carbon end (the better nucleophilic site) attacks the sp3 carbon. In AgCN the Ag–C bond is covalent; the carbon is shielded by Ag+, which also helps to remove Br− (SN1-like character), so the N lone pair attacks the carbocation-like centre.

How to read a mechanism entry below: each step is one bond-making/bond-breaking event, shown as a drawn structure; the line under it says what the curved arrow does and why the step happens (charge, orbital overlap, leaving-group ability). The "MO lens" lines name the orbitals (HOMO = highest occupied, LUMO = lowest unoccupied) whose overlap drives the step. Rule of thumb for any polar reaction: the nucleophile's HOMO donates into the electrophile's LUMO; the closer in energy and the better the overlap, the faster the reaction.
