16. Claisen condensation

16.1 The reaction and the mechanism

Two molecules of an ester with α-hydrogens condense, in the presence of at least one full equivalent of alkoxide base, to give a β-keto ester and one molecule of alcohol. The typical example is the self-condensation of ethyl acetate (two C2 esters give one C4 keto ester): 2 CH3COOC2H5 → CH3COCH2COOC2H5 + C2H5OH.

NaOEt (− EtOH)⟶ + CH₃COOEt (attack at C=O)⟶ − EtO⁻⟶ EtO⁻ (final deprotonation, drives reaction)⟶
Claisen mechanism: enolate formation, nucleophilic acyl substitution, loss of ethoxide, deprotonation of the doubly activated C–H
  • 1. EtO− removes an α-H of ethyl acetate (unfavourable equilibrium, K ≈ 10−9) to give the enolate.
  • 2. The enolate carbon attacks the C=O of a second ester molecule: tetrahedral alkoxide (nucleophilic acyl substitution, addition step).
  • 3. The C=O reforms and EtO− is expelled: ethyl acetoacetate. The sum of steps 1–3 is thermoneutral or slightly uphill.
  • 4. EtO− removes the very acidic H (pKa 10.7) between the two carbonyls of the product: an essentially irreversible, strongly downhill step that pulls the whole sequence forward.
  • 5. Acid work-up (H3O+) protonates the stabilised enolate to give the neutral β-keto ester.
Key idea Driving force: the final deprotonation of the β-keto ester. Hence (i) a stoichiometric (not catalytic) amount of base is needed; (ii) the ester must have at least two α-hydrogens (one is lost in the new C–C bond formation, the other must remain to be removed in step 4).
Trap Ethyl isobutyrate (CH3)2CHCOOEt has only one α-H, so the Claisen product (CH3)2CHCO–C(CH3)2COOEt has no acidic H between the carbonyls and the equilibrium lies on the side of the starting material. NaOEt fails; a much stronger base (Ph3CNa, LDA) is needed. Similarly, esters without α-H (HCOOEt, PhCOOEt, (CH3)3CCOOEt, diethyl carbonate, diethyl oxalate) cannot form an enolate and act only as the electrophile.
Trap Alkoxide must match the ester. NaOMe with ethyl acetate causes transesterification (a mixture of methyl and ethyl esters). Use NaOEt with ethyl esters; NaOMe with methyl esters. Hydroxide must never be used (saponification competes).

16.2 Crossed (mixed) Claisen condensations

Two different esters give up to four products when both have α-H. A useful crossed Claisen needs one partner without α-H (used in excess), acting only as the electrophile:

The more reactive carbonyl electrophile (formate, oxalate) reacts fastest.
  • Ketone + ester: the ketone enolises more readily (pKa 19 vs 25), the ester is the acylating agent: acetone + EtOAc/NaOEt → acetylacetone (pentane-2,4-dione); acetophenone + EtOAc → benzoylacetone; cyclohexanone + diethyl carbonate → ethyl 2-oxocyclohexanecarboxylate; ketone + diethyl oxalate → α,γ-diketo esters.
  • Counting products: if both esters have α-H (e.g. ethyl acetate and ethyl propanoate) the mixture contains four β-keto esters: two self-condensation products, two crossed products.

Example 42 Adv

Counting products

Ethyl acetate and ethyl propanoate are mixed with NaOEt. How many different β-keto esters (constitutional) can form? How many if ethyl benzoate is added to ethyl acetate alone?

Show solution
  1. Enolate A (from CH3COOEt) or enolate B (from CH3CH2COOEt) can attack either ester: AA, AB, BA, BB, i.e. 4 products: CH3COCH2COOEt; CH3COCH(CH3)COOEt (enolate B on acetate); CH3CH2COCH2COOEt (enolate A on propanoate); CH3CH2COCH(CH3)COOEt.
  2. Ethyl acetate + ethyl benzoate: PhCOOEt cannot enolise: the products are ethyl acetoacetate (self) and ethyl benzoylacetate (crossed). Using excess PhCOOEt makes the crossed product main: 2 products.

16.3 Retro-Claisen (acid cleavage)

Concentrated hydroxide or ethoxide attacks the keto carbon of a β-keto ester that has no acidic C–H left (disubstituted at the α-carbon) and cleaves the C–C bond: RCOCR′2COOEt + OH− → RCOO− + R′2CHCOOEt (the Claisen in reverse). This is the basis of the acid cleavage of alkylated acetoacetic esters (Section 17).

16.4 The Dieckmann cyclisation

An intramolecular Claisen condensation of a diester gives a cyclic β-keto ester. The ring that closes contains the α-carbon, the ester carbonyl and the carbons between: 1,6-diesters (adipates) give five-membered rings; 1,7-diesters (pimelates) give six-membered rings; 1,5- and 1,4-diesters give strained four- and three-membered rings and do not cyclise (the intermolecular reaction dominates); seven-membered and larger rings form only under high dilution.

1. NaOEt, EtOH 2. H₃O⁺⟶
Diethyl adipate → ethyl 2-oxocyclopentanecarboxylate
1. NaOEt 2. H₃O⁺⟶
Diethyl pimelate → ethyl 2-oxocyclohexanecarboxylate

Because the cyclic β-keto ester has an acidic C–H (between C=O and CO2Et), the final deprotonation drives the reaction exactly as in the Claisen condensation. The product can be alkylated (RX/NaOEt) and then hydrolysed and decarboxylated (like acetoacetic ester) to give 2-substituted cyclopentanones or cyclohexanones.

Dieckmann ring size

Predict the product from (a) diethyl hexanedioate, (b) diethyl heptanedioate, (c) diethyl pentanedioate (glutarate) with NaOEt.

Show solution
  1. Example 44 Adv

Regiochemistry of a Dieckmann

Diethyl 2-methylhexanedioate, EtOOC–CH(CH3)–CH2CH2CH2–COOEt, is treated with one equivalent of NaOEt. Two cyclisations seem possible. Which product forms, and why?

Show solution
  1. (i) Enolate at C-5 (the CH2 next to the far ester) attacks the ester carbonyl at C-1: five-membered ring; the product ethyl 3-methyl-2-oxocyclopentane-1-carboxylate has an H between the two carbonyls, so it is deprotonated by ethoxide at the end (driving force).
  2. (ii) Enolate at C-2 (tertiary, bearing CH3) attacks C-6: the ring product would have a quaternary carbon (CH3 and CO2Et on the same carbon) and no acidic H between the carbonyls, so there is no driving force; also the hindered tertiary enolate forms less readily.
  3. Product: ethyl 3-methyl-2-oxocyclopentane-1-carboxylate (route i).
