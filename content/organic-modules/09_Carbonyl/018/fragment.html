14. The mechanism, in three lines

Everything in this part follows from one pattern: the nucleophile adds to carbon, the π electrons go to oxygen, and the alkoxide is protonated. The two catalytic variants are:

  • Nucleophilic (base-promoted) pathway – used by strong or anionic nucleophiles (CN−, RMgX, H−, RO−, acetylide). Step 1: Nu− attacks C (rate-determining). Step 2: the alkoxide takes H+ from a proton source.
  • Acid-catalysed pathway – used by weak neutral nucleophiles (ROH, H2O, RNH2, N2H4). Step 1: oxygen is protonated (or hydrogen-bonded), which makes carbon strongly electrophilic. Step 2: the neutral nucleophile attacks. Step 3: loss of H+.

Key idea Reversibility decides the product. If the nucleophile is a poor leaving group (carbanions R−, H−), the alkoxide formed cannot expel it and addition is irreversible (kinetic control): Grignard and hydride reductions go to completion. If the nucleophile is also a good leaving group (CN−, HSO3−, RO−/ROH, H2O, amines), addition is reversible and the position of the equilibrium matters: crowded ketones give little adduct at equilibrium.
Shortcut In an addition–elimination sequence (ammonia derivatives, acetal formation), the first stage is addition to C=O to give a tetrahedral intermediate; the second is loss of water. A question that asks "which compounds react" is asking whether the tetrahedral intermediate can lose H2O, and that depends on having the right N–H or O–H pattern.
