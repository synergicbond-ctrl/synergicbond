Practice 1 L1 Foundation

Which of the following eliminations are correctly classified by the relative positions of the two groups that are lost?

(A) Loss of HBr from CHBr3 with potassium tert-butoxide, giving :CBr2, is a 1,1- (α-) elimination.

(B) Loss of HI from 2-iodopentane to give pent-2-ene is a 1,2- (β-) elimination.

(C) Loss of ZnBr2 from 1,3-dibromopropane with zinc dust, giving cyclopropane, is a 1,3- (γ-) elimination.

(D) Loss of H2O from butan-2-ol to give but-2-ene is a 1,3- (γ-) elimination.

Show answer
  1. Classify by counting the atoms between the two atoms that carry the departing groups: the same atom gives a 1,1-elimination, adjacent atoms give 1,2, and atoms separated by one further atom give 1,3.
  2. A: H and Br leave from the same carbon and the product is a carbene, so the 1,1-label is correct. B: H and I leave from neighbouring carbons and a C=C bond forms, so it is a 1,2-elimination. C: the two Br atoms sit on C1 and C3, and ring closure joins them, so it is 1,3.
  3. D: in butan-2-ol the OH is on C2 and the lost H comes from C3 (or C1); these are adjacent carbons, so this is a 1,2- (β-) elimination, not 1,3. Statement D is wrong; the answer is A, B, C.

Answer: A,B,C

Practice 2 L1 Foundation

An E2 reaction obeys rate = k[R–X][B−]. Solvent is added until the volume doubles, so that both concentrations fall to one half of their original values (temperature unchanged). By what factor does the initial rate decrease?

Show answer
  1. Write the new rate with each concentration halved: rate′ = k(½[R–X])(½[B−]) = ¼ k[R–X][B−].
  2. The rate is first order in each reactant, so the factors multiply: (½)(½) = ¼. The rate falls by a factor of 4 (an E1 reaction under the same dilution would fall only by 2, because only [R–X] appears).

Answer: 4

Practice 3 L1 Foundation

Which description fits the reaction-energy profile of a concerted E2 elimination?

(A) One transition state and no intermediate; both the substrate and the base are involved in it.

(B) Two transition states separated by a carbocation intermediate.

(C) Two transition states separated by a carbanion intermediate.

(D) One cyclic transition state in which no external base takes part.

Show answer
  1. E2 is a single, concerted step: the C–H bond breaks, the C=C π bond forms and the C–X bond breaks together, so the profile has one maximum and no minimum between reactants and products.
  2. B describes E1, C describes E1cB, and D describes thermal syn eliminations (Ei) such as ester pyrolysis. Only A matches E2.

Answer: A

Practice 4 L1 Foundation

For one fixed secondary bromide in a suitable solvent, the E2 rate with the following bases decreases in the order

(A) NH2− > EtO− > CH3COO− > I−

(B) I− > CH3COO− > EtO− > NH2−

(C) EtO− > NH2− > I− > CH3COO−

(D) CH3COO− > EtO− > NH2− > I−

Show answer
  1. The base is removing a proton in the rate-determining step, so the E2 rate follows base strength, i.e. the pKa of the conjugate acid.
  2. Conjugate-acid pKa values: NH3 about 38, EtOH about 16, CH3COOH about 5, HI about −10. Hence basicity and E2 rate fall in the order NH2− > EtO− > CH3COO− > I−, which is option A.

Answer: A

Practice 5 L1 Foundation

Raising the temperature increases the proportion of elimination relative to substitution for most halide/base pairs. The best explanation is that

(A) elimination converts the same reactants into more product molecules, so its activation entropy is more favourable and the –TΔS‡ term helps it more as T rises

(B) substitution is endothermic while elimination is exothermic, so heating shifts the equilibrium

(C) heat converts the nucleophile into a stronger base without changing the substrate

(D) the leaving group becomes a better leaving group only in the elimination pathway when heated

Show answer
  1. Compare particle counts. Elimination: R–X + B− gives alkene + HB + X− (two molecules become three). Substitution: R–X + Nu− gives R–Nu + X− (two become two).
  2. In ΔG‡ = ΔH‡ − TΔS‡ the more favourable entropy term of elimination is multiplied by T, so it offsets its larger enthalpy barrier increasingly as temperature rises. Options B, C and D invent effects that do not exist.

Answer: A

Practice 6 L2 Main

Taken together, which of the following observations support a concerted E2 mechanism and argue against an earlier carbanion mechanism in which the C–H bond is broken first and the halide leaves afterwards?

(A) When the reaction is interrupted in EtO−/EtOD, the unreacted halide contains no deuterium.

(B) With the same base, the rate constants for R–Cl, R–Br and R–I differ strongly, in the order I > Br > Cl.

(C) Replacing the β-hydrogens by deuterium slows the reaction by a factor of about five.

(D) The alkene is accompanied by an isomer with a rearranged carbon skeleton.

Show answer
  1. A reversible carbanion would exchange with EtOD before eliminating, so recovered starting material would carry deuterium; its absence (A) argues against reversible carbanion formation.
  2. If proton removal were slow and irreversible, the nature of X would not affect the rate. A strong dependence on X (B) shows that C–X cleavage is part of the rate-determining step. A primary isotope effect of several units (C) shows that C–H cleavage is also in that step; both bonds therefore break in one step, which is E2.
  3. Rearranged skeletons (D) are a signature of a carbocation (E1), not of E2, so D is not supporting evidence. Answer: A, B, C.

Answer: A,B,C

Practice 7 Worked example

3-Chloro-1-phenylpropan-1-one, PhCOCH2CH2Cl, gives phenyl vinyl ketone in dilute NaOD/D2O. Observed: (i) at low conversion rate = k[ketone][OD−]; (ii) unreacted ketone recovered from a half-finished run has deuterium on the CH2 group next to C=O; (iii) the reaction is much faster for the bromide than for the chloride. Which mechanism fits all three observations?

(A) E1: ionisation of C–Cl to a carbocation, then loss of H+

(B) Concerted E2

(C) E1cB: fast reversible formation of the enolate, then rate-limiting loss of Cl−

(D) SN2 attack of OD− at the CH2Cl carbon

Show answer
  1. Observation (i) needs the base in the rate law, which excludes E1 (rate independent of base). Observation (ii) shows that the C–H bond next to C=O is broken and re-formed faster than product forms, i.e. a carbanion (enolate) is made reversibly. A concerted E2 gives no exchange in recovered substrate.
  2. Observation (iii) shows that C–Cl/C–Br cleavage occurs in the slow step. If proton removal were slow and irreversible, the halogen would not matter, so the slow step must be the expulsion of X− from the enolate.
  3. Pre-equilibrium: ketone + OD− ⇌ enolate (K). Slow: enolate → CH2=CH–COPh + Cl− (k2). Rate = k2K[ketone][OD−], second order, as in (i).
  4. The acidic α-hydrogen (stabilised by the carbonyl) and a modest leaving group are the textbook set-up for E1cB; the product is conjugated with C=O. Answer C. A (E1) is excluded by (i); B by (ii); D does not form an alkene and would not exchange the α-hydrogens.

Answer: C

Practice 9 Worked example

Bromocyclohexane carrying one deuterium on C2 (a CHD group next to the CHBr carbon) is treated with NaOEt in ethanol. Both D- and H-removal give cyclohexene. Assume that the primary isotope effect is kH/kD = 6.0 for removal of a given β-hydrogen versus a given β-deuterium, and that every β C–H and C–D bond is equally accessible (ignore the chair restrictions). What is the ratio (cyclohexene containing one D) : (cyclohexene containing no D)?

Show answer
  1. List the β-positions: C2 carries one H and one D; C6 carries two H. So there are three β C–H bonds and one β C–D bond.
  2. Removing any hydrogen leaves the deuterium in the molecule, giving cyclohexene-d1. Removing the deuterium gives ordinary cyclohexene (d0).
  3. Let k be the rate constant for removing one H. Then kD = k/6. Rate to d1 = 3k. Rate to d0 = k/6.
  4. Ratio d1 : d0 = 3k ÷ (k/6) = 3 × 6 = 18, i.e. 18 : 1 (d0 is only about 5% of the alkene).
  5. Check on the logic: if there were no isotope effect the ratio would be statistical, 3 : 1; the isotope effect multiplies this by kH/kD = 6.

Answer: 18

Practice 11 Worked example

Which statements about competition between substitution and elimination are correct?

(A) 1-Bromobutane with NaOEt in ethanol gives mainly butyl ethyl ether (SN2).

(B) 2-Bromobutane with NaOEt in ethanol gives mainly alkenes (E2).

(C) 2-Bromo-2-methylbutane with NaOEt in ethanol cannot give an ether by SN2; any ether that appears forms by SN1.

(D) 1-Bromobutane with KOC(CH3)3 in tert-butanol gives mainly butyl tert-butyl ether.

Show answer
  1. Step 1, primary substrate: the α-carbon is unhindered, so a small strong nucleophile/base such as EtO− reaches it easily and SN2 outruns E2. Statement A is true.
  2. Step 2, secondary substrate: attack at carbon is slowed by the two alkyl groups, while removal of a peripheral β-hydrogen is not, so a strong base gives mostly E2. B is true.
  3. Step 3, tertiary substrate: the back side of the α-carbon is blocked, so SN2 is impossible. With a strong base only E2 occurs; some ether can come from SN1 through the carbocation (which also feeds E1). C is true.
  4. Step 4, bulky base: tert-butoxide is too hindered to attack carbon but removes an exposed β-hydrogen, so even a primary bromide gives mainly alkene (but-1-ene). D is false.
  5. Answer: A, B, C.

Answer: A,B,C

Practice 14 L2 Main

2-Bromopropane is treated with NaOH. In 60% ethanol/40% water the alkene fraction is smaller than with sodium ethoxide in absolute ethanol. The best reason is that

(A) the more polar medium, with a localised charge on the attacking oxygen, favours substitution more than elimination, while the less polar medium favours the charge-dispersed E2 transition state

(B) water is a stronger base than ethoxide

(C) water converts the secondary substrate into a tertiary one

(D) ethanol cannot act as a nucleophile at all

Show answer
  1. In the E2 transition state the negative charge of the base is spread over five atoms (base, H, two carbons, leaving group); in the SN2 transition state it is more concentrated.

Practice 15 L2 Main

2-Bromo-2-methylbutane gives mostly alkenes plus a small amount of ether with NaOEt in ethanol at 25 °C. At a higher temperature the proportion of ether falls further. Which statement is correct?

(A) The ether arises from SN1 reaction of the carbocation formed in the competing unimolecular path; a higher temperature favours elimination because of its larger entropy gain.

(B) The ether arises from SN2 at the tertiary carbon, and heating destroys the nucleophile.

Practice 28 Worked example

(2S,3S)-2-Bromo-3-phenylbutane is treated with sodium ethoxide in ethanol. Considering only anti-periplanar loss of HBr across C2–C3 (the hydrogen on C3 and the bromine on C2), give the alkene with its correct geometry.

Show answer
  1. Step 1: C3 carries a single hydrogen, so the geometry of the alkene is fixed by the conformation in which that hydrogen is anti-periplanar (180°) to the C2–Br bond. There is no choice of hydrogen to average over.
  2. Step 2: look along the C2–C3 bond. On C2 the groups other than Br are CH3 and H. On C3 the groups other than H are CH3 and phenyl. With H(C3) and Br(C2) placed 180° apart, the (2S,3S) configuration puts the phenyl group on C3 anti to the CH3 group on C2 (and gauche to the H on C2); the two methyl groups are gauche.
  3. Step 3: groups that are gauche to one another in this conformation end up on the same side of the new C=C, and groups that are anti end up on opposite sides. So phenyl and the C2 methyl are trans, and the two methyls are cis.
  4. Step 4: CIP priorities: on C3, phenyl outranks methyl; on C2, methyl outranks H. Phenyl and the C2 methyl are on opposite sides, so the alkene is (E)-2-phenylbut-2-ene.
  5. Step 5: cross-check: the (2S,3R) diastereomer would place phenyl gauche to the C2 methyl and therefore give the Z isomer. The same elimination can also go toward C1, but the question restricts the pathway to C2–C3.

  1. Step 1: pyrrolidine is a secondary amine; two equivalents of CH3I give N,N-dimethylpyrrolidinium iodide.
  2. Step 2: Ag2O/H2O gives the hydroxide; heating causes a first Hofmann elimination. HO− removes a hydrogen from C3 (β to nitrogen) and the C2–N bond breaks, so the ring opens to CH2=CH–CH2–CH2–N(CH3)2 (N,N-dimethylbut-3-en-1-amine).
  3. Step 3: a third CH3I gives CH2=CH–CH2–CH2–N+(CH3)3 I−; Ag2O/H2O gives the hydroxide.
  4. Step 4: the second Hofmann elimination can only use the hydrogens on the carbon β to nitrogen, which are the allylic CH2 hydrogens (acidic and leading to a conjugated product). Loss of H from this carbon and N(CH3)3 from the next gives CH2=CH–CH=CH2.
  5. Step 5: the hydrocarbon is buta-1,3-diene; total CH3I used is three per pyrrolidine.

Show answer

Practice 54 Worked example

3,3-Dimethylbutan-2-ol is converted into its S-methyl xanthate: (1) NaOH, then CS2; (2) CH3I. The xanthate is then heated (Chugaev elimination). Give the alkene formed and state why no rearranged alkene is obtained.

Show answer
  1. Step 1: the alcohol is converted by NaOH into its alkoxide, which attacks CS2 to give the sodium O-alkyl xanthate, R–O–C(=S)–S− Na+.
  2. Step 2: CH3I alkylates sulfur (SN2), giving the methyl xanthate ester, R–O–C(=S)–S–CH3.
  3. Step 3: on heating, the thiocarbonyl sulfur removes a β-hydrogen through a six-membered cyclic transition state. The C–O bond breaks and the products are the alkene, COS and CH3SH. The reaction is a syn elimination.

  5. Step 5: no carbocation is formed, so no methyl shift can occur. (Acid-catalysed dehydration of the same alcohol goes through a secondary cation that rearranges to 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene.)

Practice 56 L2 Main

Which statements apply to thermal Ei eliminations such as acetate pyrolysis, the Chugaev reaction and the Cope elimination?

(A) Their cyclic five- or six-membered transition states cause a large negative entropy of activation.

(B) The hydrogen and the leaving group depart from the same side of the C–C bond in a syn arrangement.

(C) No carbocation forms, so the carbon skeleton does not rearrange.

(D) The rate increases in proportion to the concentration of an added strong base.

Show answer
  1. Gathering the reacting atoms into a ring costs freedom of motion, so the entropy of activation is strongly negative; this is part of the evidence for a cyclic transition state (A).
  2. The ring can only be completed if the β-hydrogen and the leaving group are on the same face, so the elimination is syn (B). Ionisation does not occur, so there is no rearrangement (C).
  3. The base that takes the proton is part of the molecule (carbonyl or thiocarbonyl atom, or the N-oxide oxygen), so an external base has no effect (D false). Answer: A, B, C.

Answer: A,B,C

Practice 63 Worked example

2,2-Dimethylcyclohexanol is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid. Give the major alkene, and explain how it forms.

Show answer
  1. Step 1: H3PO4 protonates the OH group, and loss of water gives a secondary carbocation at C1.
  2. Step 2: the neighbouring carbon C2 is quaternary, so no β-hydrogen is available toward C2, and the secondary cation is high in energy. A 1,2-methyl shift from C2 to C1 converts it into a tertiary cation at C2 (1,2-dimethylcyclohexyl cation).
  3. Step 3: loss of a proton from the neighbouring carbon C1 (now bearing the shifted methyl and one H) gives the tetrasubstituted alkene 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene, the most stable alkene available (Zaitsev). Loss from C3 or from a methyl group would give less substituted alkenes.
  4. Step 4: the rearranged skeleton is typical of E1 elimination from alcohols under acid.
