6.2 Glycoside formation and the anomeric effect

Step 1: H+ on the anomeric OH⟶ Step 2: − H2O⟶
Hemiacetal → oxonium ion → oxocarbenium ion
Step 3: CH3OH adds to C-1⟶ Step 4: − H+⟶
Oxocarbenium + methanol → methyl α-D-glucopyranoside (drawn: the α-product; the β-product forms by attack on the opposite face)
  • Step 1. The anomeric OH is protonated. Why this oxygen: loss of water from here is assisted by the ring oxygen's lone pair, so it leads to the stabilised oxocarbenium ion; protonating the ring oxygen instead only leads to ring opening (also reversible, and part of mutarotation).
  • Step 2. Water leaves, assisted by the ring oxygen lone pair, giving the oxocarbenium ion: a resonance hybrid C+–O ↔ C=O+. MO view: the lone pair of O-5 donates into the empty p orbital on C-1 (the LUMO of the cation), forming a partial π bond. This 2-centre/2-electron stabilisation (the same as a carbonyl) is why acetal formation is easy: it is far lower in energy than a simple carbocation.
  • Step 3. Methanol attacks the planar cationic carbon from the top or bottom face, its O lone pair filling the C=O+ π*. Both faces are open: α/β mixture.
  • Step 4. Loss of H+ gives the neutral acetal. Under thermodynamic control (acid, long time) the α-glycoside predominates.

Why the α-anomer is favoured: the anomeric effect in MO language

In the α-glycoside the C-1–OMe bond is axial and antiperiplanar to a lone pair on the ring oxygen (O-5). That lone pair (nO) can overlap with σ*C1–OMe: the donation lowers the energy by ≈ 4–8 kJ mol−1. In the β-glycoside (equatorial C–OMe) no ring lone pair is antiperiplanar to the C1–O1 bond, so there is no n → σ* gain. The same n → σ* interaction shortens the C1–O5 bond and lengthens C1–O1 in axial glycosides. Hydrolysis reverses steps 1–4 and also passes through the oxocarbenium ion, so a glycoside survives base but not acid.
