6. Cleavage of ethers by HI and HBr

Ethers are cleaved by concentrated HI or HBr (HCl is too weak a source of nucleophile) on heating. The first step is protonation of oxygen, which converts RO− into a good leaving group, neutral ROH. The halide then attacks one of the two carbon atoms.

  1. Protonation: R–O–R′ + HX → R–O+(H)–R′ + X−.
  2. If one group is methyl, primary or secondary, the halide attacks the less hindered carbon by SN2, producing the alkyl halide of the smaller group and the alcohol of the larger group.
  3. If one group is tertiary, benzylic or allylic, the C–O bond to that group ionises (SN1) and the halide is captured by the stable carbocation.
  4. With excess HX and heat, the alcohol formed is itself converted into an alkyl halide, so two molecules of RX result and water is formed.

Reactant:

Reagent: HI (1 equivalent), heat

Product:

SN2 at the methyl carbon: iodomethane and propan-2-ol.

Reactant:

Reagent: HI (1 equivalent), cold

Product:

SN1 through the tert-butyl cation: 2-iodo-2-methylpropane and methanol.

Reactant:

Reagent: HI, heat

Product:

Aryl alkyl ethers always give a phenol and an alkyl halide.

Aryl alkyl ethers cleave at the alkyl–oxygen bond because a halide cannot attack an sp2 ring carbon by SN2 (backside attack is blocked by the ring) and an aryl cation is far too unstable for SN1. The phenol that is formed does not react further with HI because the Ar–OH bond is strengthened by conjugation. Diaryl ethers are not cleaved by HI at all. Boron tribromide cleaves aryl methyl ethers at low temperature through a related mechanism and is a standard laboratory reagent for this transformation. tert-Butyl ethers are also cleaved by trifluoroacetic acid at room temperature.

Key idea. Site selectivity: the halide ends up on the carbon that is more easily attacked by SN2 (methyl > primary > secondary) unless a tertiary, benzylic or allylic carbon is present, in which case the cation-forming SN1 path controls.
