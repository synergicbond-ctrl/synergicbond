3.2 Fractional distillation

When two miscible liquids have close boiling points (difference less than about 40 K) simple distillation does not separate them: each distillate fraction is still a mixture, enriched in the more volatile component. A fractionating column (packed with glass beads, rings or with plates) is fitted between the flask and the condenser. In the column the rising vapour condenses and re-vaporises many times; each cycle is one "theoretical plate" and enriches the vapour in the lower-boiling component. A column with many plates separates liquids with small boiling-point differences. Examples: acetone (329 K) from methanol (338 K); ethanol from methanol; the crude oil fractions (petrol, kerosene, diesel) are separated this way on a very large scale.

T–x diagrams (not to scale). Repeated vaporisation–condensation up the column enriches the more volatile liquid; an azeotrope cannot be separated beyond its composition by simple fractionation.
IMP In a T–x diagram the vapour curve lies above (richer in the more volatile component than) the liquid curve. The staircase between the two curves is the number of theoretical plates needed. For an azeotrope the two curves touch, so beyond that composition the fractionating column gives no further separation.
Concept Link Raoult's law (physical chemistry): for an ideal pair the vapour is richer in the more volatile component; an azeotrope corresponds to a positive or negative deviation large enough to produce an extremum in the vapour-pressure curve.
