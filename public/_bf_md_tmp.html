3.4 Physical state, allotropy, density and phase-change data

The B¹²-based allotrope structures are drawn once in Section 7.2; the physical-property consequences are summarised here.

Energetic basis of the inert-pair effect: in the classical bonding picture, formation of three bonds in M(III) requires electronic promotion/reorganisation, and the extra bond energy obtained must compensate for that cost. Down Group 13, M-X bond strengths generally decrease while the promotion cost remains substantial and is particularly large for Tl. Relativistic stabilisation of the Tl 6s² pair further favours retention of the ns² pair. Consequently the +1 oxidation state becomes progressively more stable down the group, with Tl(I) strongly preferred over Tl(III).

PART 4

4. Oxidation states, bonding and general characteristics of compounds

4.1 The +3 oxidation state and why many compounds are covalent

The valence configuration ns²np¹ suggests +3. Boron always forms covalent bonds; Al, Ga, In and Tl form many covalent anhydrous compounds but can produce hydrated ions in water.

  1. Fajans rule: a small, highly charged M³⁺ centre strongly polarises anions and favours covalency.

  2. The sum IE₁ + IE₂ + IE₃ is very large; removal of three electrons is expensive.

  3. Group 13 electronegativities are higher than those of s-block metals, so the electronegativity difference with many partners is insufficient for purely ionic bonding.

  4. Hydration can reverse the balance: anhydrous AlCl3 is molecular/dimeric in the vapour and in non-polar media, whereas its low-temperature crystal has octahedrally coordinated Al and appreciable ionic character; water provides enough hydration energy to yield [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ and Cl⁻.

4.2 The +1 state and inert-pair effect

The inert-pair effect describes the reduced participation of the outer ns² pair in bonding. If the energy required to promote or reorganise these electrons is not compensated by the additional bond energy obtained in the higher oxidation state, the ns² pair remains largely non-bonding.

The effective M–Cl bond stabilisation decreases approximately in the order Ga–Cl > In–Cl > Tl–Cl. This decrease in bond stabilisation contributes to the increasing stability of the +1 oxidation state down Group 13.

JEE Trap

JEE TRAP: The stability order of the +1 oxidation state is Al < Ga < In < Tl. Tl(I) is the predominant state, while Tl(III) is strongly oxidising. Compounds in +1 are generally more ionic than the corresponding +3 compounds.

Down the group, the ns² pair participates progressively less in bonding. Poor d/f shielding and relativistic stabilisation in the heaviest member hold the s pair tightly.

The phrase inert-pair effect describes the non-participation; the energetic cause is that the energy needed to unpair/promote the s electrons is not repaid by formation of weaker M-X bonds.

The same p-block pattern is illustrated by Sn(II)/Pb(II) compared with Sn(IV)/Pb(IV), and Sb(III)/Bi(III) compared with Sb(V)/Bi(V): the oxidation state two units below the group maximum becomes progressively more stable down a group.

Consequently p-block variable valency commonly changes by two because an ns² pair is used or used; in many d-block series adjacent oxidation states commonly differ by one because individual d electrons can be removed successively.

Both are broad patterns, not exception-free rules.

4.3 Electron deficiency, Lewis acidity and coordination

Key Point

Key Point: A trivalent Group 13 centre such as B in BF₃ has only six electrons around the central atom and is electron deficient. It can accept a lone pair and behaves as a Lewis acid:

BCl₃ + :NH₃ ⟶ BCl₃·NH₃

The geometry changes from planar around B to tetrahedral in the adduct.

MOT Lens

MOT LENS – BF₃: The empty B 2p orbital interacts with the symmetry-matched combination of filled F 2p lone-pair orbitals. This produces delocalised pi bonding over the BF₃ framework, shortening and strengthening B-F bonds. Because the occupied pi orbitals are strongly F-weighted, this is better viewed as ligand-to-boron pi donation rather than three localised B=F bonds.

JEE TRAP: Lewis acidity of BX₃ is not predicted by electronegativity alone. For many common hard donors, the trend is BF₃ < BCl₃ < BBr₃ < BI₃. F→B π donation is strongest in BF₃; the exact acidity also depends on donor identity, solvent and structural reorganisation.

Molecular polarity: BF₃ has zero net dipole moment because its trigonal-planar geometry is perfectly symmetrical; the three B-F bond dipoles cancel vectorially.

A neutral trihalide MX₃ has only six electrons around M after three sigma bonds. It can accept a lone pair and behaves as a Lewis acid. Boron normally has covalence 3 or 4; heavier members can achieve coordination numbers above four.

8.1 Boron trioxide, B₂O₃

B₂O₃ is a colourless/glassy acidic oxide. It contains trigonal BO₃ units joined into a network, dissolves many metal oxides to form characteristically coloured borate glasses, and is an essential glass/ceramic flux.

8.2 Borate structures and condensation

Simple borates are constructed from trigonal BO₃ units and/or tetrahedral BO₄ units. Corner sharing produces discrete anions, rings, chains, sheets and three-dimensional frameworks. In concentrated solutions polymeric borate species become increasingly important.

Structural summary: (1) monomeric BO₃ units can polymerise by sharing oxygen atoms; (2) some boron centres change from trigonal BO₃ to tetrahedral BO₄ on hydration or condensation; (3) protonation occurs preferentially at the more basic oxygen sites; (4) hydrated borates can condense by loss of H₂O with rearrangement of B–O bonds; and (5) H₃BO₃ is a common precursor to complex borate polyanions.

JEE Trap

JEE TRAP: Do not assign one hybridisation to every boron atom in a borate. In the tetraborate ion of borax, two boron centres are trigonal and two are tetrahedral.

8.3 Borax (Sodium Tetraborate Decahydrate)

8.3.1 Formula and structure

Note

• Conventional formula: Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O.

• More informative structural formula: Na₂[B₄O₅(OH)₄]·8H₂O.

• The borate ion present in crystalline borax is [B₄O₅(OH)₄]²⁻.

• It contains four boron atoms: two are trigonal (approximately BO₃, sp²) and two are tetrahedral (approximately BO₄, sp³); the units are joined through bridging oxygen atoms.

• The structural formula shows that four H atoms occur as –OH groups in the borate anion and eight H₂O molecules are present as water of crystallisation.

Key Point

Key Point: Do not treat borax as merely "Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O" when a structure-based question is asked. Remember: 2 trigonal B centres + 2 tetrahedral B centres in [B₄O₅(OH)₄]²⁻.

8.3.2 Solubility and alkaline nature

Borax is sparingly soluble in cold water but appreciably more soluble in hot water. Consequently, it can be purified by crystallisation from a concentrated hot solution.

In water, the structurally meaningful tetraborate species undergoes hydrolysis:

B₄O₅(OH)₄²⁻ + 5H₂O ⇌ 2B(OH)₃ + 2B(OH)₄⁻

Thus, borax solution contains boric acid and tetrahydroxyborate in equal stoichiometric amounts. This weak-acid/conjugate-base pair allows borax solution to act as a buffer.

The overall hydrolysis responsible for its alkaline nature is:

B₄O₅(OH)₄²⁻ + 5H₂O ⇌ 4B(OH)₃ + 2OH⁻
8.3.3 Preparation from orthoboric acid

Orthoboric acid is neutralised with a calculated amount of sodium carbonate. The hot solution is then concentrated and cooled to crystallise borax decahydrate.

4H₃BO₃ + Na₂CO₃ —heat→ Na₂B₄O₇ + CO₂↑ + 6H₂O

The resulting hot solution is concentrated and cooled to obtain borax decahydrate:

Na₂B₄O₇(aq) + 10H₂O —cool; crystallise→ Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O(s)

The complete crystallisation reaction may also be written as:

4H₃BO₃ + Na₂CO₃ + 4H₂O → Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O + CO₂↑
8.3.4 Industrial preparation from colemanite

Finely powdered colemanite is boiled with aqueous sodium carbonate. Calcium is precipitated as CaCO₃, while soluble sodium borates remain in the filtrate.

Ca₂B₆O₁₁·5H₂O(s) + 2Na₂CO₃(aq) —boil→ Na₂B₄O₇(aq) + 2NaBO₂(aq) + 2CaCO₃(s)↓ + 5H₂O

Note

• Boil powdered colemanite with Na₂CO₃ solution.

• Filter off insoluble CaCO₃.

• Concentrate the filtrate and allow Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O to crystallise.

• The mother liquor contains sodium metaborate. Passing CO₂ converts metaborate into additional tetraborate:

• 4NaBO₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → Na₂B₄O₇(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq)

• Concentrate and cool again to obtain an additional crop of borax crystals.

Thus, the industrial method is a sequence of reaction, filtration, crystallisation and recovery rather than a single crystallisation step.

8.3.5 Reaction with acids

Acidification of borax produces orthoboric acid:

Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O + 2HCl → 4H₃BO₃ + 2NaCl + 5H₂O

The corresponding ionic equation is:

B₄O₅(OH)₄²⁻ + 2H⁺ + 3H₂O → 4B(OH)₃
8.3.6 Borax as a primary standard in acidimetry

Pure borax decahydrate can be used as a primary standard for standardising strong acids. One mole of borax consumes two moles of a monoprotic strong acid.

Tetraborate hydrolysis produces two B(OH)₃ and two [B(OH)₄]⁻ units. Only the two tetrahydroxyborate ions consume hydronium ions:

B(OH)₄⁻ + H₃O⁺ → B(OH)₃ + 2H₂O

Hence:

1 mol borax ≡ 2 mol HCl

The titration reaction is:

Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O + 2HCl → 4H₃BO₃ + 2NaCl + 5H₂O

Molar mass of Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O ≈ 381.37 g mol−1; hence its acid-base equivalent mass is ≈ 190.69 g equiv−1.

Methyl orange or screened methyl orange is suitable. The ordinary methyl-orange transition range is approximately pH 3.1–4.4.

8.4 Orthoboric acid, H₃BO₃ or B(OH)₃

Key Point

Key Point: Orthoboric acid is a white crystalline solid with a soapy touch. It is sparingly soluble in cold water and much more soluble in hot water. It is a weak monobasic Lewis acid rather than a conventional proton donor.

B(OH)₃ + 2H₂O ⇌B(OH)4- + H₃O⁺

For dilute B(OH)₃ at 25 °C, pKₐ is about 9.25. At higher boron concentrations, polyborate equilibria also become important; a triborate equilibrium may show an apparent pK near 6.84.

10.1 Why boranes use multicentre bonds

MOT Lens

MOT LENS – 3c–2e bond: For one B-H-B bridge, combine a boron hybrid orbital from each B with the H 1s orbital. Three molecular orbitals result: bonding, approximately non-bonding and antibonding. Only two electrons are available, so they occupy the lowest bonding MO and are delocalised over all three atoms. The electron pair therefore binds the B-H-B unit without requiring two separate two-electron B-H bonds.

This electron accounting is the essence of electron-deficient bonding. Diborane has 12 valence electrons in total: four terminal B-H bonds use 8 electrons, leaving only 4 electrons for the two B-H-B bridges.

Cluster-name origins: closo means closed and denotes a complete cage; nido comes from Latin nidus (nest) and has one vertex missing from the corresponding closo parent; arachno comes from Greek arachne (spider web) and is still more open; conjuncto means joined together and describes fused/linked cluster units.

A normal two-centre two-electron bond for every B-H/B-B link would require more electrons than boron supplies. Boranes therefore combine terminal B-H 2c–2e bonds, B-B framework bonding and bridging B-H-B three-centre two-electron (3c–2e, 'banana') bonds. Wade's rules classify cluster skeletons by skeletal-electron-pair count.

10.3 Diborane structure and physical properties

Key Point

Key Point: The four terminal H atoms and both B atoms lie in one plane. The two bridging H atoms lie above and below that plane. Terminal B-H bonds are ordinary 2-centre–2-electron bonds; bridge B-H-B bonds are 3-centre–2-electron bonds, traditionally called banana bonds.

Representative structural values are: terminal B-H about 119 pm, bridge B-H about 134 pm, terminal H-B-H angle about 120 degrees and B-H-B bridge angle about 97 degrees.

JEE Trap

JEE TRAP: The bridge B-H bonds are longer than terminal B-H bonds because one electron pair is shared over three centres. Do not assign a normal single-bond order of 1 to each B-H segment in a bridge.

10.6 Borazine, B₃N₃H₆

MOT Lens

MOT LENS – borazine vs benzene: Six ring p orbitals combine into delocalised pi molecular orbitals, but the B and N atomic orbital energies are not equal. Occupied pi orbitals are polarised toward electronegative N, while low-lying acceptor character is greater at B. Consequently the B-N bonds have partial pi character but the ring is less electronically uniform and less aromatic than benzene; borazine is therefore more reactive toward addition/hydrolysis.

Because electron density is higher at nitrogen, protonation by HCl occurs preferentially at N rather than B. This polarity is a useful way to remember why 'inorganic benzene' is a structural analogy, not a claim of identical aromatic stability.

B₃N₃H₆ + 3HCl ⟶ B₃N₃H₉Cl₃
B₃N₃H₆ + 9H₂O slow hydrolysis on heating⟶ 3NH₃ + 3H₃BO₃ + 3H₂

JEE Trap

JEE TRAP: Borazine is planar and delocalised, but B-N bonds are polar. Do not transfer benzene's equal C-C electron distribution or low addition reactivity directly to borazine.

Borazine ('inorganic benzene') is a colourless liquid with a planar six-membered alternating B-N ring. It is isoelectronic with benzene, but B-N bonds are polar (B delta+, N delta-) and pi delocalisation is unequal, so it is much more reactive toward addition and hydrolysis.

Borazine is also known as borazole or triboranetriamine; the preferred formula-based name is borazine, B₃N₃H₆.

Borazine boils at about 53–56 °C and melts near −57 °C. It has an ammonia-like odour, reacts slowly with water and is soluble in common non-protic organic solvents.

Aromaticity cannot be assigned a single universal percentage. Borazine is less aromatic than benzene because the polar B–N bonds localise electron density. On strong heating, B–N ring coupling, dehydrogenation and polymerisation can occur, with products depending on temperature and conditions.

10.7 Boron nitride and boron carbide

Metal borides form a large family with many stoichiometries, such as M₂B, MB, MB₂ and MB₆. They are generally very hard, refractory and chemically resistant.

Boron carbide is one of the hardest refractory boron-rich solids and is used in abrasives, armour applications and neutron-absorbing components. It can be prepared by high-temperature reactions involving boron compounds and carbon.

4BCl₃ + 6H₂ + C high temperature⟶ B₄C + 12HCl

B₄C contains B₁₂-type icosahedral units linked through carbon-containing chains. This cluster-based structure is more appropriate than a simple diamond-like B–C network description.

Direct BN formation:

2B(s) + N₂(g) very high temperature⟶ 2BN(s)

From boron oxide/ammonia:

B₂O₃(s) + 2NH₃(g) 900-1200 °C⟶ 2BN(s) + 3H₂O(g)

From boron trichloride/ammonia:

BCl₃(g) + NH₃(g) high temperature⟶via adduct/aminolysis BN(s) + 3HCl(g)

Boron carbide:

2B₂O₃(s) + 7 C(s) electric furnace⟶about 2000-2500 °C B₄C(s) + 6CO(g)

B₄C coating on carbon fibre:

4BCl₃(g) + C(fibre) + 6H₂(g) chemical-vapour deposition⟶about 1700-1800 °C B₄C-coated fibre + 12HCl(g)

Hexagonal BN has alternating B and N atoms in planar six-membered rings. Representative data are B-N about 145 pm, interlayer separation about 330 pm and an average B-N bond order near 1.33; the unequal B/N electronegativities make the layers electronically different from graphite even though the topology is analogous.

Layer registry in common h-BN (AA-prime polytype): successive sheets are stacked so that boron and nitrogen sites are approximately eclipsed in an alternating B-over-N/N-over-B arrangement. AA-prime is the common conventional h-BN stacking, but other BN polytypes/stacking sequences such as AB/ABC and metastable AA are known; therefore this arrangement should not be presented as the only possible stacking. The about 330 pm interlayer separation and polar layer registry distinguish conventional h-BN electronically from graphite.

One B atom plus one N atom supplies the same total number of valence electrons as two carbon atoms. This isoelectronic relationship explains the close structural analogy between hexagonal BN and graphite. Hexagonal BN is a white, slippery solid despite this topological similarity.

Additional properties of h-BN: it is a wide-band-gap electrical insulator, chemically resistant, thermally conductive, has low water adsorption and can be optically transparent when sufficiently pure and thin.

Cubic BN is produced industrially by converting hexagonal BN under very high pressure and temperature, usually with catalysts. Its diamond-like network gives extreme hardness, but unlike diamond it retains useful chemical stability when machining ferrous alloys.

Chemical note: Superconductivity is not a standard property of ordinary BN; any such behaviour is restricted to specialised phases, defects or extreme conditions.
