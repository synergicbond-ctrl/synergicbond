18. Electrophilic addition to alkenes: the general picture

The π electrons of C=C are held less tightly than σ electrons and are accessible, so an alkene reacts with electrophiles (E+) to convert the weaker π bond (≈ 260 kJ) into two stronger σ bonds. The addition is exothermic (ΔH ≈ −120 to −130 kJ for H2, HX, X2).

  • Step 1 (slow, rate-determining): the π electrons attack E+; a σ bond forms to one carbon and the other carbon becomes electron-deficient, as a carbocation (or a bridged onium ion for Br+, Hg2+, etc.).
  • Step 2 (fast): a nucleophile (Nu−) attacks the cation (or opens the bridge).
Energy profile of electrophilic addition of HBr: the first TS (formation of the carbocation) is higher; the cation is an intermediate

Reactivity of alkenes towards electrophiles increases with the stability of the carbocation formed and with the electron density of C=C: (CH3)2C=CH2 > CH3CH=CH2 > CH2=CH2 > CH2=CH–Br > CH2=CH–COOH (EWG lowers the rate). Relative rates of acid-catalysed hydration: ethene 1, propene 1.6 × 106, 2-methylpropene 2.5 × 1011.

18.1 Markovnikov's rule: statement and reason

Original rule (1869): in the addition of H–X to an unsymmetrical alkene, the hydrogen adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens, and X goes to the carbon with fewer hydrogens. Modern, mechanism-based rule: the electrophile (H+) adds so as to give the more stable carbocation; the nucleophile then attacks that carbon. Stability of carbocations: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3+ (hyperconjugation and inductive effect of alkyl groups); allyl and benzyl cations are stabilised by resonance, and a carbocation next to O or N lone pair is stabilised by resonance (X–C+ ↔ X+=C).

18.2 HX: reactivity and conditions

Reactivity of HX: HI > HBr > HCl ≫ HF (acid strength; HF adds only in special conditions, e.g. HF/pyridine). Solvent: ether or acetic acid; no catalyst for HBr/HI, AlCl3 may be needed for HCl with poor alkenes. Water must be avoided if the alkyl halide is wanted (H2O competes).

Example 29 Foundation

Predict the products of HX addition

(a) 2-Methylbut-2-ene + HBr (b) 1-methylcyclohexene + HI (c) pent-1-ene + HCl (d) ethene + HCl, AlCl3.

Show solution
  1. (a) H+ adds to C-3 (the CH carbon) to give the tertiary cation (CH3)2C+–CH2CH3: 2-bromo-2-methylbutane.
  2. (b) H+ adds to ring carbon 2 (the CH carbon), giving the tertiary cation at C-1: 1-iodo-1-methylcyclohexane.
  3. (c) Secondary cation at C-2: 2-chloropentane (racemic, new stereocentre).
  4. (d) Ethene is the least reactive alkene (it would need a primary cation): chloroethane, slowly and only with a Lewis acid.

18.3 Carbocation rearrangements in HX addition

A carbocation intermediate can rearrange (1,2-H shift, 1,2-alkyl shift, ring expansion) to a more stable cation if one is accessible. Products from the unrearranged and rearranged cations both form; the rearranged product normally dominates when it comes from a much more stable cation.

HCl⟶
3-Methylbut-1-ene → 2° cation → 1,2-hydride shift → 3° cation → 2-chloro-2-methylbutane (major; 2-chloro-3-methylbutane is minor)
HCl⟶
3,3-Dimethylbut-1-ene → 2° cation → 1,2-methyl shift → 3° cation → 2-chloro-2,3-dimethylbutane (major, about 80 %)
HBr⟶
Vinylcyclohexane + HBr: hydride shift gives 1-bromo-1-methylcyclohexane (tertiary)
HCl⟶
Vinylcyclobutane + HCl: ring expansion (strain relief) gives the 1-methylcyclopentyl cation and 1-chloro-1-methylcyclopentane
Example 30 Advanced

Rearrangement vs no rearrangement

Which of the following give rearranged products on addition of HCl? (A) 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene (B) but-1-ene (C) 3-methylbut-1-ene (D) 2-methylpropene

Show solution
  1. A: yes. The secondary cation sits next to a quaternary carbon; a methyl shift gives a tertiary cation.
  2. B: the secondary cation could only shift to another secondary cation, so there is no driving force: no rearrangement (2-chlorobutane).
  3. C: yes. The secondary cation next to a tertiary C–H undergoes a hydride shift to a tertiary cation.
  4. D: the first-formed cation is already tertiary: no rearrangement.
  5. Answer: A and C.

18.4 Addition of sulfuric acid, water (hydration) and HOX

  • H2SO4 (cold, conc.): alkene → alkyl hydrogen sulphate (Markovnikov): CH3CH=CH2 + H2SO4 → CH3CH(OSO3H)CH3; on warming with water → 2-propanol. This is the industrial hydration of propene to isopropyl alcohol. It is also the reason conc. H2SO4 dissolves alkenes but not alkanes.
  • Acid-catalysed hydration: alkene + H2O, dil. H2SO4 or H3PO4 (or dilute acid, 60–200 °C): Markovnikov alcohol; carbocation → rearrangement possible, reversible (the reverse of dehydration). Ethene → ethanol (H3PO4/SiO2, 300 °C, 60 atm, industrial).
