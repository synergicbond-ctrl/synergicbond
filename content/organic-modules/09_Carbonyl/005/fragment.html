3. Physical properties

3.1 Boiling points Aldehydes and ketones cannot hydrogen-bond to each other (there is no O–H or N–H). Their molecules associate only by dipole–dipole forces. Their boiling points are therefore higher than those of hydrocarbons and ethers of comparable molar mass but lower than those of alcohols and carboxylic acids.

Key idea Order of boiling points at about equal molar mass: alkane < ether < aldehyde ≈ ketone < alcohol < carboxylic acid. Among isomeric carbonyls, a ketone boils slightly above the aldehyde (propanone 56 °C, propanal 49 °C) because the two alkyl groups stabilise the dipole and polarise the C=O more; this is a small effect and not worth applying to long chains.
Boiling points rise with chain length by about 20–25 °C per CH2 at low carbon number (methanal −19, ethanal 20, propanal 49, butanal 75, pentanal 103 °C) and fall with branching (2-methylpropanal 64 °C, compared with butanal 75 °C).
3.2 Solubility
The carbonyl oxygen is a hydrogen-bond acceptor. Formaldehyde, ethanal and propanone are miscible with water in all proportions. From four carbons upward solubility drops quickly as the hydrophobic chain grows (butanal is soluble to about 7 g per 100 g water; hexanal to about 0.6 g). Aromatic aldehydes and ketones (benzaldehyde, acetophenone) are only sparingly soluble because the phenyl group is large and hydrophobic. All carbonyls dissolve freely in organic solvents.
Trap "Aldehydes and ketones form hydrogen bonds with water" is correct only in the sense of the C=O oxygen accepting H-bonds. They cannot be H-bond donors (no O–H), so they do not self-associate through hydrogen bonds. This is the reason for the boiling-point order above.
3.3 Smell, state, useful facts
  • Methanal (formaldehyde) is a gas (b.p. −19 °C) with a pungent smell. Its 37–40 % aqueous solution containing 10–15 % methanol is formalin, used as a preservative. In water, methanal exists mainly as the hydrate CH2(OH)2 (methanediol).
  • Ethanal boils at 20 °C: nearly a gas at room temperature.
  • Lower aldehydes have sharp, irritating odours; higher aldehydes and ketones (and aromatic ones) are fragrant and appear in perfumes (benzaldehyde: bitter almond; cinnamaldehyde: cinnamon; vanillin: vanilla; camphor; muscone).
  • Propanone (acetone) is a common solvent and the simplest ketone; it is miscible with water.
  • Polymers of aldehydes: methanal polymerises to paraformaldehyde (HO–(CH2O)n–H) and trimerises to trioxane; ethanal trimerises with a trace of acid to paraldehyde (a cyclic trimer, b.p. 124 °C) and tetramerises at low temperature to metaldehyde (a cyclic tetramer). Paraldehyde and metaldehyde are depolymerised by heating with acid.

Ring strain raises the C=O stretching frequency in small ring ketones: cyclohexanone 1715, cyclopentanone about 1745, cyclobutanone about 1780 cm-1. Conjugation lowers it by 20–40 cm-1 because the C=O bond acquires some single-bond character (O−–C=C+ resonance).

Shortcut A quick structure clue: "IR peak near 1700 and a one-proton singlet at δ 9.7" says aldehyde; "IR 1715, no peak at δ 9–10, a three-proton singlet near δ 2.1" says methyl ketone. NMR of the –CHO proton is far downfield because of the strong deshielding of the carbonyl and the anisotropy of the π bond.
