5. Addition (chain-growth) polymerisation: the general picture

Every chain-growth polymerisation has the same three acts, whatever the active centre: initiation (an active centre is created and adds the first monomer), propagation (the active centre adds monomer after monomer, regenerating itself at the new chain end, thousands of times in a second), and termination (the centre is destroyed or transferred). The active centre may be a free radical, a carbocation, a carbanion or a carbon–metal bond; the choice is fixed by the substituent X on CH2=CHX.

IMP Matching monomer to mechanism: X that stabilises a radical (aryl, vinyl, –CN, –COOR, –Cl) → free-radical. X that donates electrons and stabilises a cation (alkyl, –OR, aryl) → cationic. X that withdraws electrons and stabilises a carbanion (–CN, –COOR, –NO2, aryl, vinyl) → anionic. Non-polar α-olefins (ethene, propene) with no stabilising group → coordination (Ziegler–Natta, metallocene) or high-pressure radical (ethene only).
Validity Check The monomer–mechanism pairing is a strong guide, not an absolute law. Styrene polymerises by radical, cationic and anionic routes because its phenyl ring stabilises all three active centres. Ethene, which has no stabilising group, is polymerised by a radical route only under severe conditions (1000–2000 atm), and ordinarily by coordination catalysts.

Initiation (second half): the radical adds to the CH2 end of the alkene, because that gives the more stable radical (benzylic for styrene), and steric hindrance is smaller at CH2.

The new radical is benzylic, hence stabilised by resonance with the ring.

Propagation: the chain-end radical repeats this addition. Each step turns one π bond (≈ 270 kJ mol-1 in C=C) into a σ bond (≈ 350 kJ mol-1), so each step releases about 80–90 kJ mol-1 (ΔH ≈ −90 kJ mol-1 for ethene; ΔS is negative because free monomers become one chain; so ΔG = ΔH − TΔS becomes zero at a ceiling temperature, above which no polymer forms).

+ propagation, many times⟶
Head-to-tail addition: substituted carbon is always the radical site, so Ph groups end up on alternate carbons (1,3-pattern).
IMP Head-to-tail (1,3) enchainment dominates: –CH2–CHX–CH2–CHX–. Head-to-head (–CHX–CHX–) is rare because it forces the radical to be attacked at the more hindered carbon, giving a less stable (primary) radical. Tail-to-tail meeting occurs only at termination (coupling).

Termination has two pathways:

+ combination (coupling)⟶
Two chain radicals couple: one longer chain (twice the average length) with a head-to-head link at the centre. Polystyrene terminates mostly by coupling.
+ disproportionation⟶ +
One radical abstracts a β-hydrogen from the other: two chains result, one saturated end and one unsaturated end. Important for PMMA at higher temperature, where coupling is hindered.

Chain transfer. A growing radical may abstract an atom (H or Cl) from a monomer, solvent, initiator or already-formed polymer, killing that chain but creating a new radical elsewhere.

  • Transfer to solvent or deliberately added thiol (R–SH) or CCl4 controls M (lower molar mass); thiols are called "chain-transfer agents" or modifiers (used to make Buna-S processable).
  • Transfer to polymer by intermolecular H-abstraction makes a radical in the middle of a chain; a monomer then adds to it, producing a long-chain branch. LDPE is branched for this reason (and also because of back-biting below).
  • Back-biting (intramolecular, 1,5-H shift) in ethene polymerisation: the chain-end radical abstracts a H from the carbon four atoms along the chain via a six-membered transition state, giving a mid-chain secondary radical; it then grows a butyl (C4) short-chain branch. This is why LDPE has many short branches and low crystallinity.
1,5-H shift (back-biting)⟶
Model on a heptyl chain: the H on C-5 moves to the radical carbon C-1 through a six-membered transition state; the primary end radical becomes a more stable secondary radical inside the chain.
Exam Trap Back-biting is a 1,5-H shift (six-membered TS counting the H), not a 1,4- or 1,6-shift. The mid-chain radical on C-5 then adds ethene, and the four carbons C-1 to C-4 left behind form a butyl branch. Do not call the branches "methyl" or "ethyl" in LDPE; ethyl and butyl branches dominate.

Inhibitors and retarders. Hydroquinone, benzoquinone, BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene), oxygen and nitroso compounds react with radicals to give stable non-propagating radicals. This is why monomers are shipped with 10–100 ppm inhibitor and why polymerisation reactors are purged free of air (except the O2-initiated LDPE route).

Example 3 Adv

Effect of initiator concentration

Quadrupling the initiator concentration in a free-radical polymerisation (all else equal): by what factor do the rate and the average chain length change?

Show solution
  1. Rp ∝ [I]1/2, so the rate rises by √4 = 2.
  2. ν ∝ Rp/Ri ∝ [I]1/2/[I] = [I]−1/2, so chain length falls by 1/√4 = ½.
  3. Conclusion: more initiator → faster reaction but shorter chains. The two goals (fast and high M) pull in opposite directions in radical polymerisation; in step-growth polymerisation, in contrast, the rate and the final M are linked through conversion alone.
Experimental Connection Commercial monomers such as styrene and acrylates are shipped with a few tens of ppm of an inhibitor (a hydroquinone-type compound) and kept cool, because radicals from traces of peroxide or light would otherwise start polymerisation in the drum. The inhibitor is removed or overwhelmed by extra initiator before use.

5.2 Why polymerisation is thermodynamically allowed

ΔH is about −80 to −90 kJ mol-1 per monomer for vinyl monomers, ΔS about −100 to −120 J K-1 mol-1. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS < 0 below the ceiling temperature Tc = ΔH/ΔS (for α-methylstyrene Tc is only about 61 °C, because the crowded quaternary centre weakens the new σ bond; for ethene it is far above any working temperature).

Example 4 Adv

Ceiling temperature

For a monomer ΔHp = −35 kJ mol-1 and ΔSp = −104 J K-1 mol-1 (standard-state values). Estimate Tc.

Show solution
  1. At Tc: ΔG = 0, so Tc = ΔH/ΔS = (−35 000)/(−104) = 337 K (≈ 64 °C).
  2. Above 337 K the polymer depolymerises back to monomer. The numbers are close to the real figures for α-methylstyrene.

5.3 Cationic polymerisation

Active centre: carbocation. Monomers with electron-donating groups that stabilise a cation: isobutene (CH2=C(CH3)2), vinyl ethers, α-methylstyrene, styrene (benzylic). Initiators: strong protic acids with non-nucleophilic anions (HClO4, CF3SO3H) or Lewis acids with a proton source (BF3 + H2O, AlCl3 + HCl, SnCl4 + H2O, TiCl4), always at low temperature (−80 to −100 °C) in a non-nucleophilic medium (CH2Cl2, CH3Cl).

+ initiation: H⁺ from BF₃·H₂O⟶
Protonation of the CH₂ end gives the tertiary cation (Markovnikov).
+ propagation⟶ propagation⟶
Each addition keeps the cation tertiary. (Last structure schematic.)

Termination: loss of H+ from the chain end to the counter-anion (re-forming the initiator complex, and leaving a terminal C=C), chain transfer to monomer, or combination with the counter-anion (e.g. Cl-). Cationic chains do not terminate by coupling (like charges repel).

IMP Butyl rubber = isobutene (≈ 98 %) + a little isoprene (≈ 2 %), cationic copolymerisation with AlCl3 in CH3Cl at −100 °C. The isoprene units put a few C=C into the chain so that sulphur vulcanisation is possible; the dense methyl groups pack so tightly that gas permeability is very low, hence inner tubes and tubeless-tyre liners.

5.4 Anionic polymerisation and living polymers

Active centre: carbanion. Monomers with electron-withdrawing groups: acrylonitrile, methyl methacrylate, methyl acrylate, nitroethene, styrene and 1,3-butadiene (aryl or vinyl stabilises the negative charge by delocalisation). Initiators: n-BuLi, Grignard reagents, NaNH2 (strong nucleophiles); sodium naphthalenide (electron transfer, gives dianion that grows from both ends).

+ initiation⟶
n-Butyl anion adds to the CH₂ end, putting the negative charge on the benzylic carbon.

Cyanoacrylate: both –CN and –COOEt stabilise the carbanion so well that even the weak nucleophile OH- (from adsorbed water) starts polymerisation. That is why superglue "sets" on skin and on damp surfaces and is stored dry.

Living polymerisation. With rigorously purified monomer and solvent, nothing terminates the carbanion: it cannot couple (same charge), cannot lose H−, and does not transfer to non-protic solvents. The chain ends stay "alive". Consequences that JEE and board exams use:

  • All chains start at the same time and grow at the same rate → narrow molar-mass distribution (Poisson): PDI = 1 + 1/Xn ≈ 1.01–1.1.
  • Xn = (moles of monomer consumed)/(moles of initiator molecules with one growing chain each); for a dianionic initiator (two chains per initiator), Xn = 2 × (moles monomer)/(moles initiator).
  • Adding a second monomer after the first is used up continues growth → block copolymers (SBS: polystyrene–polybutadiene–polystyrene thermoplastic elastomer).
  • Quenching with CO2 gives chains ending in –COOH; with ethylene oxide gives –OH ends (telechelic polymers).
  • Traces of H2O, CO2, O2, alcohols kill the chain ends instantly (protonation) → these polymerisations need dry, oxygen-free apparatus.
Example 5 Adv

Mass from a living polymerisation

In an anionic polymerisation, 2.0 × 10−3 mol of n-BuLi is used with 208 g of styrene (all converted). Find Mn, Xn and the expected PDI order.

Show solution
  1. Moles styrene = 208/104 = 2.00 mol. Chains = 2.0 × 10−3 (one chain per n-BuLi).
  2. Xn = 2.00/2.0 × 10−3 = 1000.
  3. Mn = 1000 × 104 = 1.04 × 105 g mol-1 (plus the 57 g mol-1 butyl end, negligible).
  4. PDI = 1 + 1/Xn = 1.001; in practice 1.02–1.10 owing to mixing; a living system gives PDI close to 1.
Why It Happens Why a living carbanion does not terminate. A growing carbanion cannot couple with another (like charges repel), cannot easily expel H− (a very poor leaving group), and in a dry aprotic solvent has no proton source. With nothing to terminate, the chain stays reactive until monomer or a quencher is added; that is exactly what “living” means.

5.5 Coordination polymerisation: Ziegler–Natta and after

In 1953 Karl Ziegler found that TiCl4 with Al(C2H5)3 polymerises ethene at 6–7 atm and 333–343 K to linear polythene; Giulio Natta used the same catalyst to make isotactic polypropene. Both shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The active catalyst is a surface of TiCl3 (reduced by the alkylaluminium) with a Ti–C bond and a vacant coordination site.

IMP Cossee–Arlman mechanism (the accepted picture): (1) monomer binds side-on (π-coordination) to the vacant octahedral site on Ti; (2) the alkyl/polymer chain on Ti migrates to the nearer alkene carbon (4-centre TS, migratory insertion) forming a new C–C bond; (3) the chain is now one unit longer and the vacancy has switched to the position the chain just left; the cycle repeats. The chain end is a polymeryl–Ti σ bond and never a free ion or radical, so the stereochemistry is controlled by the chiral surface.

Why only isotactic polypropene from Ziegler–Natta? The monomer approaches the Ti centre with its methyl group away from the polymer chain and with the same face each time (steric control by the chiral catalyst surface), giving all-same-side CH3.

Later catalyst families

  • Phillips catalyst: CrO3 on silica; HDPE at about 100–150 °C.
  • Metallocene/MAO (Kaminsky, 1980): Cp2ZrCl2 + methylaluminoxane; single-site, so very narrow distribution (PDI ≈ 2) and exact control of comonomer/tacticity; gives LLDPE and syndiotactic polystyrene.
