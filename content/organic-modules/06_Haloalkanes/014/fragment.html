13. The E2 reaction

13.1 Mechanism and kinetics

One concerted step: the base abstracts H+ from the β-carbon as the C–X bond breaks and the π-bond forms. All bond-making and bond-breaking events occur in the same transition state, so the rate depends on both reactants.

Formula rate = k [R–X][B−] (second order overall)
  • Kinetic isotope effect. Because the β C–H bond is broken in the rate-determining step, replacing H by D slows the reaction: kH/kD ≈ 3–8 (e.g. C6H5CH2CH2Br with EtO−: ≈ 7). In E1 there is no primary isotope effect (kH/kD ≈ 1, the C–H breaks after the slow step), a useful diagnostic. The same experiment on E1cB-type systems gives different numbers (see section 15).
  • Reactivity of the halide (in E2): 3° > 2° > 1°: the reverse of SN2, because the alkene being formed is more stable the more substituted it is and this stability is partly felt in the transition state. Also R–I > R–Br > R–Cl ≫ R–F in rate (C–X breaking is involved).
  • Base: must be strong. The usual bases: HO− (alcoholic KOH), RO−/ROH (NaOEt/EtOH, t-BuOK/t-BuOH), NH2−, LDA, and non-nucleophilic amidine bases DBU and DBN. Weak bases (H2O, ROH, I−, CN− at ordinary basicity) are not E2 bases.
  • Solvent and temperature: high temperature favours elimination over substitution (ΔS > 0: 1 + 1 molecules → 3 molecules; ΔG = ΔH − TΔS gets more negative as T rises). Less polar solvents (alcohol, not water) increase base strength for E2: "alcoholic KOH" eliminates, "aqueous KOH" substitutes.
Trap Aqueous KOH → substitution (alcohol). Alcoholic KOH → elimination (alkene). In water, HO− is heavily solvated (less basic, still a good nucleophile) and water is polar; in ethanol KOH partly exists as ethoxide, a stronger base. For primary halides, even alcoholic KOH gives mainly substitution product except with heat/hindered base; for 2° and 3° the elimination is major.

13.2 Stereochemistry of E2: the anti-periplanar requirement

The orbital of the C–H bond being broken and the σ* orbital of the C–X bond must overlap, as in the formation of the π-bond. The best overlap is when H–C–C–X lie in one plane in the anti-periplanar arrangement (dihedral 180°); the syn-periplanar arrangement (0°, eclipsed) is the other geometry with overlap but is higher in energy (eclipsing, and base and LG on the same side) and is seen only when anti is impossible (rigid ring systems; some 2° ammonium, ion-pair-assisted cases).

Newman projection down C2–C3 in an anti-periplanar conformation: Br (front, top) is anti to H (back, bottom). This H is lost.
Key idea E2 is stereospecific: the relative configuration of the two stereocentres in the substrate decides the geometry (Z or E) of the alkene formed when the β-carbon has only one H. The reactant is rotated into the anti-periplanar conformation, and the groups that end up on the same side become cis.
Example 26 Adv

Stereospecific E2

(2S,3S)- and (2S,3R)-2-bromo-3-methylpentane are treated separately with sodium ethoxide in ethanol. Predict the stereochemistry of the 3-methylpent-2-ene formed in each case (the H on C3 is anti to Br).

Show solution
  1. C3 carries only one hydrogen, so the anti-periplanar H–C3–C2–Br arrangement is unique for each diastereomer. Rotate about the C2–C3 bond until H(C3) is anti to Br(C2). The remaining groups on C2 (CH3, H) and on C3 (CH3, C2H5) then fall on one side or the other.
  2. Computed geometry (3-D conformer analysis): in the (2S,3R) (and enantiomer (2R,3S)) isomer the C2 methyl and the C3 ethyl are gauche (60°) in the reactive conformer, so they become cis: (Z)-3-methylpent-2-ene.
  3. In the (2S,3S) (and (2R,3R)) isomer they are anti (180°), so they become trans: (E)-3-methylpent-2-ene.
  4. (CIP: at C3 the ethyl group outranks methyl; at C2 methyl outranks H; cis(CH3, Et) = Z.)

(2S,3R)-2-bromo-3-methylpentane gives the Z-alkene (CH3 on C2 and C2H5 on C3 on the same side).

(2S,3S)-2-bromo-3-methylpentane gives the E-alkene.
Shortcut When the β-carbon has two hydrogens (e.g. 2-bromobutane losing H from the CH2), the rotor can place either of them anti to Br, with two different conformers that lead to the Z and the E alkene: the one with the smaller steric strain (trans, E) predominates. This is why 2-bromobutane gives mostly trans-but-2-ene.

13.3 Regiochemistry: Zaitsev (Saytzeff) and Hofmann orientation

  • Zaitsev rule: the major alkene is the more substituted (more stable) one. It is the product when a small base (HO−, EtO−) removes the proton from the more substituted β-carbon (the one with fewer H in the substrate). Stability: tetrasubstituted > trisubstituted > disubstituted (trans > cis) > monosubstituted, through hyperconjugation, and conjugation if available.
  • Hofmann rule: the major alkene is the less substituted one. Occurs with (i) a bulky base (t-BuOK, LDA, t-BuOK in t-BuOH, triethylcarbinol), whose approach to the hindered interior H is blocked; (ii) poor leaving groups with partial carbanion character in the TS (–F, –N+R3, –S+R2): the TS resembles a carbanion, so the most acidic (least substituted, with the least electron-donating alkyls) β-H is lost; (iii) steric crowding in the substrate (a highly branched alkyl group).

13.4 E2 in cyclohexane systems

Cyclohexane rings can only accommodate anti-periplanar H–C–C–X when both H and X are axial on adjacent carbons (trans-diaxial), because equatorial bonds are gauche, not anti, to the ring bonds. Consequences:

  • Elimination rate is determined by the proportion of the ring-flipped conformer in which X is axial.
  • Only an axial H on an adjacent carbon, trans to the axial X, can be lost. If the β-carbon's H is equatorial (because of a bulky substituent forced axial/equatorial by ring flip), that H cannot be removed; the less substituted alkene forms.
NaOEt⟶
1-Bromo-4-tert-butylcyclohexane: the tert-butyl group "locks" the ring with t-Bu equatorial. cis isomer (Br axial) eliminates fast; trans isomer (Br equatorial) very slowly, since the ring must flip to put t-Bu axial.
Example 27 Adv

Locked ring

The cis- and trans-isomers of 1-bromo-4-tert-butylcyclohexane are treated with sodium ethoxide. Which reacts faster and why?

Show solution
  1. The bulky t-Bu group (A-value ≈ 4.9 kcal mol−1) stays equatorial. In the cis isomer, the Br is then axial: it already has two axial β-H anti to it. E2 occurs readily (in practice 102–103 times faster).
  2. In the trans isomer, with t-Bu equatorial, Br is equatorial and no β-H is anti-periplanar. To eliminate, the ring has to flip to the conformer with both t-Bu and Br axial (very unstable, ≈ 5 kcal/mol higher), so the reaction is very slow (and substitution or other pathways compete).
  3. cis reacts faster.
Example 28 Adv

Menthyl and neomenthyl chlorides

Menthyl chloride (1R,2S,5R-type; all three substituents equatorial in the stable chair) and neomenthyl chloride (Cl epimer; Cl axial when the isopropyl and methyl are equatorial) are treated with NaOEt in EtOH. Which is faster, and what are the alkenes?

Show solution
  1. Neomenthyl chloride: Cl is already axial in the stable chair. Two β-carbons each have an axial H trans to Cl: the ring CH2 (C-2) and the C–H bearing the isopropyl group (C-4, whose axial H is available because isopropyl is equatorial). Loss of the more substituted-site H gives the Zaitsev alkene: 3-menthene (≈ 78 %) + 2-menthene (≈ 22 %). Reaction is fast.
  2. Menthyl chloride: Cl equatorial in the stable chair. For E2 the ring must flip so that Cl, isopropyl and methyl are all axial. In that conformer the isopropyl-bearing carbon has its H equatorial, so it cannot be lost: only the axial H of the C-2 CH2 is anti. The only product is 2-menthene (the less substituted, Hofmann-type alkene), and the reaction is about 200 times slower.

Example 29 Adv

Regiochemistry in a ring

Predict the major elimination product from cis- and from trans-1-bromo-2-methylcyclohexane with NaOEt/EtOH.

Show solution
  1. E2 needs Br axial and an axial anti H.
  2. trans isomer: in the conformer with Br axial, the 2-methyl group is also axial (trans-diaxial); the C-2 H is therefore equatorial and cannot be removed. Only the C-6 axial H is anti: product 3-methylcyclohexene (the less substituted, "Hofmann-like" because of geometry, not of base).
  3. cis isomer: Br axial, CH3 equatorial; C-2 H is axial and anti to Br. Zaitsev: 1-methylcyclohexene (major), with some 3-methylcyclohexene from the C-6 H.
NaOEt⟶
The trans isomer gives 3-methylcyclohexene: only the C-6 hydrogen can be anti to the axial Br.
Key idea For any cyclohexyl halide: draw both chairs, put X axial, then ask which adjacent carbons carry an axial H trans to X. The alkene forms from those H atoms only. If none is available, the substrate does not undergo E2 (it may undergo E1/SN).
γ-BHC (lindane) and the β-isomer. Hexachlorocyclohexane (C6H6Cl6) exists as several stereoisomers (α, β, γ, δ …). The β-isomer has all six Cl equatorial: no Cl has an anti-periplanar H, so it resists dehydrochlorination by base (and is persistent), whereas isomers with axial Cl lose HCl readily to give trichlorobenzenes.

13.5 Effect of the leaving group on orientation

Poor leaving groups shift orientation toward Hofmann: for 2-pentyl-X with NaOEt/EtOH the percentage of pent-1-ene: X = I ≈ 30, Br ≈ 31, Cl ≈ 33, F ≈ 70+, SMe2+ ≈ 87, NMe3+ ≈ 98. (Values illustrative of the trend from classical studies.)

13.6 Elimination from dihalides: alkynes

  • Vicinal dihalide (X on adjacent carbons) with 2 equivalents of a strong base (alcoholic KOH at high temperature, or NaNH2) → alkyne (two successive E2 steps).
  • Geminal dihalide (both X on one carbon) → alkyne with the same reagents.
  • With NaNH2, a terminal alkyne product is deprotonated to the acetylide (needs 3 equivalents of base overall); water then regenerates the terminal alkyne. With alcoholic KOH, a terminal alkyne can isomerise to an internal one at high temperature, and NaNH2 converts internal alkynes to terminal acetylides.
3 NaNH2, then H2O⟶
1,2-Dibromopropane to propyne. Third equivalent of NaNH2 ends up as the sodium salt of propyne, protonated in the work-up.
2 NaNH2, liq. NH3⟶
Ethylidene chloride (gem-dichloride) to acetylene via vinyl chloride (the second elimination is harder than the first).
Trap The second E2 (vinyl halide → alkyne) is much slower than the first and needs the stronger base NaNH2 or high temperature: alcoholic KOH at room temperature stops at the vinyl halide.
