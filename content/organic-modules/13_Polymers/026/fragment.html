21A. Conducting polymers: conjugation as a molecular-orbital picture

Polyacetylene, –[CH=CH]–n, is the simplest conjugated polymer; made from ethyne with a Ziegler–Natta catalyst (Shirakawa), it can be obtained as cis or trans films. Its chain carries one p orbital per carbon, and these overlap sideways into a long π system.

Alternating single and double bonds; each carbon is sp² with one p orbital in the π system.
  • MO picture. N p orbitals combine into N π molecular orbitals; the lowest N/2 are filled. As N grows the orbitals crowd together into bands, and the HOMO–LUMO gap shrinks (butadiene ≈ large gap, hexatriene smaller ...). This is the same reason that conjugated dyes absorb at longer wavelength as the chain lengthens.
  • Why pure trans-polyacetylene is only a semiconductor. Bond alternation (long single, short double) opens a gap (Peierls distortion) at the Fermi level.
  • Doping. Oxidising with I2 or AsF5 (p-doping) removes electrons from the filled π band, or reducing with sodium (n-doping) adds electrons to the empty band. The resulting charge carriers (polarons/solitons) move along the chain and conductivity rises by several orders of magnitude, up to metallic values. Heeger, MacDiarmid and Shirakawa shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this discovery.
  • Other conducting polymers: polyaniline, polypyrrole, polythiophene, PEDOT:PSS (touch screens, OLEDs, antistatic coatings).
IMP Saturated polymers such as polyethene are insulators because every electron sits in a localised σ bond: the HOMO–LUMO (σ–σ*) gap is huge. Conduction in a polymer needs an extended, partially filled π system.
