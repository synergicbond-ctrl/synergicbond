30.1 Alcohols

Practice 68 L2 ALC

Arrange in decreasing rate of reaction with HBr: 3-methylbutan-2-ol, 2-methylbutan-2-ol, pentan-1-ol, benzyl alcohol. Mention the mechanism type for each.

Show answer
  1. HBr reacts by SN1 where a stable cation can form and by SN2 for primary alcohols.
  2. Benzyl (resonance-stabilised cation) and 2-methylbutan-2-ol (3° cation) are fastest and similar; 3-methylbutan-2-ol (2° cation, then hydride shift) is slower; pentan-1-ol (SN2 on the protonated alcohol) is slowest.
  3. Benzyl alcohol ≈ 2-methylbutan-2-ol > 3-methylbutan-2-ol > pentan-1-ol. The 3-methylbutan-2-ol product is 2-bromo-2-methylbutane (hydride shift).
Practice 69 L2 ALC

Predict the product of 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol with conc. HCl and give the mechanism steps.

Show answer
  1. Protonation of OH and loss of water give the 2° cation CH3–CH+–C(CH3)3.
  2. A methyl group migrates (1,2-shift) to give the 3° cation (CH3)2C+–CH(CH3)2.
  3. Cl− captures it: 2-chloro-2,3-dimethylbutane.
Practice 70 L2 ALC

Neopentyl alcohol is heated with HBr. Name the product and explain why 1-bromo-2,2-dimethylpropane is not formed.

Show answer
  1. The β-carbon is quaternary, so backside attack (SN2) at CH2 is blocked; a free primary cation is too unstable. As water leaves, a methyl group migrates simultaneously to form the 3° cation (CH3)2C+CH2CH3.
  2. Product: 2-bromo-2-methylbutane.
Practice 71 L2 ALC

(R)-Butan-2-ol is treated separately with (i) SOCl2 alone, (ii) SOCl2/pyridine, (iii) conc. HCl/ZnCl2. Give the stereochemical outcome in each case.

Show answer
  1. (i) SNi: retention, (R)-2-chlorobutane. (ii) Free Cl− backside attack on the chlorosulphite: inversion, (S)-2-chlorobutane. (iii) SN1: planar cation, mainly racemic 2-chlorobutane.
Practice 72 L2 ALC

Write the products of (i) (S)-butan-2-ol + TsCl/pyridine; (ii) the product of (i) + NaBr. Give the net stereochemical change.

Show answer
  1. (i) The tosylate with retention: (S)-butan-2-yl tosylate. (ii) SN2 by Br− with inversion: (R)-2-bromobutane.
  2. Net: inversion of configuration (S → R) in two steps; the C–O bond of the alcohol is not broken in the first step.
Practice 73 L2 ALC

Give the major dehydration product and name the key steps: 2-methylcyclohexanol with H2SO4, heat.

Show answer
  1. E1: protonation, loss of water gives the 2° cation at C-1; loss of H from C-2 (tertiary C–H) gives the more substituted alkene.
  2. Major: 1-methylcyclohexene (Zaitsev); minor: 3-methylcyclohexene.
Practice 74 L2 ALC

Cyclohexylmethanol is dehydrated with H3PO4. Predict the product and say why a 1° cation is not involved.

Show answer
  1. Loss of water with simultaneous 1,2-hydride shift from the ring C–H gives the tertiary 1-methylcyclohexyl cation; loss of H+ gives 1-methylcyclohexene.
  2. The shift is concerted with the departure of water so that a primary cation never forms.
Practice 75 L2 ALC

Which of the following give the same alkene on dehydration: butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol?

Show answer
  1. Butan-1-ol → but-1-ene (some but-2-ene by hydride shift); butan-2-ol → but-2-ene (major); 2-methylpropan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol → 2-methylpropene (the 1° alcohol via hydride shift to the 3° cation).
  2. Pair giving the same product: 2-methylpropan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol.
Practice 76 L2 ALC

POCl3/pyridine converts cis- and trans-2-methylcyclohexanol into different alkenes. Identify and explain.

Show answer
  1. E2 needs an anti-periplanar H. In the cis isomer the conformer with OH axial has an axial H on C-2 (anti to OH): 1-methylcyclohexene.
  2. In the trans isomer OH is axial only in the diaxial conformer, where C-2 has an equatorial H; the anti H is on C-6: 3-methylcyclohexene.
Practice 77 L2 ALC

Why does dehydration of tertiary alcohols require milder conditions than primary alcohols?

Show answer
  1. The rate-determining step is formation of a carbocation (or E2 transition state with cationic character); the 3° cation is more stable (hyperconjugation, +I), so the activation energy is lower: 3° alcohols dehydrate in 20–50 % acid at 25–80 °C; 1° need 95 % acid at 170–180 °C.
Practice 78 L2 ALC

Explain why ethanol gives diethyl ether at 413 K but ethene at 443 K.

Show answer
  1. Ether formation is an SN2 (bimolecular, low activation energy, small entropy gain) favoured at the lower temperature with excess alcohol; elimination has higher activation energy but forms more molecules (greater ΔS), so it wins at the higher temperature.
Practice 79 L2 ALC

A mixture of ethanol and propan-1-ol is heated with conc. H2SO4 at 413 K. How many ethers can form?

Show answer
  1. Three: diethyl ether, dipropyl ether and ethyl propyl ether (mixed). The mixture is hard to separate.
Practice 80 L2 ALC

Give the products of oxidation of (i) CH3CH2CH2OH and (ii) (CH3)2CHCH2OH with (a) PCC, (b) hot acidified KMnO4.

Show answer
  1. (i) PCC: propanal; KMnO4: propanoic acid. (ii) PCC: 2-methylpropanal; KMnO4: 2-methylpropanoic acid.
Practice 81 L2 ALC

Why does PCC stop at the aldehyde but Jones reagent does not?

Show answer
  1. PCC is used in anhydrous CH2Cl2: the aldehyde cannot form a hydrate (gem-diol), which is the species that Cr(VI) oxidises further. In aqueous acetone/H2SO4 (Jones) the hydrate forms and is oxidised to the acid.
Practice 82 L2 ALC

Describe the mechanism of chromic acid oxidation of propan-2-ol (3 key steps).

Show answer
  1. (1) Formation of the chromate ester (CH3)2CH–O–CrO3H from the alcohol and H2CrO4. (2) Removal of the α-H by water (E2-like) while the O–Cr bond breaks: C=O forms, Cr(VI) becomes Cr(IV). (3) Cr(IV) is reduced further (disproportionation or oxidation of another alcohol) to green Cr(III). Product: propanone.
Practice 83 L2 ALC

Which alcohol, on dehydrogenation over Cu at 573 K, gives (i) an aldehyde; (ii) a ketone; (iii) an alkene? Give one example each.

Show answer
  1. (i) A primary alcohol, e.g. ethanol → ethanal. (ii) A secondary alcohol, e.g. propan-2-ol → propanone. (iii) A tertiary alcohol, e.g. 2-methylpropan-2-ol → 2-methylpropene.
Practice 84 L2 ALC

What is the Oppenauer oxidation? Give the reagents and compare it with the MPV reduction.

Show answer
  1. Oxidation of a secondary alcohol to a ketone with Al(O-t-Bu)3 and excess acetone (hydride transferred through a six-membered cyclic transition state to acetone). MPV is the reverse: ketone + Al(O-i-Pr)3/propan-2-ol → alcohol + acetone; equilibrium is driven by excess of the cheap alcohol/ketone.
Practice 85 L2 ALC

An unknown alcohol C4H10O is oxidised by acidified K2Cr2O7 to an acid C4H8O2 without loss of carbon; with Lucas reagent it shows no turbidity at room temperature. Suggest two possible structures.

Show answer
  1. Oxidation to an acid with the same carbon number = a primary alcohol; no Lucas reaction = not benzylic/allylic.
  2. Butan-1-ol or 2-methylpropan-1-ol.
Practice 86 L2 ALC

How many of these give a positive iodoform test: CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3, CH3CH2CH2OH, C6H5CH(OH)CH3, CH3COCH2CH3, CH3CH2CHO, CH3CHO, C6H5CH2OH?

Show answer
  1. Positive: butan-2-ol, 1-phenylethanol, butanone, ethanal (four).
  2. Negative: propan-1-ol, propanal, benzyl alcohol.
  3. Four.
Practice 87 L2 ALC

Describe the mechanism of the iodoform reaction for propanone in 3 steps.

Show answer
  1. (1) OH− forms the enolate of CH3COCH3; I2 iodinates the α-carbon. Each iodination makes the next α-H more acidic, so all three H of one CH3 are replaced to give CI3COCH3. (2) OH− attacks the C=O, forming the tetrahedral intermediate. (3) CI3− is expelled (good leaving group, stabilised by three I), giving CH3COOH and CHI3 after proton transfer (acetate in base).
Practice 88 L2 ALC

How would you distinguish by chemical tests: butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol?

Show answer
  1. Lucas test: 2-methylpropan-2-ol turbid at once; butan-2-ol in about 5 min; butan-1-ol none. Confirm with Victor Meyer (red/blue/colourless), or iodoform (butan-2-ol only).
Practice 89 L2 ALC

Starting from ethanol, how can you prepare (a) ethyl ethanoate, (b) ethanal, (c) ethene, (d) iodoform?

Show answer
  1. (a) CH3COOH, H+, heat (esterification). (b) PCC or Cu/573 K. (c) conc. H2SO4, 443 K. (d) I2/NaOH, warm.
Practice 90 L2 ALC

Starting with propene, synthesise (a) propan-1-ol, (b) propane-1,2-diol, (c) glycerol.

Show answer
  1. (a) BH3·THF; H2O2/NaOH. (b) Cold dilute alk. KMnO4 (or OsO4). (c) Cl2, 773 K (allyl chloride) → HOCl → NaOH (epichlorohydrin) → H2O/OH− → glycerol.
Practice 91 L2 ALC

Starting with ethanol alone as the organic source, prepare butan-1-ol.

Show answer
  1. Ethanol → ethanal (PCC), → aldol (dil. NaOH) → crotonaldehyde (heat) → H2/Ni to butan-1-ol. Alternatively: ethanol → ethylene oxide path: ethanol → ethyl bromide → C2H5MgBr; ethanol → ethene → ethylene oxide (O2/Ag); then Grignard + oxirane → butan-1-ol.
Practice 92 L2 ALC

How many moles of HIO4 are consumed by (a) propane-1,2-diol; (b) glycerol; (c) erythritol, HOCH2(CHOH)2CH2OH; (d) butane-1,3-diol? Name the products.

Show answer
  1. (a) 1 mol: HCHO + CH3CHO. (b) 2 mol: 2HCHO + HCOOH. (c) 3 mol: 2HCHO + 2HCOOH. (d) 0 (a 1,3-diol is not cleaved).
Practice 93 L2 ALC

cis-Cyclopentane-1,2-diol reacts with HIO4 readily, but a rigid trans-diol in a fused ring does not. Why?

Show answer
  1. Cleavage goes through a five-membered cyclic periodate ester; the two OH groups must be able to reach the same iodine, which is easy for cis (and flexible trans) diols but impossible when the two OH are held diequatorial and far apart.
Practice 94 L2 ALC

Write the product of pinacol rearrangement of (a) 1,1-diphenylethane-1,2-diol and (b) 2,3-diphenylbutane-2,3-diol and explain the migrating group in each.

Show answer
  1. (a) OH leaves from the diaryl carbon (stable cation); H migrates from CH2OH: Ph2CH–CHO. (b) Phenyl migrates rather than methyl: CH3CO–C(CH3)Ph2 (3,3-diphenylbutan-2-one).
Practice 95 L2 ALC

A diol with M = 118 (C6H14O2) gives, on pinacol rearrangement, a ketone C6H12O that gives a positive iodoform test. Identify the diol.

Show answer
  1. Pinacol (2,3-dimethylbutane-2,3-diol; M = 118) → pinacolone, C6H12O, (CH3)3C–CO–CH3, which has CH3CO– (iodoform positive ✓).
Practice 96 L2 ALC

Write the equation for the action of excess HI on glycerol and calculate the mass of I2 liberated from 9.2 g of glycerol (C3H8O3).

Show answer
  1. C3H8O3 + 5HI → C3H7I + 3H2O + 2I2.
  2. 9.2 g glycerol / 92 g mol−1 = 0.10 mol → 0.20 mol I2 = 0.20 × 253.8 = 50.8 g.
Practice 97 L2 ALC

The explosion 4C3H5(NO3)3 → 12CO2 + 10H2O + 6N2 + O2 is carried out on 454 g of nitroglycerine (M = 227). Calculate the total moles of gaseous products if water is steam.

Show answer
  1. 454 g = 2.00 mol. Per 4 mol: 29 mol gas. So 2.00 × 29/4 = 14.5 mol of gas (about 325 L at STP if everything were gaseous).
Practice 98 L2 ALC

4.6 g of ethanol reacts with excess sodium. Calculate the volume of H2 at STP.

Show answer
  1. 4.6 g / 46 = 0.10 mol; 2 ROH → 1 H2; so 0.05 mol H2 = 0.05 × 22.4 = 1.12 L.
Practice 99 L2 ALC

When 1.48 g of a monohydric saturated alcohol is treated with CH3MgI, 448 mL of CH4 (STP) is liberated. Find the alcohol’s formula and name the isomers that are secondary.

Show answer
  1. 448 mL = 0.02 mol; M = 1.48/0.02 = 74. CnH2n+2O: 14n + 18 = 74 → n = 4: C4H10O.
  2. Secondary: butan-2-ol only.
