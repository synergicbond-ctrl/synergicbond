12. Aromaticity, anti-aromaticity and non-aromaticity

12.1 The four conditions and the Hückel rule

A ring is aromatic if and only if it satisfies all four of the following conditions. Fail any one and the ring is not aromatic.

  • 1. Cyclic. The π-electrons must circulate round a closed loop.
  • 2. Planar (or very nearly planar), so that the p-orbitals on all ring atoms are parallel and overlap continuously.
  • 3. Completely conjugated: every ring atom must have a p-orbital that is part of the π-system (no sp³ atoms in the ring). Ring atoms may contribute 0 (empty p), 1 (one electron in a double bond or a radical) or 2 electrons (a lone pair in a p-orbital).
  • 4. Contains (4n + 2) π-electrons (n = 0, 1, 2, 3, …): 2, 6, 10, 14, 18 …. This is the Hückel rule.

A ring that satisfies 1–3 but has 4n π-electrons (4, 8, 12 …) is anti-aromatic: it is less stable than the open-chain analogue, and the molecule distorts (non-planar or bond-localised) to escape the destabilisation. A ring that fails condition 1, 2 or 3 is non-aromatic; its stability is normal.

12.2 Why 4n + 2: a picture of the orbitals

For a planar cyclic array of N p-orbitals the π molecular orbitals form a pattern (Frost circle): one lowest-energy orbital, then pairs of degenerate orbitals, and (for even N) one highest orbital. A closed shell, with every bonding orbital filled and no electron in the non-bonding or antibonding orbitals, requires 2 electrons for the lowest orbital plus 4 for each degenerate pair: 2, 6, 10, 14 … = 4n + 2. With 4n electrons the last pair of electrons must enter a degenerate set of non-bonding orbitals, one electron each (a diradical, Hund's rule), a very unstable arrangement — this is anti-aromaticity.

Benzene π molecular orbitals (Hückel energies in units of β; α is the energy of an isolated p-orbital)
OrbitalEnergyOccupation in benzene
ψ₆α − 2βempty (antibonding)
ψ₄, ψ₅α − β (degenerate)empty (antibonding)
ψ₂, ψ₃α + β (degenerate)filled (bonding)
ψ₁α + 2βfilled (bonding)

Total π energy of benzene = 2(α + 2β) + 4(α + β) = 6α + 8β. Three isolated ethene π bonds would give 3 × 2(α + β) = 6α + 6β. The difference, 2β, is the delocalisation (resonance) energy in Hückel theory; with β ≈ −75 kJ mol⁻¹ this is about 150 kJ mol⁻¹, in agreement with the thermochemical value in Section 9.5.

Illustration 12.2 Adv

Hückel energy of cyclobutadiene

For cyclobutadiene the Hückel π energies are α + 2β, α, α, α − 2β. Compare the delocalisation energy with that of two isolated ethene π-bonds.

  1. Four π-electrons: two in α + 2β, one each in the two degenerate non-bonding orbitals (energy α). Total = 2(α + 2β) + 2α = 4α + 4β.
  2. Two isolated π-bonds: 2 × 2(α + β) = 4α + 4β.
  3. The delocalisation energy is zero — and the molecule has two unpaired electrons in a planar square structure, so it is unstable (distortion to a rectangle gives some stabilisation). Hence cyclobutadiene is not stabilised, in sharp contrast to benzene (2β).

Anti-aromatic ions and rings
  • Evidence for the aromatic ions: tropylium bromide (C₇H₇⁺Br⁻) is an ionic solid that dissolves in water, in contrast to ordinary alkyl bromides; the cyclopropenyl cation forms isolable salts (triphenylcyclopropenium bromide is stable); the cyclopentadienyl anion is formed with unusual ease (pKₐ of cyclopentadiene is 16, as acidic as water, whereas an alkane has pKₐ ≈ 50): ferrocene is made from it.
  • Evidence for anti-aromaticity: the cyclopentadienyl cation is so unstable that it cannot be generated from cyclopentadienyl halides by ionisation; the 4π cyclopropenyl anion is not observed as a stable species, whereas the 2π cation is.
Illustration 12.3 Main

Acidity of cyclopentadiene vs cycloheptatriene

Cyclopentadiene (pKₐ 16) is about 20 pKₐ units more acidic than cycloheptatriene (pKₐ 36) although both give a conjugated carbanion. Explain.

  1. Removing H⁺ from the CH₂ of cyclopentadiene gives the cyclopentadienyl anion: 5 carbons, 6π electrons, planar, cyclic, conjugated — aromatic, a very stable anion.
  2. Removing H⁺ from cycloheptatriene gives the cycloheptatrienyl anion: 7 carbons, 8π electrons: anti-aromatic and therefore destabilised (it is even less stable than a simple allyl-type carbanion).
  3. Conversely, cycloheptatriene readily loses hydride to give the aromatic tropylium cation, whereas cyclopentadiene does not lose hydride to give the anti-aromatic Cp⁺.

12.5 Heterocycles

In a heterocycle the heteroatom's contribution to the π-system depends on its role.

  • Pyrrole-type atom (N–H, O, S with two σ-bonds in the ring): the lone pair sits in a p-orbital and is counted (2 e⁻); the other lone pair of O and S (the second one) lies in the plane and is not counted.
  • Pyridine-type atom (N with one double bond, two σ-bonds in the ring): contributes 1 e⁻ to the π-system; the in-plane lone pair is not part of it and is free to be protonated.

Illustration 12.5 Main

Basicity from aromaticity

Why is pyridine (pKₐ of conjugate acid 5.2) a base while pyrrole (pKₐ of conjugate acid ≈ −3.8, protonated on carbon) is not?

  1. In pyridine the nitrogen is sp², its lone pair lies in the plane of the ring and is not part of the aromatic sextet. Protonation leaves the sextet intact (pyridinium is still aromatic).
  2. In pyrrole the nitrogen's lone pair is one of the six π-electrons. Protonating N would take the lone pair out of the ring and destroy the aromatic sextet; hence it is not basic (protonation, in strong acid, occurs at C-2 and gives a non-aromatic cation).
Illustration 12.6 Adv

Electron count in furan

How many π electrons does furan have, which lone pair of oxygen is included, and in which orbital is the other one?

  1. Furan: two C=C bonds (4 π e⁻) + one lone pair of O in a p-orbital (2 e⁻) = 6π.
  2. The second lone pair of oxygen is in an sp² orbital lying in the plane of the ring (perpendicular to the π cloud, not counted).

12.7 Rings with exocyclic double bonds: carbonyls, fulvenes and quinones

  • Rule: treat a ring carbon bearing an exocyclic C=O (or C=C polarised toward the electronegative end) as carrying a positive charge with an empty p orbital: the ring has the π-count of the 'ring cation' resonance form. If that ring cation form is aromatic the compound is stabilised and polar; if anti-aromatic the compound is destabilised.
cyclopropenone (2π ring) cyclopentadienone (4π ring) tropone (6π ring) p-benzoquinone (non-aromatic) 6,6-dimethylfulvene azulene
Ring systems with C=O or exocyclic C=C
  • Cyclopropenone: the C⁺–O⁻ form has the aromatic 2π cyclopropenyl cation in its ring; the compound is unusually stable and polar (μ = 4.4 D).
  • Cyclopentadienone: the C⁺–O⁻ form has the anti-aromatic 4π Cp⁺ ring; the ketone is unstable and dimerises rapidly even at low temperature.
  • Tropone: the polar form has the aromatic tropylium ring (6π); tropone is polar, stable, basic at O (protonation gives hydroxytropylium).
  • Fulvene (pentafulvene, C₆H₆): the dipolar form would have an aromatic Cp⁻ ring and a carbocation on the exocyclic carbon; the molecule is only weakly polar and non-aromatic overall; donor groups on the exocyclic carbon (for example 6,6-bis(dimethylamino)fulvene) increase the polarity.
  • p-Benzoquinone: the six-membered ring has two C=O and two C=C: not aromatic (cross-conjugated diketone); it is a good oxidising agent because reduction gives the aromatic hydroquinone.
Illustration 12.8 Adv

Stability order

Arrange in decreasing stability (or aromatic character): tropone, cyclopentadienone, cyclopropenone, cyclobutadiene.

  1. Cyclopropenone (2π ring cation, stable, isolable) and tropone (6π ring cation, stable) are stabilised by aromatic dipolar structures. Cyclopentadienone (4π) is anti-aromatic and cyclobutadiene (4π) is anti-aromatic and the least stable of all.
  2. Tropone ≈ cyclopropenone > cyclopentadienone > cyclobutadiene (the last two undergo spontaneous dimerisation).

Illustration 12.10 Adv

Order of resonance energy

Arrange in decreasing resonance energy: benzene, thiophene, pyrrole, furan.

  1. Resonance energies (kcal mol⁻¹): benzene 36, thiophene 29, pyrrole 21, furan 16. The more electronegative the heteroatom, the more tightly it holds its lone pair and the less it delocalises it. S is less electronegative than N, and N than O.
  2. benzene > thiophene > pyrrole > furan. Their reactivity toward electrophiles (EAS) is the reverse: pyrrole > furan > thiophene > benzene.
