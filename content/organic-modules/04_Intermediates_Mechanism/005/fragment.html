4. Energetics of a reaction

4.1 Reading a reaction-coordinate diagram

The vertical axis is Gibbs free energy (potential energy or enthalpy are often drawn instead); the horizontal axis is the progress of the reaction. Peaks are transition states; valleys are reactants, intermediates and products.

  • The activation energy Ea (strictly ΔG‡ or ΔH‡) is the height of the peak above the reactants and controls the rate.
  • The free-energy or enthalpy change (ΔG or ΔH) is the difference between products and reactants and controls the position of equilibrium, not the rate.
  • For the same reaction Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH, with ΔH negative for an exothermic reaction.
  • A catalyst provides a path with lower barriers; it does not change ΔH or the equilibrium constant and speeds the forward and reverse reactions equally.
  • In a multistep profile the rate-determining step is the one whose transition state lies highest relative to the lowest preceding well.
Exam Trap A large negative ΔH does not mean a fast reaction, and a fast reaction need not be strongly exothermic. Thermodynamics says whether a reaction can go; kinetics says how fast it goes. A mixture of petrol and air is thermodynamically unstable yet persists until a spark supplies the activation energy.

4.2 Rates and barrier differences

The Arrhenius equation is k = A e−Ea/RT. In transition-state theory k = (kBT/h) e−ΔG‡/RT, where kBT/h = 6.2 × 1012 s−1 at 298 K. For two competing paths whose barriers differ by ΔΔG‡ the ratio of rate constants is

Formula k1/k2 = exp(ΔΔG‡/RT); at 298 K, log10(k1/k2) = ΔΔG‡/5.71 kJ mol−1 = ΔΔG‡/1.364 kcal mol−1
A difference of 5.7 kJ mol−1 gives a ratio of 10 : 1 (91 % : 9 %), 11.4 kJ mol−1 gives 100 : 1 (99 %) and 17.1 kJ mol−1 gives 1000 : 1. At higher temperature RT is larger, so the same barrier difference gives less selectivity.
Example 4 Basic

Selectivity from barriers

Two products P and Q form irreversibly from one intermediate. The transition state leading to P is 5.7 kJ mol−1 lower than the one leading to Q. Find the P : Q ratio at 298 K and at 400 K.

Show solution
  1. At 298 K: ln(kP/kQ) = 5700/(8.314 × 298) = 2.30, so kP/kQ = e2.30 = 10.0 and P is 91 % of the product.
  2. At 400 K: 5700/(8.314 × 400) = 1.714, so the ratio is e1.714 = 5.55 : 1 and P is 85 %.
  3. Raising the temperature lowers the selectivity because the barrier difference is a smaller fraction of the thermal energy RT. Selective kinetically controlled reactions are therefore run cold.

4.3 The Hammond postulate

  • Statement. If two states occur consecutively on a reaction path and have nearly the same energy, they also have nearly the same structure. The transition state of a step therefore resembles whichever neighbouring species (reactant or product of that step) is closer to it in energy.
  • In a strongly exothermic step the transition state is early and resembles the reactants.
  • In a strongly endothermic step the transition state is late and resembles the products.
  • A step that forms an unstable intermediate (carbocation, radical, carbanion) is endothermic, so its transition state resembles the intermediate. Whatever stabilises the intermediate (more alkyl groups, conjugation) therefore also stabilises the transition state, though to a smaller extent.
Hammond postulate: the transition state resembles the nearer-in-energy neighbour

Exam Trap The postulate describes what a transition state looks like; it does not say whether the reaction is fast. An early transition state is not the same thing as a low barrier, and a late one does not imply a slow reaction.
Why It Happens The transition state for the formation of an unstable intermediate lies close to the intermediate in energy, so, by the Hammond postulate, it also resembles it in structure. Features that stabilise the intermediate (alkyl groups, conjugation) therefore stabilise the transition state in the same direction, which is why carbocation stability controls the rates of SN1 and E1 reactions.
Validity Check The postulate is useful for a single step in which the transition state is near a reactant, intermediate or product in energy. For a step with a high barrier in both directions, neither neighbour is a good model of the transition state, and the postulate gives no guidance.

4.4 Kinetic and thermodynamic control

When a reactant can give two products through different transition states, the outcome depends on whether the product-forming steps are reversible.

  • Kinetic control (irreversible steps, usually low temperature and short times): the product that forms faster, through the lower transition state, predominates. [A]/[B] = kA/kB = e−ΔΔG‡/RT.
  • Thermodynamic control (reversible steps, usually higher temperature and longer times): the more stable product predominates whatever the barriers. [A]/[B] = K = e−ΔG°/RT.

To identify the regime, take the product that dominates at low temperature and warm it with the reagent or catalyst: if it converts to the other isomer, the first product was formed under kinetic control.

Counterexample Kinetic and thermodynamic control give the same product when the same product is formed by the lower transition state and is also the more stable one; the temperature or time then has no effect on the product ratio. The comparison of the two regimes is informative only when the faster-forming product is not the more stable one.
Special Case Kinetic control can be maintained at higher temperature if the products cannot re-form the intermediate (very large reverse barrier), and a reaction that is reversible at −78 °C (a very weak bond formed) can be under thermodynamic control there. Temperature is a guide, not a definition: the criterion is the reversibility of the product-forming step.
Concept check. A student heats the low-temperature product of a reaction and obtains an isomer. Which product was formed under kinetic control, and what does the experiment show?
Show answer
The low-temperature product was kinetic; it re-forms the intermediate at the higher temperature, showing that the product-forming step is reversible and that the isomer is the more stable (thermodynamic) product.

4.5 The Curtin–Hammett principle

If two reactive conformers or intermediates interconvert much faster than they react, the ratio of the products is determined only by the difference in free energy between the two product-forming transition states, not by the populations of the conformers. A less populated conformer can give the major product. The E2 elimination of menthyl and neomenthyl chlorides illustrates the related idea that only the conformer with the leaving group axial (and a β-hydrogen axial, anti-periplanar to it) can eliminate, so reaction rates follow the population of that reactive conformer.

Example 5 Advanced

Kinetic or thermodynamic?

Hydrogen bromide adds to buta-1,3-diene to give mainly 3-bromobut-1-ene at −80 °C and mainly 1-bromobut-2-ene at 40 °C. Explain this using the intermediate, and say what happens if the −80 °C product is warmed.

Show solution
  1. Protonation of C-1 gives the allylic cation CH3–CH+–CH=CH2 ↔ CH3–CH=CH–CH2+; positive charge is shared between C-2 (secondary) and C-4 (primary), with more on C-2.
  2. Bromide attacks the secondary carbon, which carries more positive charge, through the lower transition state: this gives the 1,2-adduct, and attack is irreversible at −80 °C.
  3. The 1,4-adduct has a more substituted (disubstituted) double bond and is more stable.
  4. At 40 °C the allylic C–Br bond of the 1,2-adduct ionises again at a useful rate, the cation re-forms, and the system moves into the lower well: the 1,4-adduct predominates (thermodynamic control).
  5. Warming the pure 1,2-adduct converts it into the 1,4-adduct, which proves that the low-temperature product was formed under kinetic control.

Example 6 Advanced

Equilibrium composition

At 298 K the 1,4-adduct is 8.0 kJ mol−1 more stable than the 1,2-adduct. Calculate the equilibrium ratio at 298 K and at 400 K.

Show solution
  1. K = eΔG/RT, with ΔG = 8000 J mol−1. At 298 K: K = exp(8000/(8.314 × 298)) = exp(3.229) = 25.3, so about 96 % is the 1,4-adduct.
  2. At 400 K: K = exp(8000/(8.314 × 400)) = exp(2.406) = 11.1, so about 92 % is the 1,4-adduct.
  3. The thermodynamic preference decreases slightly as temperature rises, but the rate of equilibration increases, so the equilibrium is actually reached.
Special Case Curtin–Hammett conditions. The principle holds only when conformers (or intermediates) interconvert much faster than either reacts. If one conformer reacts faster than it can be replenished, the product ratio instead reflects the initial populations.
Concept check. Two rapidly interconverting conformers react through transition states of free energy 60 and 55 kJ mol−1 (relative to the more stable conformer). By what factor does the second product predominate at 298 K?
Show answer
exp(5000/(8.314 × 298)) = 7.5 : 1; only the difference between the transition states matters.

4.6 The frontier-orbital view of reactivity

A curved arrow represents the flow of electrons from a filled orbital to an empty one, and the orbital picture explains why particular pairs of species react and why they approach each other in a particular geometry. Two rules are enough.

  • Rule 1. Stabilisation comes from filled–empty interaction. When two orbitals interact they form a bonding and an antibonding combination. If both are filled, the net effect is repulsion. If one is filled and the other empty, the two electrons enter the lowered combination and bonding results. The dominant interaction is between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the nucleophile and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the electrophile.
  • Rule 2. Overlap and energy gap decide the strength. The interaction is large when the two orbitals overlap well (the right symmetry and direction) and when their energies are close. A reaction therefore has a preferred geometry: the one that maximises overlap between the HOMO and LUMO.

MOT Lens Why filled–empty interaction bonds and filled–filled repels. Two interacting orbitals produce one lower and one higher combination. Two electrons enter the lower: the energy falls, which is bonding. Four electrons fill both: the destabilisation of the upper combination exceeds the stabilisation of the lower, so the net effect is repulsion (the reason a nucleophile is not attracted to a filled π bond). Hence reactivity is always a question of which orbital of one reactant is filled and which orbital of the other is empty.
Bonding Insight Curved-arrow structures are localised models: each pair of electrons is a bond or a lone pair. MO theory describes the same electrons delocalised over the molecule. The two descriptions agree when the model is used within its limits: arrows are excellent for tracking where electrons go; MOs are needed for questions of geometry (backside attack, the Bürgi–Dunitz angle), conjugation, aromaticity and pericyclic selectivity. Neither replaces the other, and the localised drawing of resonance structures is the localised model's way of representing a delocalised orbital.

Three consequences follow. (1) In allyl the middle orbital is nonbonding with a node at the central carbon (coefficients 0.707, 0, −0.707). The allyl cation, radical and anion differ only in whether this orbital holds 0, 1 or 2 electrons, so they have the same π delocalisation energy (0.83|β|) and the charge, spin or lone-pair density sits on the two terminal carbons. A nucleophile attacks the allyl cation at a terminus because the LUMO has no weight on the central carbon. (2) In the benzyl cation, radical or anion the nonbonding orbital has squared coefficients of 4/7 (0.571) on the benzylic carbon and 1/7 (0.143) on each of the two ortho and the para ring carbons, and zero on the meta carbons; the charge or spin is therefore delocalised onto o- and p-positions only.
