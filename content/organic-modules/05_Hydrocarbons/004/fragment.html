3. Alkanes: structure, isomerism and physical properties

Alkanes are saturated: every carbon is sp3, bonded tetrahedrally (109.5°), with C–C = 154 pm and C–H = 109 pm. Only σ bonds are present. The C–C and C–H bonds are nearly non-polar (electronegativity C 2.5, H 2.1), so alkanes carry no site that a nucleophile or electrophile can attack. This is why their only common chemistry is radical chemistry (needing homolysis) and combustion. Older name: paraffins (Latin parum affinis = little affinity).

Carbon and hydrogen types are the language of radical halogenation and of NMR-free JEE symmetry counting:

  • Primary (1°) carbon: attached to one other carbon. Its H's are 1° hydrogens.
  • Secondary (2°) carbon: attached to two other carbons.
  • Tertiary (3°) carbon: attached to three other carbons.
  • Quaternary (4°) carbon: attached to four carbons (it carries no hydrogen).

3.2 Physical properties and their reasons

Alkane molecules are held together only by London dispersion forces, which grow with surface area and polarisability.

  • Boiling point rises with chain length (about 20–30 K per CH2), because the contact surface and the number of dispersion contacts rise.
  • Branching lowers the boiling point at equal carbon number: a branched molecule is more spherical, has less surface area, and fewer dispersion contacts. n-Pentane 309 K > isopentane 301 K > neopentane 283 K.
  • Melting point depends on both attractions and packing in the crystal. Highly symmetric molecules pack well, so neopentane (melting point −17 °C) melts much higher than n-pentane (−130 °C) and isopentane (−160 °C). In the straight-chain series the melting points alternate: even-numbered chains lie higher than odd-numbered neighbours because even chains pack better (their two terminal CH3 groups point opposite ways).
  • Solubility: insoluble in water (cannot hydrogen bond), soluble in non-polar solvents. Density < 1 g mL-1, so they float on water.
