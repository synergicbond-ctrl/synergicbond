7. Other tautomerisms: imine-enamine, nitro-aci and lactam-lactim

The same ideas extend to other functional groups that have a mobile hydrogen on an atom adjacent to a multiple bond. Three further types are commonly asked.

Imine-enamine. An imine with an α-hydrogen is in equilibrium with an enamine, the nitrogen analogue of an enol. As with ketones, the imine form is normally favoured; an enamine is more important as a reactive intermediate (enamine alkylation and acylation) than as the dominant form.

Nitro-aci (nitronic acid). A nitroalkane having an α-hydrogen, for example nitromethane, is in equilibrium with its aci form, CH2=N(O)OH. This is why nitroalkanes with α-hydrogens dissolve in aqueous alkali: the aci form is acidic (nitromethane has pKa about 10). Nitro compounds without an α-hydrogen, such as nitrobenzene, do not do this.

Lactam-lactim. An amide whose N-H is next to C=O (a lactam when cyclic) is in equilibrium with the imidic acid (a lactim), which has C=N and an OH. 2-Pyridone is the lactam; 2-hydroxypyridine is the lactim. In the solid state and in polar solvents the lactam (pyridone) form dominates, because the amide-like C=O is strong and dimers are held by hydrogen bonding; in the gas phase the two forms are close in energy.

2-Pyridone (lactam, left) and 2-hydroxypyridine (lactim, right).

Key idea. In every case the rule is the same: tautomers are related by moving H (plus the π bond) across a three-atom unit H-X-Y=Z to X=Y-Z-H, where X and Z may be C, N or O. The more stable tautomer usually keeps the stronger bonds (C=O over C=C, C=O over C=N) or gains aromatic stabilisation.
Trap. Ordinary amides, esters and ketones are not drawn as their lactim or enol forms in a mechanism unless the question asks about the tautomer. The minor tautomer matters in reactivity (for example in enolate chemistry), not in structure assignment.
