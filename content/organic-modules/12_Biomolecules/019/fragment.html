3.1 Levels of protein structure

Proteins are polypeptides (usually 50–3000 residues, 5–300 kDa) with a defined three-dimensional fold that determines function. Four levels of organisation are recognised.

LevelWhat it isStabilised by
Primarythe amino acid sequence (N → C) and disulfide positionscovalent bonds (peptide, S–S)
Secondarylocal regular folds of the backbone: α-helix, β-sheet, turnshydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H
Tertiarythe overall 3D fold of one polypeptideside-chain interactions: hydrophobic effect (main driver), H-bonds, salt bridges (–COO− … +H3N–), disulfide bonds, van der Waals, metal ions
Quaternaryarrangement of several folded chains (subunits)same non-covalent forces; sometimes S–S

The α-helix

  • Right-handed coil of the backbone; 3.6 residues per turn; pitch 5.4 Å (rise per residue 1.5 Å, so 3.6 × 1.5 = 5.4 Å).
  • Each C=O is H-bonded to the N–H of the residue four positions ahead (i → i + 4), closing a 13-atom ring (so the helix is called the 3.613 helix). All H-bonds are parallel to the helix axis.
  • Side chains point outwards, so bulky and charged residues interfere; proline (no N–H, ring-restricted φ) and glycine (too flexible) break helices.
  • Typical dihedral angles φ ≈ −57°, ψ ≈ −47°.
  • Examples: keratin (hair, nails, wool: two helices wound into a coiled coil, with disulfide cross-links; perm = reduce S–S, reshape, re-oxidise), myosin, haemoglobin and myoglobin (≈ 75–80 % helical).

The β-pleated sheet

  • Extended strands (rise ≈ 3.3–3.5 Å per residue) side by side, with H-bonds between strands (perpendicular to the strand direction). Side chains alternate above and below the sheet.
  • Parallel (both strands N → C in the same direction) or antiparallel (opposite directions; the stronger, with straight H-bonds).
  • Examples: silk fibroin (antiparallel sheets of repeating Gly–Ala/Ser; flexible but inextensible), β-keratin of feathers; amyloid fibrils (disease: Alzheimer's, prions).
  • β-turns (four residues, H-bond from C=O of residue i to N–H of i + 3) reverse the chain direction; Pro and Gly are frequent in turns.

Collagen

The most abundant animal protein. Each chain has the repeat Gly–X–Y (X often Pro, Y often 4-hydroxyproline); three left-handed chains twist into a right-handed triple helix (Gly, being the smallest, must be at every third position in the centre). Hydroxylation of Pro needs vitamin C (ascorbic acid); its absence causes scurvy (weak collagen: bleeding gums).

Key idea Anfinsen's experiment (ribonuclease A): after urea/β-mercaptoethanol denaturation and removal of the denaturant, the protein refolds spontaneously into the active enzyme. So the primary sequence alone determines the native fold. (Large proteins often need chaperones in the cell.)
<h4>Haemoglobin and sickle-cell disease</h4>

Haemoglobin A is a tetramer 2α + 2β (574 residues, M ≈ 64.5 kDa) with four haem groups, each an iron(II) in a porphyrin; each Fe binds one O2 reversibly (cooperative binding; sigmoidal curve). Iron content: 4 × 55.85/64 500 = 0.35 % by mass. In sickle-cell anaemia a single substitution, Glu-6 → Val in the β-chain (a codon change GAG → GUG, i.e. A → T in DNA), exposes a hydrophobic patch so that deoxy-Hb S polymerises into fibres, distorting the red cells.

Trap The haem Fe is Fe(II); oxidation to Fe(III) gives methaemoglobin which cannot bind O2. Carbon monoxide binds Fe(II) ≈ 200 times more strongly than O2.
