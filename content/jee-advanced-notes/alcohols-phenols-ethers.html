1. Classification and Structure

Classification of alcohols

An alcohol has an –OH group on an sp3 carbon. A phenol has –OH directly on an aromatic ring carbon. An ether has R–O–R′ (O bonded to two carbons).

  • By number of –OH: monohydric (one –OH), dihydric (two, e.g. glycol), trihydric (three, e.g. glycerol).
  • By the carbinol carbon (C bearing –OH): 1° (attached to one C), 2° (two C), 3° (three C).
  • Allylic alcohol: –OH on the C next to C=C. Benzylic: –OH on the C next to an aromatic ring. These can be 1°, 2° or 3°.
  • Vinylic alcohol (enol): –OH directly on C=C carbon (sp2). Usually unstable and tautomerises to the carbonyl compound.

Classification of ethers

Simple (symmetrical) ether: both groups same, e.g. C2H5–O–C2H5. Mixed (unsymmetrical): groups different, e.g. CH3–O–C2H5, C6H5–O–CH3. Cyclic ethers: oxirane (epoxide), oxolane (THF), 1,4-dioxane.

Structure and bonding

  • In alcohols O is sp3 with two lone pairs. The C–O–H angle in methanol is 108.9°, a little less than 109.5° because lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than bond pair repulsion.
  • In phenol the C–O bond is 136 pm (methanol: 142 pm). Reasons: (i) partial double-bond character from conjugation of an O lone pair with the ring, (ii) the carbon is sp2 (more s character, shorter bond).
  • In ethers the C–O–C angle is 111.7° (larger than tetrahedral) because of repulsion between the two bulky alkyl groups. C–O ≈ 141 pm.
Polarity: all three are polar (C–O and O–H bonds). Dipole moment: CH3OH 1.71 D, C2H5OC2H5 1.18 D. Ethers lack O–H, so they cannot H-bond with each other but can accept H-bonds from water.

2. Nomenclature

IUPAC rules for alcohols

  1. Choose the longest chain that contains the C bearing –OH (it need not be the longest chain overall).
  2. Number from the end that gives –OH the lowest locant. –OH has priority over C=C, C≡C, halogens and alkyl groups.
  3. Replace the final “-e” of the alkane with “-ol”: alkanol. With two or more –OH, keep the “e”: ethane-1,2-diol, propane-1,2,3-triol.
  4. Unsaturated alcohols: alk-x-en-y-ol, e.g. pent-4-en-2-ol.
  5. Cyclic alcohols: the ring C bearing –OH is C-1; the locant 1 is usually not written.

Phenols

The parent is “phenol” (benzenol). Ring C bearing –OH is C-1. Common names: cresols (methylphenols), catechol, resorcinol, quinol.

Ethers

Common names: name both alkyl/aryl groups alphabetically + “ether” (ethyl methyl ether). IUPAC: ethers are alkoxyalkanes; the larger group is the parent, the smaller R–O– is the alkoxy prefix.

3. Preparation of Alcohols

Acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes

Alkene + water in the presence of acid gives an alcohol. Addition follows Markovnikov's rule because the reaction goes through the more stable carbocation. Rearrangement of the carbocation is possible.

Mechanism (one step per drawing)

Step 1: the π bond takes H+ from H3O+. H goes to the terminal C so that the 2° carbocation forms.

Step 2: water (nucleophile) attacks the carbocation.

Step 3: water removes H+ from the oxonium ion.

Rearrangement example. 3,3-Dimethylbut-1-ene with H2O/H+ gives mainly 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol, because the 2° carbocation undergoes a 1,2-methyl shift to the 3° carbocation.

Oxymercuration–demercuration

Markovnikov addition of H–OH without rearrangement. Hg(OAc)2 forms a bridged mercurinium ion (no free carbocation), water attacks the more substituted C (anti addition), then NaBH4 replaces –HgOAc by H.

Hydroboration–oxidation

B2H6 (as BH3·THF) adds across C=C in one concerted four-centre step: B goes to the less substituted C, H to the more substituted C. Oxidation by alkaline H2O2 replaces B by OH with retention. Net result: anti-Markovnikov, syn addition of H and OH, no rearrangement.

  • Aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol. Reagents: H2/Pd or Ni or Pt, NaBH4, LiAlH4.

Grignard reagents are destroyed by any acidic H (H2O, ROH, RCOOH, NH, terminal alkyne). So substrates with –OH, –NH2, –COOH must be protected, and ether must be dry.

Other methods

  • Hydrolysis of alkyl halides: R–X + aq. KOH → R–OH + KX (1° by SN2; 3° gives mainly alkene, so better with water/Ag2O).
  • From 1° amines: RCH2NH2 + HNO2 (NaNO2 + HCl) → RCH2OH + N2↑ + H2O (carbocation, mixtures possible).
  • Methanol (industrial): CO + 2H2 → CH3OH (ZnO–Cr2O3, 200–300 atm, 573–673 K).
  • Ethanol by fermentation: C12H22O11 + H2O → C6H12O6 (glucose) + C6H12O6 (fructose) (invertase); C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2 (zymase). Air must be kept out, otherwise ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid.

4. Physical Properties

Boiling point

  • Alcohols and phenols have high boiling points due to intermolecular H-bonding. Ethers cannot H-bond with each other, so their boiling points are close to alkanes of similar mass.
  • B.p. rises with molar mass (larger van der Waals forces) and falls with branching (smaller surface area): butan-1-ol 391 K > butan-2-ol 373 K > 2-methylpropan-2-ol 355 K.

Solubility

Lower alcohols are miscible with water (H-bonding with water). Solubility falls as the hydrophobic alkyl part grows. Ethers of low mass are fairly soluble (ethoxyethane ≈ butan-1-ol, ≈ 7–8 g per 100 mL) because the ether O accepts H-bonds from water.

Intramolecular H-bonding in o-nitrophenol

o-Nitrophenol has intramolecular H-bonding (chelation), p-nitrophenol has intermolecular H-bonding (association). So o-nitrophenol has lower b.p., lower water solubility and is steam volatile; this is used to separate the two isomers by steam distillation.

5. Chemical Reactions of Alcohols

Acidity and reaction with active metals

Alcohols are very weak Brønsted acids. They react with Na, K, Al to give alkoxides and H2: 2R–OH + 2Na → 2R–O–Na+ + H2↑.

  • Acid strength (in solution): CH3OH > 1° > 2° > 3°. Bulky alkoxides are poorly solvated, and alkyl groups (+I) destabilise the anion.
  • Water (pKa 15.7) is a slightly stronger acid than ethanol (≈ 15.9), so R–O– is a stronger base than OH–: RO–Na+ + H2O → ROH + NaOH.
  • Electron-withdrawing groups raise acidity: CF3CH2OH (pKa ≈ 12.4) is much more acidic than ethanol.
  • Alcohols are also weak bases (O lone pairs): R–OH + H+ ⇌ R–OH2+. This protonation is the first step of most C–O cleavage reactions.

Esterification (O–H cleavage)

R–OH + R′COOH ⇌ R′COOR + H2O (conc. H2SO4, reversible; water is removed to push it forward). With acid chlorides or anhydrides the reaction is fast; pyridine is added to absorb HCl.

Isotope labelling: with R–18OH, the 18O ends up in the ester, not in water. So the alcohol loses H (O–H cleavage) and the acid loses OH (acyl–O cleavage).

Reaction with HX; Lucas test (C–O cleavage)

R–OH + HX → R–X + H2O. Reactivity of HX: HI > HBr > HCl. Reactivity of alcohols: 3° > 2° > 1° (3° and 2° by SN1 via carbocation; 1° by SN2). HCl needs anhydrous ZnCl2 (Lewis acid) for 1° and 2°.

Oxidation and dehydrogenation

  • 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid with strong oxidants (acidified K2Cr2O7, KMnO4). To stop at aldehyde: PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) in CH2Cl2, or CrO3 in anhydrous medium, or distil the aldehyde as it forms.

Pinacol–pinacolone rearrangement

A vicinal diol (pinacol) with acid gives a ketone (pinacolone) by carbocation formation and 1,2-shift. The driving force is the very stable protonated carbonyl (oxocarbenium) ion.

Step 1: protonation and loss of water gives the 3° carbocation.

Step 2: 1,2-methyl shift; the positive charge moves to the C bearing –OH (stabilised by O lone pair).

Step 3: loss of H+.

Migratory aptitude (usual): H > aryl > 3° alkyl > 2° > 1° > CH3. In unsymmetrical diols, the OH that leaves is the one giving the more stable carbocation.

Periodic acid cleavage of 1,2-diols

HIO4 breaks the C–C bond between two –OH carbons (cyclic periodate ester): each carbinol C becomes C=O. Ethane-1,2-diol → 2 HCHO. Glycerol + 2HIO4 → 2HCHO + HCOOH. Pb(OAc)4 does the same.

Iodoform test and distinguishing tests

Alcohols with the CH3–CH(OH)– unit (ethanol and methyl carbinols) give a yellow precipitate of CHI3 with I2 + NaOH (the alcohol is first oxidised to CH3CO–). Methanol does not respond.

7. Acidity of Phenols

Why phenol is acidic

Phenol (pKa 10.0) is about a million times more acidic than ethanol. (i) The O is attached to sp2 carbon, which withdraws electrons. (ii) The phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised: the negative charge spreads to the ortho and para carbons. Phenol itself also has resonance, but its structures involve charge separation, so the phenoxide gains more stabilisation.

Resonance of phenoxide ion (one shift per drawing)

In I the charge is on electronegative O (most important). II–IV place the charge on carbon. Phenol does not react with NaHCO3 (H2CO3, pKa 6.4, is stronger), but dissolves in NaOH.

Effect of substituents

  • Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO2, –CN, –X) increase acidity, most strongly at o and p, where –NO2 takes the negative charge by resonance (−R). At m only −I acts.
  • Electron-releasing groups (–CH3, –OCH3, –NH2) decrease acidity (o/p mostly). –OCH3 at m increases acidity slightly (−I only, no +R at m).
  • o-Nitrophenol is a little weaker than p-nitrophenol because intramolecular H-bonding holds the proton.

8. Electrophilic Substitution and Other Reactions of Phenol

Why the ring is activated

–OH donates an O lone pair into the ring (+R > −I), raising electron density at o and p. So –OH is a strongly activating, o/p-directing group. Phenoxide (–O–) is even more activating (reacts with weak electrophiles such as CO2 and ArN2+).

Halogenation

  • Bromine water (polar solvent, phenol partly ionised): instant white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Test for phenol.
  • Br2 in CS2 or CHCl3 at 273 K (non-polar, low T): monobromination, p-bromophenol (major) + o-bromophenol.
+

Nitration

  • Dilute HNO3, 298 K: mixture of o- and p-nitrophenol (separated by steam distillation; o-isomer is steam volatile).
  • Conc. HNO3: 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid), but with much oxidation. Better route: sulphonate first with conc. H2SO4 to phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid, then conc. HNO3 replaces both –SO3H and nitrates position 6.
+

Sulphonation

Conc. H2SO4: at 288–293 K, o-hydroxybenzenesulphonic acid (kinetic product); at 373 K, p-hydroxybenzenesulphonic acid (thermodynamic product). Sulphonation is reversible, so the ortho isomer changes to para on heating.

Kolbe's reaction (Kolbe–Schmitt)

Sodium phenoxide reacts with CO2 (weak electrophile) at about 400 K and 4–7 atm. Acidification gives salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid). The Na+ chelates phenoxide O and CO2, directing attack to the ortho position. (With K-phenoxide at higher T the para acid is favoured.)

Reimer–Tiemann reaction

Phenol + CHCl3 + aq. NaOH (340 K), then H+: –CHO is introduced at the ortho position, giving salicylaldehyde. The electrophile is dichlorocarbene.

Step 1: formation of dichlorocarbene

CHCl3 + OH– ⇌ –:CCl3 + H2O; then –:CCl3 → :CCl2 + Cl– (α-elimination).

Step 2: phenoxide attacks the carbene at the ortho carbon

Step 3: tautomerisation, hydrolysis, acidification

With CCl4 in place of CHCl3, the product is salicylic acid (–COOH introduced).

Fries rearrangement

Phenyl esters with anhydrous AlCl3 give hydroxyaryl ketones. Low temperature (≈ 298 K) favours para; high temperature (≈ 433 K) favours ortho (stabilised by chelation with Al).

Coupling with diazonium salts

Phenol couples with benzenediazonium chloride in mildly alkaline solution (pH 9–10) at the para position, giving orange p-hydroxyazobenzene. If para is blocked, coupling occurs at ortho.

Other reactions

  • Zinc dust distillation: C6H5OH + Zn → C6H6 + ZnO.
  • Oxidation: Na2Cr2O7/H2SO4 gives p-benzoquinone. Phenol slowly turns pink in air for the same reason.
  • Hydrogenation: H2/Ni, 433 K → cyclohexanol.
  • Neutral FeCl3: violet complex, [Fe(OC6H5)6]3– (test for phenolic –OH; enols also respond).
  • Phthalein reaction: phenol + phthalic anhydride (conc. H2SO4) → phenolphthalein (pink in base).
  • Friedel–Crafts is poor on free phenol (O complexes AlCl3); use ester/Fries route instead.
  • Williamson: phenoxide + CH3I → anisole (see Section 9).

9. Preparation of Ethers; Williamson Synthesis

Dehydration of alcohols

2C2H5OH → C2H5OC2H5 + H2O (conc. H2SO4, 413 K). Mechanism: protonated alcohol is attacked by a second alcohol molecule (SN2), then loss of H+. Suitable only for symmetrical ethers from 1° alcohols; 2° and 3° alcohols give alkenes. A mixture of two alcohols gives three ethers.

Williamson ether synthesis

An alkoxide (or phenoxide) reacts with an alkyl halide by SN2: R–O–Na+ + R′–X → R–O–R′ + NaX. Both symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers can be made.

Rule: the alkyl halide must be 1° (or CH3). The bulky or aryl part must come from the alkoxide/phenoxide. A 3° halide with a strong base undergoes E2 and gives an alkene. Aryl halides do not undergo SN2.

Alkene + ROH with Hg(OAc)2 (or Hg(O2CCF3)2), then NaBH4: Markovnikov ether, no rearrangement. Useful for ethers with a 3° group: 2-methylpropene + CH3OH → tert-butyl methyl ether.

10. Reactions of Ethers; Ether Cleavage

Cleavage by HI / HBr

Ethers are fairly unreactive (used as solvents), but are cleaved by hot conc. HI or HBr (HI > HBr >> HCl). R–O–R′ + HI → R–OH + R′–I; with excess HI the alcohol also becomes R–I.

  • Step 1: protonation of ether O (forms a good leaving group).
  • Step 2: if both groups are 1°/CH3, SN2: I– attacks the less hindered carbon; the smaller group becomes the iodide.
  • If one group is 3° (or benzylic/allylic), SN1: the C–O bond to that group breaks, giving the 3° iodide.
  • Aryl alkyl ethers always give phenol + alkyl iodide: the O–aryl bond has partial double-bond character and sp2 C does not undergo SN1/SN2.

Ring opening of epoxides

Epoxides are reactive because of ring strain. The site of attack depends on the conditions.

  • Acidic (H+/CH3OH): protonated epoxide has SN1-like character; the nucleophile attacks the more substituted C (more positive charge).
  • Basic (CH3O–/CH3OH, RMgX, LiAlH4): SN2; attack at the less hindered C.
  • Both are anti (backside) openings: cyclohexene oxide gives trans products.

Other reactions of ethers

  • Peroxide formation: on standing in air and light, ethers form explosive hydroperoxides (at α-C). Ether is tested with FeSO4/KSCN (red) or KI (yellow I2), and peroxides removed by shaking with FeSO4 solution before distillation.
  • As Lewis bases: ethers form oxonium salts with conc. H2SO4 (so ethers dissolve in cold conc. H2SO4, unlike alkanes) and complexes with BF3, RMgX.
  • Halogenation: Cl2 in dark substitutes α-H; in light all H can be replaced (perchlorodiethyl ether).
  • Crown ethers: 18-crown-6 binds K+ in its cavity; KMnO4 dissolves in benzene with it (“purple benzene”).

12. Solved JEE-style Examples

Examples 1–4: preparation and rearrangement

Example 1. Give the major product when 1-methylcyclohexene is treated with (a) H2O/H+, (b) B2H6 then H2O2/OH–.
(a) Markovnikov: 1-methylcyclohexan-1-ol (3°). (b) anti-Markovnikov, syn: trans-2-methylcyclohexan-1-ol.
Example 2. Which Grignard reagent and carbonyl compound give 2-phenylbutan-2-ol? Give all three combinations.
C6H5COCH3 + C2H5MgBr; C6H5COC2H5 + CH3MgBr; CH3COC2H5 + C6H5MgBr. Each, after H3O+, gives the same 3° alcohol.
Example 3. 3-Methylbutan-2-ol is heated with HBr. Predict the major product.
Protonation, loss of water → 2° carbocation → 1,2-hydride shift → 3° carbocation → Br–. Major: 2-bromo-2-methylbutane.
Example 4. What is the product when 2,3-diphenylbutane-2,3-diol is heated with H+?
Carbocation at either C (both equivalent, 3° benzylic). Phenyl has higher migratory aptitude than methyl, so Ph migrates: 3,3-diphenylbutan-2-one, CH3CO–C(C6H5)2CH3.

Examples 5–8: acidity and phenol reactions

Example 5. Arrange in decreasing acidity: phenol, p-nitrophenol, m-nitrophenol, p-cresol, ethanol, picric acid.
Picric acid > p-nitrophenol > m-nitrophenol > phenol > p-cresol > ethanol. –NO2 at para withdraws by −R and −I; at meta only −I; –CH3 releases (+I, hyperconjugation).
Example 6. Which of these liberate CO2 from NaHCO3: phenol, 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, benzoic acid, ethanol?
Only 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (pKa 0.4) and benzoic acid (pKa 4.2). Both are stronger acids than H2CO3 (pKa 6.4).
Example 7. Phenol → (i) NaOH (ii) CO2, 400 K, 6 atm (iii) H+ → A; A + (CH3CO)2O → B. Identify A and B.
A = salicylic acid (Kolbe). B = acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin), by acetylation of the phenolic –OH.
Example 8. Why does phenol give a tribromo product with bromine water but a monobromo product with Br2 in CS2?
In water, phenol partly ionises to phenoxide (much more activating) and water polarises Br2, giving Br+-like electrophile easily; all o/p positions react. In non-polar CS2 at low T, there is no ionisation and Br2 is less polarised, so reaction stops at monobromination (para major).

Examples 9–12: ethers and mixed problems

Example 9. Give the products of heating each with one mole of HI: (a) CH3OCH2CH2CH3, (b) (CH3)3C–O–CH3, (c) C6H5OCH2CH3.
(a) SN2 at CH3: CH3I + CH3CH2CH2OH. (b) SN1: (CH3)3C–I + CH3OH. (c) C6H5OH + CH3CH2I.
Example 10. Suggest the best Williamson route to (a) 2-ethoxy-2-methylpropane, (b) benzyl ethyl ether, (c) ethyl phenyl ether.
(a) (CH3)3CO–Na+ + C2H5Br. (b) C2H5O–Na+ + C6H5CH2Br (benzylic 1° halide is excellent for SN2), or C6H5CH2O– + C2H5Br. (c) C6H5O–Na+ + C2H5Br.
Example 11. Compound X (C4H10O) gives turbidity with Lucas reagent in about 5 minutes, gives a yellow precipitate with I2/NaOH, and is optically active. Identify X.
2° alcohol + CH3CH(OH)– unit + chiral centre: X = butan-2-ol.
Example 12. 2,2-Dimethyloxirane is treated separately with (a) CH3MgBr then H3O+, (b) HBr. Give the products.
(a) Basic/SN2: CH3– attacks CH2: (CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3, 2-methylbutan-2-ol. (b) Acidic: Br– attacks the 3° C: (CH3)2CBr–CH2OH, 2-bromo-2-methylpropan-1-ol.
Example 13. Ethanol with conc. H2SO4: what are the products at 413 K and 443 K? Explain.
413 K: ethoxyethane (SN2 by a second ethanol on protonated ethanol). 443 K: ethene (elimination is favoured at higher temperature because it has larger ΔS).
Example 14. A phenol C7H8O gives only one monobromo product on bromination in CS2 at 273 K. Identify it among the cresols.
p-Cresol: para is blocked, both ortho positions to –OH are equivalent, so a single product, 2-bromo-4-methylphenol, forms.
