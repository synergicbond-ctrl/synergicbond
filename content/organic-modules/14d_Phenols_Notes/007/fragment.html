8. Oxidation and reduction of phenols

Phenols are easily oxidised, and the products depend on the oxidant. Chromic acid (Na2Cr2O7 with H2SO4) converts phenol into p-benzoquinone. Catechol and hydroquinone, the 1,2- and 1,4-dihydroxybenzenes, are oxidised even by mild reagents such as silver oxide or Fe(III) to o- and p-benzoquinone respectively. Resorcinol (1,3-dihydroxybenzene) cannot give a quinone because the two oxygens cannot be conjugated through a cyclic dione.

Reactant:

Reagent: Na2Cr2O7, H2SO4

Product:

p-Benzoquinone. Phenol is oxidised at the para position.

Reactant:

Reagent: mild oxidant (e.g. Ag2O or FeCl3)

Product:

Hydroquinone → p-benzoquinone releases 2 e– and 2 H+ (a reversible redox couple); hydroquinone is a good reducing agent (photographic developer, antioxidant).

Hydroquinone and other phenolic antioxidants work by donating the phenolic hydrogen atom to chain-carrying radicals. The phenoxyl radical formed is stabilised by delocalisation into the ring and, in hindered phenols such as BHT, shielded by bulky ortho groups so that it does not propagate the chain.

Reduction can be directed at two different sites. Distillation with zinc dust removes the oxygen as zinc oxide and gives benzene. Catalytic hydrogenation over nickel at elevated temperature and pressure saturates the ring and gives cyclohexanol (3 mol H2 per mole of phenol).

Reactant:

Reagent: Zn dust, heat

Product:

Reductive removal of the OH group (the classic way to convert phenol to benzene).

Reactant:

Reagent: H2 (3 equiv.), Ni, high temperature and pressure

Product:

Cyclohexanol; a precursor of cyclohexanone and adipic acid.

Key idea. Quinone formation needs two oxygens in a 1,2 or 1,4 relationship (or a single OH that can be oxidised to the para dione). Reduction of phenol with Zn is hydrogenolysis of C–O; with H2/Ni it is addition to the ring.
