2. Reagents: electrophiles, nucleophiles, leaving groups and solvents

Every polar reaction is a nucleophile (an electron-pair donor) meeting an electrophile (an electron-pair acceptor). In orbital terms the nucleophile supplies its highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the electrophile its lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO); section 4.6 develops this idea.

2.1 Electrophiles

  • Cations: H+, NO2+ (nitronium), NO+, carbocations R+, acylium ions RCO+, halonium and mercurinium ions, diazonium ions ArN2+.
  • Lewis acids: BF3, AlCl3, FeBr3, ZnCl2, SO3, Hg2+, Ag+.
  • Polarised neutral molecules: the carbon of a carbonyl, nitrile, alkyl halide or epoxide; the proton of HX; the halogen atoms of a polarised X–Y bond.
  • Electron-deficient neutral species: carbenes (:CR2), nitrenes (:NR) and arynes, which have low-lying empty orbitals.

2.2 Nucleophiles

  • Anions: HO−, RO−, CN−, halide ions, RS−, N3−, hydride (from LiAlH4 or NaBH4), carbanions in RMgX and RLi, and enolates.
  • Neutral molecules with a lone pair: H2O, ROH, NH3, amines, phosphines, sulfides, pyridine.
  • π systems: alkenes, alkynes, arenes, enols, enol ethers and enamines (nucleophilic at carbon).
Exam Trap A reagent that is an electrophile is not necessarily an oxidant, and a nucleophile is not necessarily a reductant: the two pairs of terms describe different things (electron-pair acceptance and donation, and electron transfer). A carbene is normally treated as an electrophile because of its empty p orbital, but singlet carbenes with strongly donating substituents (N-heterocyclic carbenes) are nucleophiles.

2.3 Nucleophilicity and basicity

A base attacks a proton and is measured by an equilibrium constant (the pKa of its conjugate acid). A nucleophile attacks an atom other than hydrogen, usually carbon, and is measured by a rate. The two scales run parallel when the attacking atoms are similar, and diverge in the cases below.

  • Charge. For the same attacking atom the anion is the stronger nucleophile: HO− > H2O, RO− > ROH, NH2− > NH3.
  • Across a period, at equal charge, nucleophilicity falls as electronegativity rises, because the lone pair is held more tightly: NH2− > HO− > F−; NH3 > H2O.
  • Down a group in a protic solvent, nucleophilicity rises although basicity falls: I− > Br− > Cl− > F−, and RS− > RO−. Small anions are tightly hydrogen-bonded by the solvent and must lose part of that solvation before they can attack; large, polarisable anions are less strongly solvated and are more polarisable. In polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF, acetonitrile, acetone) the anions are weakly solvated and the order follows basicity: F− > Cl− > Br− > I−.
  • Steric bulk lowers nucleophilicity far more than basicity. Potassium tert-butoxide, lithium diisopropylamide (LDA) and DBU are strong bases and poor nucleophiles; ethoxide is both.
  • Polarisability favours attack on soft electrophilic centres (sp3 carbon, the β-carbon of an enone): RS−, I− and R3P are especially effective.

The table shows that nucleophilicity towards sp3 carbon does not follow basicity: azide, phenoxide and bromide are equally nucleophilic although their conjugate acids differ in pKa by up to 15 units; triethylphosphine is about a hundred times more nucleophilic than the more basic triethylamine. Large, polarisable atoms (S, P, I) with high-lying HOMOs are the best nucleophiles towards carbon. Within a series where the attacking atom is the same (oxygen nucleophiles), nucleophilicity does follow basicity: CH3O− > CH3CO2− > NO3−. An additional effect, the α-effect, makes a nucleophile with an adjacent atom bearing a lone pair (HOO−, NH2NH2, NH2OH) more reactive than its basicity predicts.

Compare & Distinguish Basicity is thermodynamic: the position of equilibrium in proton transfer (pKa of the conjugate acid). Nucleophilicity is kinetic: the rate of attack on an atom other than hydrogen, usually carbon. They agree for similar attacking atoms and disagree when polarisability, solvation or size matter (azide, phenoxide and bromide have equal nucleophilic constants but their conjugate acids differ by about 15 pKa units).
Exception & Its Reason The order I− > Br− > Cl− > F− holds in protic solvents but reverses in polar aprotic solvents. Reason: in a protic solvent the small anion is held by hydrogen bonds and must pay a large desolvation cost before attacking; in an aprotic solvent there is no such cost and the order follows basicity.
MOT Lens A good nucleophile towards carbon has a high-lying HOMO that overlaps well with the σ* orbital of the C–X bond. Large atoms (S, I, P) have diffuse, high-energy lone pairs, which explains their high nucleophilic constants (C6H5S− 9.9, I− 7.4, Et3P 8.7) despite modest basicity.

2.4 Ambident nucleophiles

An ambident nucleophile has two nucleophilic atoms and can react at either. The enolate is the most important case: it can alkylate at carbon or at oxygen, and the outcome depends on the electrophile, the solvent and the counter-ion. The two sites differ in character: oxygen carries the larger share of the negative charge and is the hard nucleophilic site, while carbon has the larger coefficient in the HOMO and is the soft site. Soft electrophiles such as alkyl halides react at carbon; hard electrophiles such as alkyl sulfates and sulfonates, silyl chlorides and acyl chlorides tend to react at oxygen; ethereal solvents and lithium counter-ions favour carbon, polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF) and larger cations favour oxygen. Section 6.5 gives the details. Other ambident anions include phenoxide (O- or C-alkylation), cyanide (C or N) and thiocyanate (S or N); with alkyl halides cyanide gives nitriles by attack through carbon.

2.5 Leaving groups

A good leaving group departs as a stable, weakly basic anion, and the best guide is the pKa of its conjugate acid H–L: the lower the pKa, the better the leaving group.

The sequence I > Br > Cl ≫ F in SN1, SN2, E1 and E2 reflects both the acidity of H–X and the strength of the C–X bond (CH3–F 481, CH3–Cl 350, CH3–Br 302, CH3–I 241 kJ mol−1). In nucleophilic aromatic substitution the order is reversed (F > Cl ≈ Br > I), because the rate-determining step is the *addition* of the nucleophile, which the electronegative fluorine accelerates; the C–F bond is not broken in that step.

HYF To make a poor leaving group good, protonate it (ROH → ROH2+, which leaves as water), convert it to a sulfonate (ROH + TsCl, pyridine), or replace OH by halogen (PBr3, SOCl2). Epoxides open readily because relief of ring strain makes an alkoxide an acceptable leaving group.
Exception & Its Reason The order of leaving-group ability I > Br > Cl > F, which holds for SN1, SN2, E1 and E2, is reversed in nucleophilic aromatic substitution (F > Cl ≈ Br > I). Reason: the rate-determining step in SNAr is the attack of the nucleophile; the electronegative fluorine makes the attacked carbon more electrophilic and stabilises the anionic intermediate, and the C–F bond is not broken until the fast second step.
Experimental Connection The effect of the leaving group can be measured by comparing rates of solvolysis of a series R–X in one solvent; the sequence of rates follows the order of acidity of H–X and of the C–X bond strengths given in the table of section 2.5.

2.6 Solvent effects

Solvent classes
ClassExamplesEffect
Polar proticwater, methanol, ethanol, acetic acidStabilises cations by ion–dipole interaction and anions by hydrogen bonding; favours SN1 and E1; weakens anionic nucleophiles, small ones most
Polar aproticDMSO, DMF, acetonitrile, acetone, HMPASolvates cations well and anions poorly, leaving a reactive "naked" nucleophile; favours SN2 and E2
Non-polarhexane, benzene, carbon tetrachlorideSuited to radical and concerted reactions; dissolves ions poorly

The effect of a solvent on a rate depends on whether the transition state has more or less concentrated charge than the reactants. If charge is created (ionisation of an alkyl halide), a polar solvent lowers the barrier. If a concentrated charge on a small anion is dispersed in the transition state (SN2 of Nu− with a neutral substrate), a solvent that stabilises the anion strongly in the ground state raises the barrier, so the reaction is faster in a polar aprotic than in a protic solvent.

Example 3 Basic

Solvent and rate

Explain why an SN2 reaction of an anionic nucleophile with a primary alkyl bromide is much faster in DMF than in methanol, while the solvolysis of tert-butyl chloride is faster in methanol than in DMF.

Show solution
  1. In methanol the anion is hydrogen-bonded; part of that stabilisation is lost on the way to the transition state, which raises the barrier. DMF has no O–H or N–H bonds, so it cannot hydrogen-bond to the anion, and the nucleophile is more reactive.
  2. In the ionisation of t-BuCl the transition state is much more polar than the reactant: charge separation develops. Methanol stabilises the developing chloride ion by hydrogen bonding and the cation by ion–dipole interaction; DMF can stabilise only the cation.
  3. Polar protic solvents therefore assist reactions that create charge (SN1, E1); polar aprotic solvents assist reactions in which a localised anion reacts without being tied up by solvation (SN2, E2).
Compare & Distinguish Protic solvent: has O–H or N–H bonds; stabilises anions by hydrogen bonding and cations by ion–dipole interaction; favours SN1 and E1; weakens small anionic nucleophiles. Polar aprotic solvent: large dipole but no O–H or N–H; solvates cations well and anions poorly; favours SN2 and E2 of anionic nucleophiles.
Concept check. In which solvent, DMSO or methanol, does an anionic nucleophile react faster in an SN2 reaction, and why?
Show answer
DMSO: the anion is not hydrogen-bonded, so no desolvation penalty is paid on the way to the transition state.
