6. Carbanions

6.1 Structure and bonding

A carbanion is a species in which a carbon atom has three bonds and a lone pair, so it has eight valence electrons and a negative charge. It is electron-rich: a nucleophile and a base. The lone pair is the HOMO.

  • Simple alkyl carbanion (CH3−, R3C−): pyramidal, like ammonia, with the lone pair in an orbital of substantial s character (calculated H–C–H angle about 97 to 100° for the methyl and ethyl anions). A pyramidal geometry lowers the energy of the lone pair, and inversion at carbon is fast.
  • Conjugated carbanion (allyl, benzyl, enolate, nitronate, cyclopentadienyl): the carbon is sp2 and the lone pair is in a p orbital that overlaps the π system, so the system is planar.
  • Acetylide (RC≡C−): the lone pair is in an sp orbital (50 % s character), close to the nucleus.
Carbanion geometry follows hybridisation: sp³ pyramidal, sp² planar, sp linear

Free carbanions are rare. Alkyllithium compounds exist as aggregates (tetramers and hexamers) in which the carbanion carbon is bonded to several lithium atoms, and Grignard reagents are covalent, solvated molecules. The carbon–metal bond is polarised Cδ−–Mδ+, and the more electropositive the metal (K, Na, Li > Mg > Zn > Cu), the more carbanion-like and the more reactive the reagent. Organometallic compounds behave as carbanion equivalents. They are also much slower than their basicity suggests at removing protons from carbon, because the carbanion electrons are held in a compact cluster with the metal ions; this is why organolithium and Grignard reagents add to carbonyl groups faster than they deprotonate α-carbons.

MOT Lens The lone pair of a pyramidal carbanion sits in an orbital with substantial s character. A planar (sp2) carbanion places the pair in a p orbital, which is higher in energy unless the p orbital overlaps with a π system. This is the reason that conjugated carbanions are planar and simple alkyl carbanions are pyramidal: the pair is placed where its energy is lower.
Concept check. Why do organolithium and Grignard reagents add to a C=O group faster than they deprotonate an α-carbon?

6.3 Stability of carbanions

A carbanion is stabilised by anything that lowers the energy of the lone pair or disperses the negative charge: high s character, electronegative substituents, delocalisation onto an electronegative atom, and aromaticity. Alkyl groups, which donate electrons, destabilise a carbanion. The most direct measure of carbanion stability is the pKa of the C–H acid. The values depend on the solvent and the ion pairing, so a comparison is meaningful only on a single scale. The tables below use the DMSO scale (equilibrium measurements, little ion pairing) for functionalised and hydrocarbon acids, with values in other media labelled.

(a) Hybridisation

The more s character in the orbital that holds the lone pair, the lower the energy of the lone pair. The order is sp (alkynes, pKa about 25 in water, 26.5 for phenylacetylene in DMSO) < sp2 (alkenes, arenes: ethene and benzene both above 40) < sp3 (alkanes: methane about 48). Small-ring C–H bonds, which have extra s character, are more acidic than those of unstrained alkanes.

(b) Delocalisation onto an electronegative atom

Conjugation of the lone pair with C=O, NO2, C≡N or SO2 lowers the pKa by 20 to 30 units. From the DMSO data the effect of a single group follows the order NO2 > C=O (ketone) > CO2R ≈ SO2R ≈ CN > CONR2, and two such groups give a further large increase (pentane-2,4-dione 13.3, diethyl malonate 16.4). (c) Aromaticity

Cyclopentadiene (pKa 16.0 in water, 18.1 in DMSO) is about as acidic as an alcohol because removing a proton from its sp3 CH2 gives the cyclopentadienyl anion, a planar ring with 6 π electrons. The Hückel comparison of ring and chain in section 4.6 shows the gain: the ring anion is more stable than the open pentadienyl anion by 1.0β. Indene (20.1) and fluorene (22.6) are also unusually acidic hydrocarbons for the same reason; the very large difference between fluorene and the analogous non-aromatic dibenzocycloheptatriene is attributed to the aromatic cyclopentadienide ring. Cycloheptatriene, by contrast, is barely more acidic than propene (39 against 43), because loss of a proton would give a planar seven-membered ring with 8 π electrons, which is antiaromatic; it loses hydride instead to give the aromatic tropylium cation.

Aromatic stabilisation of the anion makes the first three acids unusually strong; the 4π and 8π rings are antiaromatic
(d) Polarisability and substituent effects

Carbanions next to sulfur are stabilised by the polarisability of the large atom (for example 1,3-dithiane anions and sulfone-stabilised carbanions; CH3SO2Ph is 29.0 in DMSO). Phosphonium and sulfonium ylides (Ph3P+–CH2−) are carbanions adjacent to a positive atom and are the nucleophiles in the Wittig and related reactions. Electronegative substituents (F, OH, NH2) on the carbanion carbon stabilise by polar effects, though the effect is smaller than that of π acceptors. Alkyl groups destabilise: methane (48) is more acidic than ethane (about 51).

Compare & Distinguish Carbanion stability is increased by electron-withdrawing and conjugating groups and by s character; carbocation stability is increased by electron-donating and conjugating groups. A substituent that stabilises one usually destabilises the other (alkyl groups: cation yes, anion no), except conjugation (phenyl, vinyl), which stabilises both because delocalisation helps whichever charge is present.
Exception & Its Reason The order of acidity of the same two compounds can reverse between solvents: nitromethane is more acidic than diethyl malonate in water but less acidic in DMSO. Reason: the localised nitronate is strongly stabilised by hydrogen bonding in water, which is absent in DMSO.
MOT Lens Allyl and enolate. The allyl anion has four π electrons in ψ1 and ψ2; ψ2 is nonbonding with density on the terminal carbons only. In the enolate one terminal carbon is replaced by oxygen: both orbitals drop in energy and ψ1 becomes oxygen-like, so the charge is larger on oxygen, while the HOMO ψ2 remains larger on carbon. Reactions controlled by charge occur at oxygen, those controlled by the HOMO at carbon.
Special Case Cycloheptatriene. The loss of a proton gives an 8-π-electron planar ring, antiaromatic; therefore cycloheptatriene (pKa 39) is barely more acidic than propene (43) and prefers to lose hydride (to give the aromatic tropylium cation).

Example 18 Adv

An unusually acidic hydrocarbon

Explain why cyclopentadiene (pKa 18.1 in DMSO) is far more acidic than propene (about 38 in DMF) and why cycloheptatriene (39 in water) is hardly more acidic than propene.

Show solution
  1. Deprotonation of cyclopentadiene gives a planar ring of five p orbitals with 6 π electrons (4n + 2 with n = 1): an aromatic anion in which the charge is shared equally by five carbons. The Hückel comparison shows the ring anion is 1.0β more stable than the open-chain pentadienyl anion.
  2. Removing a proton from cycloheptatriene would give a planar anion with seven p orbitals and 8 π electrons (4n with n = 2), which is antiaromatic if planar, so no aromatic bonus is available and the acidity is that of a doubly allylic C–H.
  3. Cycloheptatriene loses hydride to a hydride acceptor instead, giving the 6 π tropylium cation.

The Grignard reagent reacts with carbonyl compounds as follows.

  • RMgX + R′CHO (formaldehyde gives a primary alcohol with one more carbon; other aldehydes give secondary alcohols) → alkoxide → alcohol after work-up.
  • RMgX + R′2C=O gives a tertiary alcohol.

6.5 Enolates

The most important carbanions in synthesis are enolates, formed by removing an α-hydrogen from an aldehyde, ketone, ester, nitrile or nitro compound. In orbital terms the enolate is an allyl-anion-like system in which one terminal carbon is replaced by oxygen. Oxygen, being more electronegative, lowers both occupied π orbitals and makes the lower one (ψ1) more oxygen-like and the upper one (ψ2, the HOMO) larger on carbon. The negative charge is greater on oxygen, but the HOMO has the larger coefficient on carbon. Reactions controlled by charge therefore occur at oxygen and those controlled by orbital overlap occur at carbon.

Why It Happens Hindered strong bases such as LDA are sensitive to the steric environment of the α-hydrogens and deprotonate the more accessible one; because the amide is a much stronger base than the enolate, the enolate cannot regain the proton and the less stable enolate is trapped (kinetic control). With a weaker base or a protic solvent the enolates exchange protons and the more substituted (more stable) enolate predominates.
6.5.1 Kinetic and thermodynamic enolates

An unsymmetrical ketone can give two enolates. For 2-methylcyclohexanone:

LDA, THF, −78 °C⟶
Kinetic enolate: the proton on the less hindered carbon is removed faster; the base is strong enough that the reaction is irreversible
weaker base or protic conditions, 25 °C⟶
Thermodynamic enolate: protons are transferred reversibly between the isomers, and the more substituted enolate predominates
  • Kinetic control: a strong, hindered base in an aprotic solvent at low temperature removes the more accessible α-proton faster and irreversibly, giving the less substituted enolate. In 3-methylbutan-2-one this is the enolate toward the methyl group.
  • Thermodynamic control: with a weaker base (comparable in basicity to the enolate), in a protic solvent or at higher temperature, protons are exchanged reversibly and the more stable, more substituted enolate predominates, just as more substituted alkenes are more stable.

Methylation of the two enolates gives 2,6-dimethylcyclohexanone (kinetic) and 2,2-dimethylcyclohexanone (thermodynamic).

6.5.2 Reactions of enolates
  • Alkylation (SN2). Enolates react with methyl, primary, allylic and benzylic halides; secondary and tertiary halides give elimination. The malonic ester and acetoacetic ester syntheses use this reaction followed by hydrolysis and decarboxylation.
  • Aldol addition and condensation. An enolate adds to the C=O of a second carbonyl compound to give a β-hydroxy carbonyl compound; loss of water gives the conjugated enone.
  • Claisen condensation. An ester enolate acylates a second ester to give a β-keto ester. A full equivalent of base is needed, because the product is deprotonated (pKa 14.2 for ethyl acetoacetate in DMSO), and this irreversible step drives the reaction.
  • Michael addition. An enolate adds to the β-carbon of an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound.
  • Halogenation. In base, halogenation of a methyl ketone is not stopped at the first halogen, because each halogen makes the remaining α-hydrogens more acidic. The trihalomethyl ketone is then cleaved (haloform reaction).

6.5.3 E1cB elimination

When the β-carbon carries an acidic hydrogen (next to C=O, NO2, CN) and the leaving group is poor (HO−, AcO−), a base first removes the acidic proton to give a carbanion, and the leaving group then departs. The rate depends on which step is slow. This is the mechanism of the dehydration of aldols.

HO⁻⟶ −HO⁻⟶
E1cB: enolate first, then loss of hydroxide to give the conjugated enone

Evidence for the carbanion pathway is H/D exchange of the β-carbon hydrogen faster than elimination. In an E2 reaction there is no intermediate; leaving group departure and proton removal are simultaneous, and the E2 transition state becomes more E1cB-like as the leaving group becomes poorer or the β-proton more acidic. Positively charged leaving groups (trimethylammonium, dimethylsulfonium) also push the transition state towards E1cB because their inductive effect acidifies the β-protons.

Special Case Non-enolisable aldehydes. Benzaldehyde has no α-hydrogen, so with concentrated hydroxide it cannot form an enolate; the hydrate anion transfers hydride (Cannizzaro reaction). An aldehyde that has an α-hydrogen undergoes aldol reaction instead, since enolate formation is faster than hydride transfer.

6.6 Carbanion rearrangements and related reactions

1,2-Shifts of hydrogen or alkyl groups to a carbanion are very rare: a migrating group would have to move to an electron-rich carbon. The following reactions involve anionic intermediates or anionic migration, and each is a favourite of problem sets.

  • Favorskii rearrangement. An α-halo ketone with an enolisable α′-position reacts with alkoxide or hydroxide to give an acid derivative with a rearranged skeleton. For cyclic ketones the product is a ring-contracted ester. The enolate formed at the α′-carbon displaces halide intramolecularly to give a cyclopropanone; the alkoxide adds to the carbonyl group and the three-membered ring opens to give the more stable carbanion, which is protonated.

  • Cannizzaro reaction. A non-enolisable aldehyde with concentrated base: hydroxide adds to C=O, and the resulting alkoxide transfers hydride to a second molecule of aldehyde. The products are an alcohol and a carboxylate. With formaldehyde as one partner (crossed Cannizzaro) formaldehyde is the reductant and is oxidised to formate.
  • Decarboxylation. β-Keto acids and malonic acids lose CO2 on heating through a cyclic six-membered transition state that gives an enol directly; trihaloacetates lose CO2 to give a trihalomethyl carbanion.
Counterexample "A chiral carbanion always racemises." In the cleavage of a chiral ketone to 2-phenylbutane the hydrocarbon shows 93 % net retention in benzene with a tight ion pair, 48 % net inversion in ethylene glycol and racemisation in DMSO. The stereochemistry depends on the lifetime of the ion pair and the availability of a proton donor.

6.7 Stereochemistry of carbanions

Experiments on the cleavage of a chiral ketone to give 2-phenylbutane show how the stereochemistry depends on the medium. With potassium t-butoxide in benzene the hydrocarbon formed has 93 % net retention of configuration; with potassium hydroxide in ethylene glycol it has 48 % net inversion; in DMSO it is racemic. A tight ion pair protonated from the side where the proton donor is delivered gives retention; a good proton source in the solvent gives inversion; and a solvent that dissociates the ion pair lets the carbanion become symmetrically solvated before protonation, which gives racemisation. A chiral carbanion therefore does not always racemise, and the outcome has to be judged from the conditions.

  • Racemisation of a chiral ketone at the α-carbon in base shows that the enolate is formed. The rate of racemisation and the rate of H/D exchange in D2O/NaOD are comparable, and exchange at all α-hydrogens of acetone gives CD3COCD3.
  • Internal return can hide a deprotonation: the ion pair formed on removal of the proton may collapse back to the starting material before exchange can occur. This is why exchange rates are used cautiously as measures of acidity.
  • A carbonyl compound whose stereocentre has no hydrogen on the α-carbon cannot racemise through an enolate.

6.8 Fates of carbanions

  • Protonation, the commonest reaction: acidity, H/D exchange, aqueous work-up of organometallic reagents.
  • Addition to C=O, C≡N or C=C–C=O: aldol, Grignard, Michael and Claisen reactions form new carbon–carbon bonds.
  • SN2 alkylation: enolates and acetylides with primary halides, cuprates with alkyl halides.
  • β-Elimination (E1cB) and α-elimination to a carbene.
  • Expulsion of a leaving group from the adjacent atom: Favorskii, haloform cleavage, retro-aldol.
  • Electron transfer to an oxidant to give a radical.
