14. Fused polycyclic arenes: naphthalene, anthracene, phenanthrene

Fusing benzene rings along a shared C–C bond gives a family in which each ring is less aromatic than benzene. This lower aromatic stabilisation per ring explains why these hydrocarbons are more easily oxidised, reduced and added to than benzene, and why their substitution patterns are not uniform.

Another way to see the same thing: naphthalene is stabilised by 255 kJ mol-1, but two isolated benzene rings would have 300; the "cost" of fusion is 45 kJ mol-1. When one ring of naphthalene reacts and loses its aromaticity, the other ring still keeps its full sextet, so the net loss is only about 255 − 150 = 105 kJ mol-1, against 150 for benzene.

14.1 Naphthalene: structure and numbering

naphthalene (positions 1–8)
Positions 1, 4, 5, 8 are α (peri-position pairs 1/8 and 4/5); positions 2, 3, 6, 7 are β. C4a and C8a are the fusion carbons (no H).
  • Three Kekulé structures: C1–C2 is double in two of three, so the C1–C2 bond order is 5/3 (bond length 136 pm); C2–C3 is double in only one of three (bond order 4/3, 142 pm). The bonds are not all equal, in contrast to benzene.
  • Naphthalene has only two types of C–H (α and β) so a monosubstituted derivative has 2 isomers (1- and 2-); there are 10 disubstituted derivatives with two identical groups.
  • Physical: white crystalline solid, m.p. 353 K, sublimes (moth balls), insoluble in water; dipole moment zero; burns with a sooty flame.

14.2 Electrophilic substitution of naphthalene

Naphthalene is more reactive than benzene (several hundred times in nitration; bromination needs no catalyst) and gives the α (1-) product under kinetic control. The reason is the stability of the arenium ion:

α-attack, charge at C2 (benzene ring intact) α-attack, charge at C4 (benzene ring intact) β-attack, charge at C1 (benzene ring intact)
Contributors that keep a complete benzene ring ("benzenoid") are by far the most stable. α-attack has two of these; β-attack has only one. (Other contributors place the charge in the second ring and disturb its aromaticity.)
Key idea α-Attack at naphthalene gives an arenium ion with two benzenoid contributors; β-attack gives one. The α-intermediate is lower in energy, so α-substitution is faster (kinetic control). The 1-substituted products dominate in nitration, bromination, chlorination and low-temperature sulphonation.

Example 30 Adv

Why does 2-naphthalenesulphonic acid form at high temperature?

Explain, with a reaction-energy picture, why sulphonation of naphthalene at 433 K gives mainly the β-acid although α-attack is faster.

Show solution
  1. The α-sulphonic acid forms faster (lower activation energy), but its SO3H group is close to the hydrogen at C8 (peri interaction, steric strain), so the 1-acid is less stable than the 2-acid.
  2. Sulphonation is reversible: at 433 K, the 1-acid re-forms the arenium ion/desulphonates quickly and the system equilibrates to the more stable 2-acid.
  3. At 353 K the reverse reaction is slow and the kinetic (1-) product is isolated. This is the classic example of kinetic vs thermodynamic control in aromatic substitution.

14.4 Addition, reduction, oxidation of naphthalene

  • Reduction: Na/EtOH (or Na/NH3(l)) → 1,4-dihydronaphthalene; Na/amyl alcohol, Δ → tetralin; H2/Ni at 450 K, 30 atm → tetralin and then decalin (cis- and trans-decahydronaphthalene).
  • Oxidation: CrO3/AcOH → 1,4-naphthoquinone; V2O5/O2 → phthalic anhydride; hot KMnO4 → phthalic acid. The more electron-rich ring is oxidised: 1-nitronaphthalene → 3-nitrophthalic acid (the nitro-bearing ring survives); 1-naphthylamine → phthalic acid (the amino ring is destroyed). This pair of experiments proved the positions of groups in naphthalene derivatives.
  • Addition: chlorine adds slowly to give 1,2,3,4-tetrachloro-1,2,3,4-tetrahydronaphthalene, a result of the lower aromaticity per ring.

Example 31 Adv

Structure from oxidation

A nitronaphthalene A gives 3-nitrophthalic acid on oxidation; an aminonaphthalene B gives phthalic acid. Identify the ring positions, and explain.

Show solution
  1. The substituted ring in A has to survive, so the ring that is oxidised is the unsubstituted ring: the nitro group strongly deactivates its own ring and the other ring is the more electron-rich one, which is cleaved. 3-Nitrophthalic acid has COOH groups at positions 1,2 and NO2 at 3, adjacent to one COOH, so the nitro group must have been at the α-position (1-): A = 1-nitronaphthalene. (A 2-nitronaphthalene would give 4-nitrophthalic acid.)
  2. In B, the donor NH2 makes its ring the more electron-rich one: the aminated ring is oxidised, leaving phthalic acid from the other ring: B = 1- or 2-naphthylamine (both give phthalic acid).

14.5 Anthracene and phenanthrene

anthracene (C9, C10 = meso positions) phenanthrene (C9, C10 = "K-region")
Linear (anthracene) and angular (phenanthrene) tricyclic arenes, both C₁₄H₁₀, both with 14 π electrons
  • Anthracene reacts at C9 and C10 because attack there leaves two intact benzene rings (the arenium ion has two benzenoid rings with the charge in the middle ring). The cost of reaction is only about 350 − 2 × 150 = 50 kJ mol-1 of aromatic stabilisation, versus 150 kJ for benzene.
  • Reactions of anthracene: Diels–Alder with maleic anhydride (as a diene across C9/C10); Br2 adds to C9–C10 and eliminates HBr to give 9-bromoanthracene; reduction (Na/EtOH or H2) gives 9,10-dihydroanthracene; oxidation (CrO3/AcOH or Na2Cr2O7) gives anthraquinone; photodimerises ([4+4]) in solution.
  • Phenanthrene is attacked at C9–C10, which behaves like an isolated double bond (bond order about 1.8: double in four of five Kekulé structures). Br2 adds at 9,10 (then HBr is lost to give 9-bromophenanthrene); OsO4 gives the 9,10-diol; CrO3 gives phenanthrene-9,10-quinone; ozone cleaves C9=C10 to give diphenaldehyde; more vigorous oxidation gives diphenic acid (biphenyl-2,2′-dicarboxylic acid).
  • Number of monosubstituted isomers: naphthalene 2, anthracene 3, phenanthrene 5.

Example 32 Adv

Comparing the three

(a) Which of benzene, naphthalene, anthracene reacts fastest with Br2 (no catalyst)? (b) Why does anthracene add maleic anhydride but naphthalene does not (readily)? (c) How many dibromo-derivatives of anthracene exist?

Show solution
  1. (a) Anthracene > naphthalene > benzene (loss of aromatic stabilisation in the first step: 150 > 105 > 50 kJ mol-1, roughly).
  2. (b) Anthracene has a middle ring whose C9, C10 carbons can behave as the ends of a diene; reaction there leaves two complete benzene rings, losing only ≈ 50 kJ mol-1. In naphthalene the diene would be inside a ring that is more heavily stabilised (loses ≈ 105 kJ mol-1), and the adduct is not favoured.
  3. (c) Positions 1, 2, 9 (and equivalents): dibromoanthracenes number 15 (all positional isomers with identical substituents).

14.6 Haworth synthesis and other polycyclic aromatics

The Haworth synthesis (section 9.7) builds naphthalene and phenanthrene from benzene or naphthalene + succinic anhydride through acylation, Clemmensen reduction, cyclisation and aromatisation (Pd/C or Se, Δ). Biphenyl has two benzene rings joined by a single bond; the rings are twisted (≈ 44° in the gas phase) and EAS occurs at 2- and 4-positions (the second ring is an o/p donor via conjugation, as with a vinyl or phenyl group). Fluorene, with a CH2 bridge, is a carbon acid with pKa ≈ 22.6 because the fluorenyl anion has 14 π electrons in a planar, fully conjugated, aromatic system. Azulene (C10H8, blue) is a non-benzenoid isomer of naphthalene with a 5-membered ring (6π) and a 7-membered ring (6π), dipole 1.0 D, and EAS at C1 and C3 of the 5-ring.
