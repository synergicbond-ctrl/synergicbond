7.2 Intermolecular dehydration (to ethers)

Excess primary alcohol with conc. H2SO4 at 413 K (140 °C): 2C2H5OH → C2H5OC2H5 + H2O. Mechanism: one molecule is protonated; a second (unprotonated) molecule acts as nucleophile in an SN2 reaction on the protonated alcohol; deprotonation of the oxonium ion gives the ether. Only suitable for primary alcohols (secondary and tertiary alcohols eliminate under these conditions). Mixed ethers are poor: a 1:1 mixture of two alcohols gives all three ethers (R2O, R′2O, ROR′).

Role of temperature. Substitution (ether) has the lower activation energy but a smaller entropy gain; elimination has higher activation energy but is favoured by high temperature (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, more molecules formed). The same principle applies to SN2/E2 competition in haloalkanes.

Example 17 L2 Main

Products of dehydration

Predict the major alkene from the dehydration of: (a) 2-methylcyclohexanol, (b) 2,2-dimethylcyclohexanol, (c) 1-phenylpropan-2-ol, (d) 3-methylpentan-3-ol.

Show solution
  1. (a) E1: secondary cation at C-1; β-H on C-2 (tertiary C–H) gives the trisubstituted alkene 1-methylcyclohexene (Zaitsev).
  2. (b) The 2° cation at C-1 sits next to the quaternary C-2. A methyl shift from C-2 to C-1 gives a 3° cation at C-2 (C-1 is now CH(CH3)). Loss of H+ from C-1 gives the tetrasubstituted 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene (major); loss from C-3 gives 1,6-dimethylcyclohexene (trisubstituted, minor).
  3. (c) CH3CH(OH)CH2C6H5: loss of H from C-1 (CH2 adjacent to Ph) gives the conjugated alkene 1-phenylpropene C6H5CH=CHCH3, stabilised by conjugation with the ring (trans); it beats the non-conjugated 3-phenylpropene.
  4. (d) 3° cation (C2H5)2C+CH3: loss of H from a CH2 gives 3-methylpent-2-ene (trisubstituted; E major) vs loss from CH3 gives 2-ethylbut-1-ene (disubstituted minor). Major: 3-methylpent-2-ene (mainly E).
Example 18 L3 Advanced

Carbocation rearrangement puzzle

An optically active alcohol C6H14O (A) gives, with conc. H2SO4 on heating, an alkene C6H12 (B) as the only major product. B on ozonolysis (Zn/H2O) gives only propanone. Identify A and B, and explain why A can be optically active though B is not.

Show solution
  1. B gives only propanone on ozonolysis, so B is (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2, 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene (symmetrical: two molecules of propanone).
  2. A must be an alcohol that gives this alkene via a cation, i.e. via the 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-yl cation. A chiral alcohol C6H14O that rearranges to this cation: 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol, (CH3)3C–CH(OH)CH3, which has one stereocentre (C-2 with H, OH, CH3, tert-butyl). Methyl shift gives the 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-yl cation; loss of H+ gives B.
  3. B is symmetrical and planar at the double bond; the stereocentre of A is destroyed when the planar cation forms, so the product is achiral and carries no memory of the chirality of A.
Example 19 L2 Main

Ether or alkene?

Ethanol and conc. H2SO4 are heated in different conditions. (i) 413 K with excess ethanol; (ii) 443 K with excess acid. Name the product and state the type of mechanism for each.

Show solution
  1. (i) Diethyl ether: SN2 between protonated ethanol (the electrophile, water leaves) and neutral ethanol (the nucleophile).
  2. (ii) Ethene: E2-like elimination from protonated ethanol (primary cation not formed).
  3. At the same temperature, using excess alcohol favours the bimolecular substitution; excess acid favours unimolecular and elimination paths.
Example 20 L1 Foundation

Rate of dehydration

Arrange in increasing ease of dehydration with conc. H2SO4: ethanol, propan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol.

Show solution
  1. The rate follows carbocation stability (formation of the cation from the protonated alcohol): 3° > 2° > 1°.
  2. Order of increasing ease: ethanol < propan-2-ol < 2-methylpropan-2-ol.
