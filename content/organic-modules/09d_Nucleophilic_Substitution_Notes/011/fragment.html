12. Substitution as a synthetic tool: named reactions and limits

Nucleophilic substitution lets a halide be converted into a wide variety of functional groups, but each method has restrictions imposed by the SN2 mechanism: use methyl and primary halides, avoid tertiary halides, and expect elimination with hindered or strongly basic reagents.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaI, dry acetone (Finkelstein)

Product:

Sodium chloride and bromide are insoluble in acetone and precipitate, driving the equilibrium to the iodide. The Swarts reaction converts RCl or RBr into RF with AgF, Hg2F2 or SbF3.

Reactant:

Reagent: Williamson synthesis

Product:

The hindered alkoxide is paired with the unhindered methyl iodide. The reverse choice (t-BuBr + MeO–) gives isobutene by E2.

Reactant:

Reagent: Gabriel synthesis: 1. potassium phthalimide; 2. N2H4 (or hydrolysis)

Product:

The N-alkylphthalimide releases a pure primary amine, EtNH2; no over-alkylation occurs. It works best with methyl and primary halides, poorly with secondary halides, and fails with tertiary and aryl halides.

  • Cyanide: RX → RCN (one more carbon), then RCOOH or RCH2NH2; primary and methyl halides only.
  • Azide: RX → RN3, then LiAlH4 or H2/Pd gives RNH2 with no polyalkylation.
  • Thiols: RX with NaSH gives RSH but the thiolate formed reacts again to give sulfides; use thiourea followed by hydrolysis to avoid this.
  • Williamson: primary alkyl halide + alkoxide; the alkoxide may be tertiary but the halide may not be.
Key idea. In planning a synthesis choose the combination in which the carbon electrophile is methyl or primary and the nucleophile may be as bulky as required.
