13. Addition and reduction reactions of the ring

Benzene resists addition because that costs the 150 kJ mol-1 of aromatic stabilisation; but under forcing conditions, or with dissolving metals, the ring is reduced.

13.1 Catalytic hydrogenation

Benzene + 3 H2 → cyclohexane at 450–470 K and elevated pressure over Ni (or at ≈ 300 K and 1–3 atm over Pt, Rh/C or Ru). The reaction cannot be stopped at cyclohexene or cyclohexadiene (these are hydrogenated faster). Substituents: C=C, C≡C, C=O and NO2 are reduced before the ring.

13.2 Birch reduction

Na or Li in liquid NH3 (≈ 240 K) with an alcohol (EtOH or t-BuOH) as the proton source reduces benzene to cyclohexa-1,4-diene. Mechanism: electron transfer to the arene gives a radical anion (blue colour of solvated electrons is consumed); protonation by ROH gives a cyclohexadienyl radical; a second electron gives a cyclohexadienyl anion; second protonation gives the 1,4-diene. The two sp3 carbons end up para to each other (1,4) because that arrangement keeps the two double bonds isolated and gives the more stable (less charge-repulsive) intermediate.

Example 29 Adv

Birch routes

(a) Convert anisole to cyclohex-2-enone. (b) Give the Birch product of p-methylanisole and of naphthalene. (c) Why does Birch reduction not reduce isolated alkenes?

Show solution
  1. (a) Birch gives 1-methoxycyclohexa-1,4-diene; mild acid (H3O+, 298 K) hydrolyses the enol ether to cyclohex-3-enone, and stronger acid isomerises it into conjugation: cyclohex-2-enone.
  2. (b) p-Methylanisole: both groups donors; reduction leaves them on sp2: 1-methoxy-4-methylcyclohexa-1,4-diene. Naphthalene (Na/NH3(l), or Na/EtOH): 1,4-dihydronaphthalene (further reduction to tetralin types with excess Na).
  3. (c) An isolated C=C has no low-lying π* able to accept an electron from Na in NH3; arenes (and conjugated systems) have lower-lying π* orbitals. So Birch conditions leave simple alkenes untouched (and permit the reduction of the ring in the presence of an isolated C=C).

13.3 Oxidative ring reactions

  • Benzene → maleic anhydride: O2, V2O5, 720–770 K.
  • Naphthalene → phthalic anhydride: O2, V2O5, 640–670 K (industrial route to phthalates and dyes); KMnO4 or CrO3 can oxidise naphthalene to 1,4-naphthoquinone (CrO3/AcOH) or to phthalic acid (hot alkaline KMnO4).
  • Anthracene → anthraquinone (CrO3, AcOH or Na2Cr2O7/H2SO4): the middle ring is oxidised, a 10-π system survives as two benzene rings.
  • Phenol → p-benzoquinone (Na2Cr2O7, H2SO4); hydroquinone → quinone (mild; reversible redox couple used in the quinhydrone electrode).
