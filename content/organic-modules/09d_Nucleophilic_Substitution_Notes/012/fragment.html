13. Intramolecular substitution: rings, epoxides and lactones

When the nucleophile and the leaving group are in the same molecule, substitution forms a ring. These reactions are often fast because the nucleophilic end is held close to the electrophilic carbon, so the entropy cost of bringing the two groups together is much smaller than in an intermolecular reaction. As a general trend, five- and six-membered rings form readily (five often fastest), three-membered rings also close quickly, while four-membered rings and rings of seven or more atoms close more slowly. High dilution favours cyclisation over the competing intermolecular reaction.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOH, H2O (alkoxide formed, then intramolecular SN2)

Product:

A halohydrin gives an epoxide. For a cyclohexane the OH and Cl must be trans-diaxial (anti-periplanar) for the oxygen to reach the back of the C–Cl bond; trans-2-chlorocyclohexanol closes, while the cis isomer cannot.

Reactant:

Reagent: Warm in water (carboxylate attacks as nucleophile)

Product:

4-Bromobutanoate cyclises to the five-membered lactone γ-butyrolactone.

Reactant:

Reagent: Na2S

Product:

Second substitution is intramolecular: sulfide first displaces one bromide, then the thiolate closes the five-membered ring (tetrahydrothiophene).

Key idea. As a general trend, when an ω-halo alkoxide could form a 4-, 5-, 6- or 7-membered ring, the five-membered ring closes fastest. The same intramolecular attack, when a sulfur or nitrogen atom is two carbons from the leaving group, produces a cyclic onium ion (neighbouring-group participation).
