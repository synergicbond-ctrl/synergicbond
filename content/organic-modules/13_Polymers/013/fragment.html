9. Named addition polymers: monomer, repeat unit, conditions, properties, uses

Each entry below gives what a JEE problem needs: the monomer, the repeat unit, how it is made, and what it is used for. Structures: * marks the open bonds to the next unit.

Polypropene (PP), –[CH2–CH(CH3)]n–

propene repeat unit
Isotactic PP: Ziegler–Natta (TiCl4/AlEt3 on MgCl2).

Isotactic PP is highly crystalline (m.p. ≈ 165 °C), stiff, resists fatigue (living hinges in flip-top caps) and chemicals. Uses: ropes, carpets, bottle caps, crates, car battery cases, fibres, reusable food containers. Atactic PP is a soft, sticky by-product (adhesives).

Polystyrene (PS), –[CH2–CH(C6H5)]n–

styrene repeat unit
Radical (peroxide) bulk, suspension or emulsion polymerisation; also anionic. Radical PS is atactic and amorphous.

Clear, brittle, glassy thermoplastic (Tg ≈ 100 °C); the bulky, planar phenyl groups stiffen the chain and block packing. Uses: disposable cups and cutlery, CD cases, wall tiles, toys. Expanded polystyrene (thermocol): beads soaked in a volatile blowing agent (pentane) are steamed, so they expand into foam: packaging and insulation. Styrene ring is untouched by the polymerisation: no aromaticity is lost.

Polyvinyl chloride (PVC), –[CH2–CHCl]n–

vinyl chloride repeat unit
Free radical (suspension). Monomer made from ethene via 1,2-dichloroethane (ClCH₂CH₂Cl → CH₂=CHCl + HCl at 500 °C) or from ethyne + HCl (HgCl₂).

Rigid, hard, strong, flame-retardant (chlorine), but thermally unstable (loses HCl above about 100 °C, so heat stabilisers are added). Plasticisers (dioctyl phthalate) soften it into flexible PVC. Uses: rigid (unplasticised): pipes, window frames, credit cards, bottles; flexible: rainwear, floor covering, hoses, cable insulation, imitation leather.

Polytetrafluoroethene (PTFE, Teflon), –[CF2–CF2]n–

tetrafluoroethene repeat unit
Free-radical, aqueous (NH₄)₂S₂O₈ / persulphate, high pressure. TFE comes from CHClF₂ pyrolysis: 2 CHClF₂ → CF₂=CF₂ + 2 HCl at about 700 °C.

Very strong, inert C–F bonds and the sheath of F atoms around the C–C backbone make PTFE resistant to acids, bases and solvents; very low friction (non-stick); stable up to about 260 °C (m.p. ≈ 327 °C, but it does not flow, so it is sintered). Uses: non-stick cookware, gaskets, bearings, tapes for sealing plumbing threads. Discovered by Roy Plunkett (1938) in a gas cylinder that had polymerised spontaneously.

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Orlon, Acrilan), –[CH2–CH(CN)]n–

acrylonitrile repeat unit
Redox/persulphate radical polymerisation in water. Industrial monomer: SOHIO ammoxidation of propene (CH₃CH=CH₂ + NH₃ + 1.5 O₂ → CH₂=CHCN + 3 H₂O).

Strong dipole–dipole forces between adjacent C≡N groups give a stiff, high-melting fibre which decomposes before it melts. Resembles wool: artificial wool, blankets, sweaters, carpets. Heating PAN fibres at about 250 °C (cyclisation) and then 1000–1500 °C (carbonisation) gives carbon fibre.

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, Perspex, Plexiglas, Lucite), –[CH2–C(CH3)(COOCH3)]n–

methyl methacrylate repeat unit
Radical (peroxide or AIBN); bulk cast or suspension.

Transparent (more than glass), light, shatter-resistant, hard and weather-resistant: organic glass. Uses: aircraft windows, lenses, signs, dental plates, bone cement, aquariums. Heating above about 300 °C depolymerises it back to monomer (ceiling temperature is low because of crowding at the quaternary carbon), so it can be recycled by pyrolysis.

Polyvinyl acetate (PVAc) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)

vinyl acetate PVAc unit PVA unit
PVAc is made by radical polymerisation; PVA only by hydrolysis of PVAc.
Exam Trap PVA cannot be made from “vinyl alcohol”. CH2=CH–OH is an enol and tautomerises at once to acetaldehyde, CH3CHO. Polyvinyl alcohol is obtained by hydrolysing (or alcoholysing) polyvinyl acetate: –[CH2–CH(OCOCH3)]– + NaOH → –[CH2–CH(OH)]– + CH3COONa. PVAc: emulsion paints, wood and paper adhesive (white glue), chewing-gum base. PVA: water-soluble film, textile sizing, fibre (vinylon); reacts with butanal to give polyvinyl butyral for safety-glass interlayers.

Polyvinylidene chloride (Saran)

vinylidene chloride repeat unit
Usually copolymerised with vinyl chloride (and acrylonitrile) to be processable.

Saran: transparent, very impermeable to O2 and water vapour: food-wrap film.

Polyisobutylene and butyl rubber (cationic; see section 5.3). Polybutadiene (Li-catalysed or Ziegler–Natta, >90 % cis-1,4) for tyre treads and golf-ball cores.

ABS (acrylonitrile–butadiene–styrene): graft/blend with a polybutadiene rubber phase in an SAN (styrene–acrylonitrile) matrix; tough, glossy, impact-resistant. Uses: LEGO bricks, helmets, car interiors, keyboards.

Cyanoacrylate (superglue): CH2=C(CN)COOR polymerises in seconds by anionic mechanism on traces of moisture (section 5.4).

Formula Monomer → polymer summary: C2H4→PE; C3H6→PP; C2H3Cl→PVC; C2F4→PTFE; C3H3N→PAN; C8H8→PS; C5H8O2→PMMA; C4H6O2→PVAc
Example 8 Main

Read the polymer back to its monomer

Give the monomer of –[CH2–CH(OCOCH3)]n– and of –[CH2–CH(CN)]n– and of –[CF2–CF2]n–.

Show solution
  1. Rule: for an addition polymer with an all-carbon backbone, cut the backbone every two carbons and put the double bond between them.
  2. 1: CH2=CH–OCOCH3, vinyl acetate. 2: CH2=CH–CN, acrylonitrile. 3: CF2=CF2, tetrafluoroethene.
Example 9 Adv

Diene backbone rule

The repeat unit of a polymer is –[CH2–C(CH3)=CH–CH2]–. Find the monomer, and say how the C=C migrated.

Show solution
  1. Four backbone carbons with a double bond between C2 and C3 means 1,4-addition of a conjugated diene. Re-form the diene: put double bonds between C1=C2 and C3=C4: CH2=C(CH3)–CH=CH2: isoprene (2-methylbuta-1,3-diene).
  2. In 1,4-addition the two terminal carbons form the new σ bonds and the two π bonds of the diene collapse into one new π bond in the middle (C2=C3).
Exception & Its Reason PTFE is a thermoplastic but is not melt-processed like polythene. Its molar mass is extremely high and its chains are very stiff, so above 327 °C it only becomes a transparent gel of enormous viscosity that does not flow. It is therefore shaped by sintering (compacting the powder and heating below the point of decomposition).
Compare & Distinguish PVAc vs PVA. PVAc has acetate ester side groups: insoluble in water, a glue and paint binder. PVA has –OH side groups (made by hydrolysing PVAc): water-soluble, a film and fibre polymer. The difference is a single side-group transformation on the same carbon backbone.
Concept Check C11 Checkpoint

Reading a polymer back

Give the monomer of –[CH2–C(CH3)(COOCH3)]– and the conditions of its usual polymerisation.

Show answer

Methyl methacrylate, CH2=C(CH3)COOCH3; free-radical (peroxide or AIBN), bulk or suspension.

Concept Check C12 Checkpoint

The PVA puzzle

Why is poly(vinyl alcohol) made from vinyl acetate and not from vinyl alcohol?

Show answer

Vinyl alcohol is an enol that tautomerises immediately to acetaldehyde, so it cannot serve as a monomer. Hydrolysing the ester of PVAc gives the polyalcohol.
