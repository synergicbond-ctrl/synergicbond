4. Bond lengths, bond strengths and bond angles

4.1 What controls bond length

Bond length is the distance between the two nuclei at the minimum of the potential-energy curve. Five factors decide it, and almost every 'compare the bond lengths' question needs only the first three.

  • Bond order: triple < double < single (more shared electrons pull the nuclei closer).
  • s-character of the hybrid orbitals used: more s ⟹ shorter (Section 3.3).
  • Atomic size: a bond to a bigger atom is longer (C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I).
  • Resonance averages the bond orders of the bonds involved: a single bond that gains partial double-bond character is shortened; a double bond that loses some π-character is lengthened.
  • Steric and electrostatic strain can lengthen a bond, especially in crowded molecules.

Exam Trap Bond length and bond strength do not always move together. Normally a shorter bond is a stronger bond (C≡C > C=C > C–C in strength). But for the halogens, C–F is the shortest C–X bond and the strongest, yet F–F (158 kJ mol⁻¹) is the weakest of the X–X bonds because of lone-pair repulsion; and the order of X–X bond enthalpy is Cl–Cl (243) > Br–Br (193) > F–F (158) > I–I (151). Check the pair of atoms before you generalise.

Illustration 4.2 Adv

Bond lengths in resonance systems

Predict which is longer in each pair and give the reason: (a) C–Cl in CH₂=CH–Cl or CH₃CH₂Cl; (b) C–O in CH₃COO⁻ or in CH₃COOH (the single-bonded C–O); (c) C–N in CH₃NH₂ or in CH₃CONH₂.

  1. (a) Chloroethane is longer. In vinyl chloride the carbon is sp² (more s-character than sp³) and a lone pair of Cl is delocalised into the C=C (+M of Cl), giving the C–Cl bond some double-bond character. Both effects shorten the bond: 172 pm vs 177 pm.
  2. (b) The C–OH bond of acetic acid is longer (about 136 pm) than either C–O bond of the acetate ion (about 126 pm, bond order 1.5). The acid has one pure C=O (122 pm) and one pure C–OH; in the ion the two bonds are equalised.
  3. (c) The C–N of methylamine is longer (147 pm) because the lone pair of nitrogen in the amide is delocalised into the carbonyl and the amide C–N has double-bond character (133 pm).

4.2 Bond angles in molecules with lone pairs

Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) gives the first-order angle, and the following corrections account for the real values.

  • Repulsion order: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair. Each lone pair squeezes the bond angle: CH₄ 109.5°, NH₃ 107°, H₂O 104.5°.
  • Electronegative substituents pull the bonding electrons away from the central atom, reducing bond-pair–bond-pair repulsion, so the angle closes: NH₃ 107° > NF₃ 102°; H₂O 104.5° > OF₂ 103°.
  • Large substituents open the angle by steric repulsion: H₂O 104.5° < (CH₃)₂O 112° and Cl₂O 111°; NH₃ 107° < (CH₃)₃N 111°.
  • Central atoms from period 3 and below use almost pure p orbitals for bonding and keep the lone pair in an s-rich orbital (the electropositive/large central atom does not hybridise), giving angles near 90°: H₂O 104.5° ≫ H₂S 92°; NH₃ 107° ≫ PH₃ 93.5° ≫ AsH₃ 92°.
  • Delocalisation of the lone pair into an adjacent π-system or empty d orbital flattens the centre: N(SiH₃)₃ is planar (angle 120°), whereas N(CH₃)₃ is pyramidal; the Si–O–Si angle in disiloxanes (about 144°) is far wider than the C–O–C angle (112°).

IMP Bent's rule. A more electronegative substituent is placed in the hybrid orbital with more p-character, and the lone pair or an electropositive substituent takes the orbital with more s-character. Corollaries: the bond angle between two highly electronegative substituents is smaller than 109.5° (F–C–F in CH₂F₂ ≈ 108°); the H–C–H angle in CH₂F₂ is larger; the lone pair of NH₃ lies in an s-rich orbital; a C–H bond on a carbon that carries an electronegative group has more s-character and is stronger.

Illustration 4.4 Adv

Why is N(SiH₃)₃ planar and N(CH₃)₃ pyramidal?

Give two consequences of the difference for basicity and for the Si–O–Si angle.

  1. In trimethylamine the nitrogen lone pair has nowhere to go: N is sp³, the lone pair is localised and available, the molecule is pyramidal (C–N–C ≈ 111°).
  2. In trisilylamine silicon has empty 3d orbitals (and low-lying σ* Si–H orbitals) that overlap with the lone pair on N: p(N) → d(Si) back-bonding. The lone pair is delocalised, N becomes sp² and planar, and the compound is only a very weak Lewis base (it does not even form a stable adduct with BF₃ whereas trimethylamine does).
  3. The same effect is why the Si–O–Si angle in siloxanes (about 144°) is much wider than the C–O–C angle in ethers (112°): the oxygen lone pairs are partly delocalised into silicon.
