15. Steric inhibition of resonance, the ortho effect and intramolecular hydrogen bonding

15.1 Steric inhibition of resonance (SIR)

Resonance needs the p-orbitals of the donor/acceptor group and of the ring to be parallel. If bulky neighbours force the group to rotate out of the plane of the ring, the overlap falls (it varies as cos θ, so it is zero at θ = 90°) and the mesomeric effect is lost or reduced. Only the inductive effect (which does not depend on geometry) remains. This is steric inhibition of resonance.

IMP SIR turns a +M or −M group into a purely inductive one. It therefore: raises the basicity of NR₂-substituted aromatics (the lone pair is no longer delocalised), lowers the acidity of phenols bearing a −M group (no delocalisation of the negative charge), raises the acidity of benzoic acids (the carboxyl group is no longer conjugated with the ring), and reduces the dipole moment of push–pull compounds.

Basicity of N,N-dimethylanilines

Arrange in increasing basicity: aniline, N,N-dimethylaniline, N,N,2,6-tetramethylaniline. Explain the position of the last member.

  1. In N,N-dimethylaniline the NMe₂ group conjugates with the ring, but +I of the two methyls raises basicity a little above aniline.
  2. In N,N,2,6-tetramethylaniline two ortho methyl groups force the NMe₂ group perpendicular to the ring: the lone pair cannot overlap with the ring π-system and is as available as in an aliphatic tertiary amine (steric inhibition of resonance). It is the strongest of the three.
  3. aniline < N,N-dimethylaniline < N,N,2,6-tetramethylaniline.

Illustration 15.3 Adv

Direction of the dipole and SIR

Why does 4-nitro-N,N-dimethylaniline have a much larger dipole moment than 2,6-dimethyl-4-nitro-N,N-dimethylaniline?

  1. In the planar molecule the +M of NMe₂ and the −M of NO₂ operate along the same axis through the ring (push–pull), giving a contribution of the charge-separated quinoid structure; the resulting extra charge separation increases μ.
  2. In the 2,6-dimethyl compound the two ortho methyl groups twist NMe₂ out of the ring plane, so its lone pair can no longer push through the ring; the push–pull contribution disappears and μ falls to the value expected from the σ-polarisation of the two groups alone. (Twisting the nitro group instead, with methyls ortho to NO₂, cuts the pull side and has the same kind of effect.)

15.2 The ortho effect in benzoic acids

Almost every ortho substituent — whether electron-donating or withdrawing — makes benzoic acid a stronger acid than expected from its electronic character, and usually stronger than benzoic acid itself.

IMP Explanation (JEE style). An ortho substituent twists the –COOH group out of the plane of the ring. This inhibits resonance between COOH and the ring — the ring can no longer donate electrons into the carbonyl by resonance. The carboxyl group becomes more electron-poor, its O–H bond is polarised more strongly, and the carboxylate anion is more stable relative to the acid. In addition, the ortho group exerts a −I effect that is strongest at this shortest distance, and (for OH, NH₂, NO₂) hydrogen-bonding or field interactions with the carboxylate.
Exam Trap 'All ortho-substituted benzoic acids are stronger than benzoic acid' is a good working rule for competitive exams; however, a few exceptions exist in the data (o-aminobenzoic acid, 4.98 in some sources). If a question lists o-NH₂ with a numeric pKₐ, use the number.
Illustration 15.4 Main

Ortho-toluic acid

The pKₐ of o-toluic acid is 3.91, m-toluic 4.27, p-toluic 4.37 and benzoic acid 4.20. Explain why o-toluic acid is stronger than benzoic acid although CH₃ is an electron-donating group.

  1. The methyl group at ortho forces COOH out of the plane (steric inhibition of resonance), which makes the acid stronger (ortho effect).
  2. The +I effect of methyl, which would decrease the acidity (as seen in the meta and para isomers, where there is no ortho effect), is outweighed.

Explain why the pKₐ of 2,6-dimethylbenzoic acid (3.25) is lower than that of o-toluic acid (3.91) and why 4-methyl-substitution has the opposite effect.

  1. Two ortho groups twist the COOH group by almost 90°: resonance with the ring is completely inhibited, so the effect is bigger than with one ortho methyl.
  2. A para methyl group has no steric interaction with COOH; it only donates electrons (+I, hyperconjugation) and weakens the acid (4.37 vs 4.20).

15.3 The ortho effect in anilines, phenols and pyridines

  • Anilines: every ortho substituent decreases basicity (usually more than the same substituent at para or meta, even for CH₃): o-toluidine 4.44 < aniline 4.60 although p-toluidine is stronger (5.08). The cause is steric hindrance to solvation of the NH₃⁺ group (and to the approach of H⁺) and the loss of coplanarity in the cation.
  • Phenols: o-alkyl phenols are slightly weaker than phenol (cresols 10.1–10.3); an ortho nitro or halogen is stronger by −I and −M. o-Nitrophenol (7.23) is not stronger than p-nitrophenol (7.15) because of intramolecular hydrogen bonding in the un-ionised molecule.
  • Pyridines: 2-substituents hinder N-protonation and N-alkylation: 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine (3.58) cannot even be solvated normally and does not react with BF₃ (it is a hindered, non-nucleophilic base used to trap protons).
Illustration 15.7 Adv

o-, m-, p-toluidine

The pKₐH of o-, m-, p-toluidine are 4.44, 4.71 and 5.08 and aniline 4.60. Explain the order and why the ortho isomer is the weakest.

  1. p-Toluidine (5.08): +I and hyperconjugation of CH₃ at para, in conjugation with NH₂, raise electron density on N.
  2. m-Toluidine (4.71): only +I at meta (weaker, no hyperconjugation toward N).
  3. Aniline (4.60).
  4. o-Toluidine (4.44): +I would raise basicity, but the ortho effect (steric hindrance to solvation of the anilinium ion and to protonation) overrides it.
  5. p-Toluidine > m-toluidine > aniline > o-toluidine.

15.4 Intramolecular hydrogen bonding and its effects on acidity and volatility

Intramolecular hydrogen bonding happens when a donor (O–H, N–H) and an acceptor (O, N) are held about 5–6 atoms apart in the same molecule: the usual chelate ring is six-membered (five- or seven-membered chelates are also seen).

salicylic acid o-nitrophenol o-hydroxybenzaldehyde maleate monoanion acetylacetone (enol)
Molecules with intramolecular O–H···O hydrogen bonds
  • Salicylic acid (2.98) vs 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (4.58) vs benzoic (4.20): in the salicylate ion the carboxylate oxygen is hydrogen-bonded to the ortho OH, a six-membered chelate that stabilises the anion. The effect on acidity is large. The second ionisation (phenolic OH, pKₐ₂ = 13.6) is very weak because the proton is held by the same hydrogen bond.
  • o-Nitrophenol: chelation in the neutral molecule makes it less acidic than expected (pKₐ 7.23 vs 7.15 for p). The hydrogen bond is in the acid; the proton is held back.
  • Volatility and solubility: o-nitrophenol (m.p. 45 °C, volatile in steam, soluble in non-polar solvents, poorly soluble in water) vs p-nitrophenol (m.p. 114 °C, involatile, intermolecular H-bonds, more soluble in water): steam distillation separates the two. Likewise o-hydroxybenzaldehyde (b.p. 197 °C) is more volatile than p-hydroxybenzaldehyde (m.p. 116 °C).
Illustration 15.8 Main

Salicylic acid

Salicylic acid is a stronger acid (pKₐ 2.98) than benzoic acid (4.20) and than p-hydroxybenzoic acid (4.58). Explain.

  1. Versus benzoic acid: OH is ortho, so it exerts a −I effect at the shortest distance; the ortho effect operates; and the salicylate anion is stabilised by an intramolecular O–H···O⁻ hydrogen bond (a six-membered chelate ring) between the phenolic OH and the carboxylate.
  2. Versus p-hydroxybenzoic acid: in the para isomer the +M of OH opposes the acid-strengthening effects and there is no chelation.
Illustration 15.9 Adv

o-Nitrophenol vs p-nitrophenol

Why is o-nitrophenol more volatile and less soluble in water than p-nitrophenol, and why is p-nitrophenol the stronger acid despite the ortho −I effect being stronger?

  1. Volatility/solubility: o-nitrophenol has an intramolecular hydrogen bond (O–H···O–N) so it does not associate with other molecules (or with water): low boiling point (relative to the para isomer), high vapour pressure, volatile in steam, lower solubility in water. p-Nitrophenol forms strong intermolecular H-bonds (with itself and with water): high m.p., not volatile, more soluble.
  2. Acidity: the chelated hydrogen of the ortho isomer is held back, so removing it requires breaking the H-bond; the stabilisation lost opposes the −I and −M gains. As a result pKₐ(o) = 7.23 > pKₐ(p) = 7.15: the para isomer is marginally stronger. Note the closeness of the values.

15.5 Steric inhibition of solvation and protonation

  • Steric inhibition of solvation (SIS): bulky groups hinder the solvent from stabilising an ion. Examples: tert-butyl alcohol (pKₐ 18) is a weaker acid than methanol in water; trialkylammonium ions are poorly solvated compared to primary ammonium ions; 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine (3.58) is a weaker base than pyridine (5.23).
  • Steric inhibition of protonation (SIP): a hindered lone pair is hard to bind to a proton; in the extreme (2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine) the base does not react with larger acids (BF₃, CH₃I) though it takes up H⁺.
  • Bridgehead/Cage compounds: quinuclidine (11.0) is a better base than Et₃N (10.75) because the alkyl groups are tied back and cannot hinder the lone pair.
Illustration 15.10 Adv

Quinuclidine vs triethylamine

Quinuclidine is a stronger base (pKₐH 11.0) than triethylamine (10.75). What explains this and what does it say about the role of steric effects?

  1. Both are tertiary amines with sp³ N and three alkyl groups, so the electronic (+I) effect is the same. In triethylamine, free rotation about the C–N bonds lets the ethyl groups crowd round the lone pair, hindering solvation of the protonated N–H.
  2. In quinuclidine the three arms are tied back by the bicyclic cage, the lone pair is completely exposed, and the ammonium ion is well solvated. The difference is therefore a steric/solvation effect, not an electronic one.
