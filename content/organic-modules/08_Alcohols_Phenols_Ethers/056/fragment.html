12. Structure, nomenclature and physical properties of phenols

Definition. A phenol has an OH group attached directly to an sp2 carbon of a benzene (or other aromatic) ring. The simplest member is phenol, C6H5OH (carbolic acid, hydroxybenzene). The oxygen lone pair is conjugated with the ring π system: the C–O bond (about 136 pm) is shorter than in methanol (142 pm) because it has partial double-bond character, and the O–H bond is more polarised. The dipole moment of phenol (about 1.5 D) is smaller than that of methanol (1.7 D) because the electron donation of oxygen into the ring (+M, towards the ring) opposes its −I effect (towards oxygen). Names. Substituted phenols are named as derivatives of phenol with OH at C-1: 2-methylphenol (o-cresol), 3-methylphenol (m-cresol), 4-methylphenol (p-cresol), 4-nitrophenol. Dihydric phenols have common names; the parent “phenol” is replaced by “benzenediol” in IUPAC names.

Trap A compound with an OH on a carbon of a side chain (benzyl alcohol, 2-phenylethanol) is an alcohol, not a phenol: it does not dissolve in aqueous NaOH, does not give a violet colour with FeCl3, and behaves like an ordinary alcohol. Benzyl alcohol and the cresols are isomers of formula C7H8O (together with anisole, C6H5OCH3): five isomers in all, a classic problem.
Physical properties.
  • Phenol is a colourless crystalline solid (mp 41 °C, bp 182 °C), turning pink or brown on exposure to air and light because of slow oxidation to quinones. It is caustic and toxic (carbolic acid, an early antiseptic; Lister used it).
  • Its boiling point is much higher than that of toluene (111 °C) or anisole (154 °C) because of intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Its solubility in water is about 8 g per 100 mL at room temperature; above 66 °C it is miscible. It is more soluble than benzene because it can H-bond to water but the hydrophobic ring limits the solubility.

Steam distillation is therefore the standard way of separating o-nitrophenol (distils) from p-nitrophenol (stays in the flask). Similarly salicylaldehyde (intramolecular H-bond, liquid, mp −7 °C, steam-volatile) is separated from p-hydroxybenzaldehyde (solid, mp 116 °C).

Boiling points of phenols

Arrange in increasing boiling point: phenol, toluene, anisole, benzyl alcohol, o-nitrophenol.

Show solution
  1. Toluene (111 °C) has only dispersion forces. Anisole (154 °C) has a dipole but no H-bond donor.
  2. Phenol (182 °C) and benzyl alcohol (205 °C) are H-bonded. o-Nitrophenol (216 °C) has a higher molecular mass and strong dipole; its intramolecular H-bond lowers its bp relative to what the intermolecular H-bond in the para isomer would give, but its mass keeps it above phenol.
  3. Order: toluene < anisole < phenol < benzyl alcohol < o-nitrophenol.
