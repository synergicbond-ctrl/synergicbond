1. The carbonyl group

In aldehydes (R–CHO) and ketones (R–CO–R′) the carbonyl carbon is sp2-hybridised. It forms three σ bonds in one plane (bond angles close to 120°) and one π bond from the unhybridised p orbitals of carbon and oxygen. The C=O bond is shorter (about 122 pm) and much stronger (about 745 kJ mol-1 in ketones) than a C–O single bond (about 143 pm and about 360 kJ mol-1). Oxygen is much more electronegative than carbon, so the π electrons are pulled towards oxygen.

The charge-separated contributor puts a partial positive charge on carbon and a partial negative charge on oxygen. It contributes only a small share, but it explains both the electrophilicity of carbon and the basicity of oxygen.

Consequences you will use again and again:

  • The carbonyl carbon is electrophilic (δ+): nucleophiles attack it. The reaction type that results is nucleophilic addition.
  • The carbonyl oxygen is weakly basic (the conjugate acid, a protonated ketone, has pKa about −7 for acetone): acids protonate oxygen and make carbon far more electrophilic. This is acid catalysis of addition.
  • The α-hydrogens are acidic (pKa about 17 for aldehydes, 19–20 for ketones in water-scale estimates; about 25 for esters), because the conjugate base (enolate) delocalises the negative charge onto oxygen. This is the basis of the aldol family, halogenation, and so on.
  • The molecule has a dipole moment: about 2.3 D for formaldehyde, about 2.7 D for acetaldehyde and about 2.9 D for acetone. That is much larger than for alkenes of similar size (propene 0.4 D) and explains the boiling points discussed below.
Shortcut Hybridisation count: in CH3–CO–CH3, C-1 and C-3 are sp3, C-2 is sp2, the oxygen is also sp2 (two lone pairs in sp2 orbitals), so acetone has 9 σ bonds and 1 π bond. Count: 6 C–H, 2 C–C and 1 C=O σ bonds = 9 σ.

Trajectory of attack A nucleophile does not attack C=O at 90° to the plane in a straight line. The favoured direction is about 107° to the C=O axis (the Bürgi–Dunitz angle), a compromise between overlap with the π* orbital (which has its large lobe on carbon) and repulsion from the oxygen lone pairs and from the substituents. After attack, carbon turns from sp2 (trigonal planar) into sp3 (tetrahedral). That change is the source of the steric effect on reactivity: the transition state moves towards a more crowded tetrahedron, so bulky groups slow it down. It is also the source of the electronic effect: alkyl groups donate electrons and reduce δ+ on carbon.

Planar carbonyl, two faces In an aldehyde RCHO or an unsymmetrical ketone RCOR′ the two faces of the planar C=O are different (enantiotopic if no other stereocentre is present; diastereotopic if there is one). Attack on either face generates a stereocentre. In the absence of any chiral influence, the two attacks are equally probable and a racemic product results. This is why every addition of CN−, a Grignard reagent or hydride to an achiral unsymmetrical carbonyl gives a racemate.
