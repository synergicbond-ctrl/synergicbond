10. Isomer families by formula type

Knowing the general formula tells you the family of functional classes that can share it. For acyclic saturated compounds these families are the ones met most often.

  • CnH2n+2O (DoU 0): alcohols and ethers only. C2H6O: ethanol, methoxymethane. C3H8O: two alcohols and one ether.
  • CnH2nO (DoU 1): aldehydes, ketones, unsaturated alcohols, enols, unsaturated ethers, cyclic ethers (oxiranes, oxetanes) and cycloalkanols. C3H6O has nine structural isomers in all.
  • CnH2nO2 (DoU 1): carboxylic acids, esters, hydroxy-aldehydes, hydroxy-ketones, alkoxy-carbonyls and cyclic hydroxy ethers or diethers. C2H4O2 has three isomers that contain C=O: ethanoic acid, methyl methanoate and hydroxyethanal.
  • CnH2n+3N (DoU 0): primary, secondary and tertiary amines (the formula C3H9N has propylamine, isopropylamine, N-methylethylamine and trimethylamine).
  • CnH2n-2 (DoU 2): alkynes, dienes (conjugated, isolated, cumulated), cycloalkenes and bicyclic alkanes.

Some members of the C3H6O family.

C2H7N: ethylamine (1°) and dimethylamine (2°).

The family decides which tests are meaningful. Aldehydes and ketones react with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine, acids with NaHCO3, primary amines with CHCl3/KOH (carbylamine), and alcohols with sodium. Of an acid and an ester of the same formula CnH2nO2, only the acid effervesces with NaHCO3, so that test separates them at once.

Key idea. Match the formula to a family (CnH2n+2O, CnH2nO, CnH2nO2, CnH2n+3N), then list only the classes that the DoU allows. Carboxylic acids and esters need two oxygens; amides need N and O.
Trap. Alcohols and ethers share CnH2n+2O only when acyclic and saturated. Add one DoU to the formula (CnH2nO) and unsaturated alcohols and cyclic ethers also appear, so counts for CnH2nO are larger than 'aldehydes plus ketones'.
