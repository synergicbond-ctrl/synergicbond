7. Substitution versus elimination: a decision guide

Every elimination competes with substitution, and the winner is decided by four variables. The following checklist gives the usual outcome for halides with a good leaving group.

  • Methyl or primary substrate, good nucleophile / weak base (I−, CN−, N3−, RS−): SN2.
  • Primary substrate, strong small base (EtO−): mainly SN2 with a little E2. With a bulky base (t-BuO−): mainly E2.
  • Secondary substrate, strong base (RO−, HO−): mainly E2. With a good nucleophile that is a weak base (I−, RS−, N3−) in a polar aprotic solvent: SN2.
  • Tertiary substrate, strong base: E2 only. Tertiary with no strong base in a polar protic solvent: SN1 + E1 mixture.
  • Temperature: higher temperature favours elimination (greater entropy gain).
  • Solvent: polar protic solvents stabilise ions and favour SN1/E1; polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF) raise the strength of anionic bases and favour SN2 and E2.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOEt, EtOH

Product:

Primary bromide with ethoxide: SN2 ether predominates.

Reactant:

Reagent: KOC(CH3)3, (CH3)3COH

Product:

Primary bromide with the bulky tert-butoxide: E2 (propene) now predominates because the bulky base cannot approach the carbon for SN2.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOEt, EtOH, heat

Product:

A tertiary halide cannot undergo SN2; with a strong base only E2 operates.

Trap. “Strong nucleophile” and “strong base” are not synonyms. Iodide, bromide, thiolates, cyanide and azide are good nucleophiles and weak bases, so they substitute even on secondary halides; hydroxide and alkoxides are strong bases and eliminate when SN2 is slowed by branching.
