7.2 Test for nitrogen: Prussian blue

Take 1 to 2 mL of the SFE. Add a few drops of freshly prepared FeSO4 solution (ferrous sulphate); a dirty green precipitate of Fe(OH)2 forms because the extract is alkaline. Boil, cool, then acidify with dilute H2SO4 or HCl (to dissolve the iron hydroxides). A Prussian blue colour (or precipitate) shows nitrogen. Often a little FeCl3 is added.

  • 6CN− + Fe2+ → [Fe(CN)6]4− (sodium hexacyanoferrate(II); formed on boiling)
  • 3[Fe(CN)6]4− + 4Fe3+ → Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3·xH2O (ferric ferrocyanide, Prussian blue; the Fe3+ comes from the air oxidation of Fe2+ during boiling or from added FeCl3)
Exam Trap Common wrong statements: (1) "blue colour is due to FeCl3 + NaCN" (it is the ferric hexacyanoferrate(II)); (2) "the ferrous sulphate must be old" (it must be fresh: old solutions have already turned yellow-brown through oxidation and give a green colour); (3) "a green colour means no nitrogen" (a small amount of nitrogen gives only a greenish blue; heat, dilute and allow to stand).

Compounds in which Lassaigne's test for nitrogen fails. (i) Compounds that lose their nitrogen on heating before it can combine with carbon and sodium: diazonium salts (lose N2). (ii) Compounds that contain no carbon to form CN−: hydrazine (N2H4), hydroxylamine (NH2OH), hydrazoic acid, and ammonium salts of inorganic acids, since N must meet C. Strictly, for a compound without carbon there can be no sodium cyanide. (iii) Compounds with N–N / N=N bonds that eliminate N2 easily on heating (some azo and diazo compounds, e.g. diazomethane, phenyl azide; azo compounds often still respond). The usual one-liner: "diazonium salts, hydrazine and hydroxylamine do not give Lassaigne's test".

Exception & Its Reason Rule: every nitrogen compound gives Prussian blue. Exceptions: diazonium salts (N2 leaves before fusion), N2H4, NH2OH (no carbon, so no CN−). Reason: CN− needs a carbon and a nitrogen to meet on hot sodium.
