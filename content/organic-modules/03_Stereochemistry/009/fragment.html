6. Geometrical isomerism: cis–trans and E/Z

6.1 Why it exists

A C=C bond consists of one σ-bond and one π-bond. The π-bond is made by sideways overlap of two parallel p-orbitals, so rotating one carbon relative to the other must break the π-bond (a barrier of roughly 250–270 kJ mol-1 for ethene, compared with 12 kJ mol-1 for the C–C bond of ethane). The two carbons and all four atoms attached to them lie in one plane. If each doubly-bonded carbon carries two different groups, the molecule can exist as two stereoisomers that do not interconvert at ordinary temperature.

Formula Condition for geometrical isomerism about C=C: a ≠ b on one carbon AND c ≠ d on the other (a,b)C=C(c,d)
Z-but-2-ene (cis) E-but-2-ene (trans) but-1-ene (a = b? no: C-1 has two H) 2-methylpropene
Only but-2-ene shows E/Z; but-1-ene and 2-methylpropene have two identical groups (H, H or CH3, CH3) on one carbon

The same logic applies to other restricted-rotation units:

  • C=N: oximes, hydrazones, imines, semicarbazones (R1R2C=N–X). The nitrogen lone pair acts as the "fourth substituent" and has the lowest priority.
  • N=N: azo compounds (E-azobenzene is the stable form; Z is formed by light).
  • Rings: cis/trans about the ring plane in 1,2-, 1,3-, 1,4-disubstituted cycloalkanes.
  • Not C≡C (linear), not C=O (two substituents only), not C=C=C in the central carbon.
Trap Compounds with C=C but two identical groups on one carbon are not geometrical isomers: CH2=CHR, R2C=CHR′, R2C=CR′2, and, importantly, X2C=CY2. So 1,1-dichloroethene has no cis/trans isomer, 1,2-dichloroethene has two, and 1,1,2-trichloroethene has none.

6.2 cis/trans versus E/Z

cis/trans: cis = two "similar" or identical groups on the same side; trans = on opposite sides. It is loose: for R–CH=CH–R′ with different R and R′ it is ambiguous unless you say which groups are compared. E/Z (CIP): on each carbon rank the two substituents by CIP priority; if the two higher-priority groups are on the same side, the compound is Z (German "zusammen"); if on opposite sides, E ("entgegen").

Key idea E/Z is unambiguous and always defined by CIP; cis/trans is defined by "same group" and can disagree with E/Z. A cis compound may be E.
(E)-1-bromo-1-chloropropene (Z)-1-bromo-1-chloropropene cis-1-bromopropene (Z) trans-1-bromopropene (E)
E/Z needs CIP priorities: Br > Cl > C > H

Let us build the habit of rank-then-compare with examples where cis ≠ Z:

cis-CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃... (Z) (Z)-1-chloropropene (Z)-1-chloro-2-methylbut-1-ene (CH₃ and Cl trans) (E)-1-chloro-2-methylbut-1-ene (CH₃ and Cl cis)
Last two: in the fourth drawing methyl and Cl are cis yet the label is E, because C2H5 outranks CH3
Example 9 Main

Assign E/Z

Assign E or Z to the following: (a) CH3CH=CHBr drawn with CH3 and Br on opposite sides; (b) HOOC–CH=CH–COOH (maleic acid, carboxyls on the same side); (c) (CH3)2CH–CH=CH–CH2OH with the isopropyl and CH2OH on opposite sides.

(a) (b) maleic acid (c)
Show solution
  1. (a) C-1: Br > H. C-2: CH3 > H. CH3 and Br on opposite sides → higher priorities opposite → E.
  2. (b) On each carbon: COOH > H. COOH groups on the same side → Z (maleic acid is the Z-isomer; fumaric acid is E).
  3. (c) C-3: carries CH(CH3)2 and H, so isopropyl wins. C-4 carries CH2OH and H, so CH2OH wins. They are on opposite sides → E.

At each end, only the two groups on that carbon are compared; the other carbon is not part of the comparison. Apply the CIP rules (section 10): first point of difference by atomic number, going outward sphere by sphere. A few frequently needed facts:

6.4 Oximes, hydrazones, azo compounds: syn/anti and E/Z

In oximes the OH lies on one side of the C=N. The nitrogen lone pair is the lowest-priority group, so at nitrogen OH wins; at carbon R beats H. R and OH on the same side = Z. The old syn/anti labels for aldoximes referred to H and OH (syn = H and OH on the same side), so (E)-aldoximes are the old "syn" forms and (Z) the old "anti" forms. This usage is deprecated: use E/Z unless a question defines syn/anti.

(E)-benzaldoxime (Z)-benzaldoxime (E)-azobenzene (Z)-azobenzene
Benzaldoxime and azobenzene: E has the two high-priority groups on opposite sides
Trap Acetone oxime, formaldoxime, and cyclohexanone oxime are not geometrical isomers (two identical groups on carbon, or a symmetrical ring). Acetophenone oxime and butanone oxime are. In the Beckmann rearrangement the group anti to the leaving OH group migrates, so the oxime geometry decides the product.

6.5 Counting geometrical isomers: the polyene formulae

Consider a conjugated or isolated polyene R–(CH=CH)n–R′ in which each C=C can show geometrical isomerism. Each C=C contributes a factor of 2 if the two ends are different, but if the molecule is symmetrical (R = R′) some combinations are identical.

Formula Unsymmetrical (R ≠ R′): number = 2n.
Formula Symmetrical (R = R′), n even: 2n−1 + 2(n/2)−1. n odd: 2n−1 + 2(n−1)/2.
Proof for the symmetric case. Reversing the chain is a symmetry of the molecule. There are 2n E/Z strings. A string is unchanged by reversal (palindromic) in 2⌈n/2⌉ cases. Counting each non-palindromic pair once (Burnside) gives (2n + 2⌈n/2⌉)/2 distinct isomers.
Formula Symmetric polyene with n double bonds: (2n + 2⌈n/2⌉) / 2. n = 2: (4 + 2)/2 = 3. n = 3: (8 + 4)/2 = 6. n = 4: (16 + 4)/2 = 10. n = 1: (2 + 2)/2 = 2.
For even n this equals 2n−1 + 2n/2−1; for odd n, ⌈n/2⌉ = (n+1)/2 and it equals 2n−1 + 2(n−1)/2: the textbook formulae.

6.6 Cumulenes: odd and even

Allenes (two cumulated C=C, X2C=C=CY2) have their two end groups in perpendicular planes and cannot show cis/trans isomerism; if each end carbon has two different groups, the molecule is axially chiral (see the section on axial chirality). Butatrienes (three cumulated C=C) behave differently: with an odd number of cumulated double bonds the end substituents lie in one plane and cis/trans exists. With an even number (allene: 2, hexapentaene: 4) the end planes are twisted by 90° and axial chirality results instead.

Key idea Cumulene rule: n cumulated double bonds. Even n (allene n = 2): axial chirality if each end has a ≠ b. Odd n (butatriene n = 3): cis/trans (E/Z) isomerism if each end has a ≠ b; no chirality.
buta-1,2,3-triene derivative (E) (Z) penta-2,3-diene (allene), axial chirality
Odd cumulene: E/Z; even cumulene: axial chirality (see 18)

6.7 Geometrical isomerism in rings

Substituents on a ring carbon are either on the same face (cis) or on opposite faces (trans) of the ring plane. Ring cis/trans exists when at least two ring carbons each bear two different groups (one of which may be H).

cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane (meso) trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane (one enantiomer) cis-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane trans-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane

Verified by computer (a claim students often get wrong). The odd/even cumulene rule is standard theory and was not machine-checked, because the toolkit does not perceive cumulene stereo: cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane is meso (achiral), trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane is chiral (an enantiomeric pair). cis-1,3-dimethylcyclohexane is meso, trans-1,3 is a chiral pair. cis- and trans-1,4 are both achiral.

Shortcut Ring counts for two identical substituents (all verified by the checks above): 1,2-dimethyl derivatives of cyclopropane, cyclobutane, cyclopentane and cyclohexane: cis = meso, trans = enantiomeric pair, 3 in all. 1,3-dimethylcyclopentane and 1,3-dimethylcyclohexane: 3. 1,3-dimethylcyclobutane: 2 (cis and trans, both achiral, because the ring has a mirror plane through C-2 and C-4). 1,4-dimethylcyclohexane: 2 (both achiral).

6.8 Physical properties of geometrical isomers

The two isomers are diastereomers (they are not mirror images), so they differ in physical properties.

Trap The higher melting point of the trans isomer is the usual rule: fumaric acid (trans) sublimes near 287 °C while maleic acid (cis) melts near 135 °C; elaidic acid (trans-C18) melts near 44 °C and oleic acid (cis) near 13 °C. But the "trans is more stable" rule has a famous exception: 1,2-dihaloethenes (F, Cl, Br) are more stable as the cis isomer (the "cis effect"). Do not apply the but-2-ene rule blindly to them.

Dipole moments

Arrange in increasing dipole moment: trans-1,2-dichloroethene, cis-1,2-dichloroethene, 1,1-dichloroethene, tetrachloroethene.

Show solution
  1. Tetrachloroethene is symmetrical: μ = 0. trans-1,2-Dichloroethene: the vectors cancel: μ = 0. 1,1-Dichloroethene has the two C–Cl dipoles at about 120°, μ ≈ 1.3 D. cis-1,2-Dichloroethene: the dipoles are about 60° apart, μ ≈ 1.9 D.
  2. Order: C2Cl4 = trans-1,2 (both zero) < 1,1-dichloroethene < cis-1,2-dichloroethene.

6.9 Interconversion and detection

cis ⇌ trans interconversion needs about 250 kJ/mol for simple alkenes. It occurs by: light (photoisomerisation: stilbene, azobenzene, retinal in vision), heat with a radical initiator (iodine catalysis: I• adds reversibly to the double bond), acid (protonation to a carbocation that rotates, for push-pull or highly substituted alkenes), or a catalyst such as Pd/H (hydrogen addition–elimination).
