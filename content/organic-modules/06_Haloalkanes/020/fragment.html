19. Allylic, benzylic and vinylic halides

19.1 Allylic and benzylic halides: unusually reactive

Allyl chloride (CH2=CH–CH2Cl) and benzyl chloride (C6H5CH2Cl) are primary halides but react faster than simple primary halides in both SN1 and SN2:

  • SN1: the cation is resonance-stabilised (allyl: delocalised over two carbons; benzyl: over the ring ortho/para positions). Substituents: p-CH3O and p-CH3 on benzyl accelerate ionisation; p-NO2 retards.
  • SN2: the transition state is stabilised by overlap of the p-orbital on the reacting carbon with the adjacent π system (allyl ≈ 40×, benzyl ≈ 100× faster than ethyl).
  • Radical stability: allyl and benzyl radicals: low C–H BDE, hence selective radical bromination with NBS.

Allylic rearrangement (SN1′, SN2′). The nucleophile may attack the γ-carbon of the allylic system with a double-bond shift (SN2′), favoured when the α-carbon is hindered, or at the cation termini in the SN1 path (SN1′). Product mixtures are common.

<h4 class="sub">19.2 Vinyl and aryl halides: why they do not react (SN1/SN2)</h4>

Reasons, in order of importance:

  • Resonance: the halogen lone pair conjugates with the π-bond (X–C=C ↔ X+=C–C−), giving the C–X bond partial double-bond character: it is stronger and shorter.
  • Hybridisation: the carbon is sp2 (33 % s-character) and more electronegative than sp3, so the C–X bond is shorter and stronger and the polarity Cδ+–Xδ− is reduced.
  • Instability of the cation: the vinyl cation (sp, empty sp orbital) and aryl cation (empty sp2 orbital in the plane, orthogonal to the π cloud) are far less stable than alkyl cations: no SN1.
  • Back-side attack is blocked: in SN2 the nucleophile approaches the sp2 carbon along the line of the C–X bond from the side opposite X: blocked by the π-electron cloud (repulsion) and the ring/alkene framework.
