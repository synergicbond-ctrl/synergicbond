8. Degrees of unsaturation and what a formula tells you

The degree of unsaturation (DoU, also called double bond equivalents) counts the number of rings plus π bonds in a molecule. It is calculated from the molecular formula CcHhNnXxOo by DoU = (2c + 2 + n - h - x)/2. Oxygen and sulfur do not appear in the formula because a divalent atom can be inserted into a bond without changing the hydrogen count; each halogen counts like a hydrogen; each nitrogen counts as an extra hydrogen that makes room for one more bond.

Formula. DoU = (2C + 2 + N - H - X)/2. Example: C6H6 has DoU = (14 - 6)/2 = 4; C2H6O has DoU = (6 - 6)/2 = 0.

Reading a formula with the DoU: DoU = 0 means a saturated acyclic compound with only single bonds (alkane, alcohol, ether, amine, haloalkane). DoU = 1 means one ring or one C=C or one C=O (aldehyde, ketone, acid, ester, alkene, cycloalkane, enol, cyclic ether). DoU = 2 may be one triple bond, two double bonds, two rings, or a ring plus a double bond. DoU = 4 or more with at least six carbon atoms strongly suggests a benzene ring (one ring and three C=C count as four). A nitro group counts as one unit, and a benzene ring bearing a nitro group has DoU 5.

DoU: benzene 4, ethanoic acid 1, propyne 2, cyclopropane 1.

The DoU narrows the field before enumerating. C4H8O (DoU 1) may be an aldehyde, ketone, enol, unsaturated alcohol or ether, or cyclic ether, but not a carboxylic acid (needs two oxygens). C7H8O has DoU 4 and a benzene ring is the only sensible way to place four degrees in seven carbons: cresols, benzyl alcohol, anisole.

Key idea. DoU tells how many rings and π bonds are available, not which ones. Compute it first, then decide which functional groups can fit. A carbonyl, a C=C and a ring each use up exactly one.
Trap. Halogens are treated like hydrogen, nitrogen is added, and oxygen is ignored. A common mistake is to subtract halogens twice or to count the S=O or N=O bonds of nitro and sulfonyl groups; in the formula method they are not counted separately.
