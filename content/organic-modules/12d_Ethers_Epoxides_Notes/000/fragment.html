1. Structure and bonding of ethers and epoxides

An ether has the general formula R–O–R′ with the oxygen bonded to two carbon atoms. The oxygen is sp3 hybridised and carries two lone pairs, so the molecule is bent. The C–O–C angle in dimethyl ether is a little larger than the tetrahedral value (about 111°) because the two alkyl groups repel each other more strongly than the two hydrogens in water do. The C–O bonds are polar, but the bent geometry means the dipoles only partly cancel, so ethers are weakly polar molecules.

Dimethyl ether, diethyl ether, oxolane (THF), 1,4-dioxane, anisole and oxirane (ethylene oxide).

Because oxygen has no O–H bond in an ether, an ether molecule cannot donate a hydrogen bond. It can still accept one through its lone pairs, which explains its solubility in water and its ability to act as a Lewis base. The same lone pairs coordinate to Mg in a Grignard reagent and to B in BF3, and they are protonated by strong acids to give oxonium ions R2OH+.

In aryl ethers such as anisole one lone pair of oxygen overlaps with the π system of the ring. This +M effect makes the ring electron rich and strengthens the Ar–O bond, which is why aryl–oxygen bonds are never broken in the acid cleavage reactions treated later.

In an epoxide (oxirane) the three ring atoms form an almost equilateral triangle with internal angles of about 60°. The bonds are bent ('banana' bonds) and the ring strain is of the same order as in cyclopropane. This strain, relieved on ring opening, is the reason epoxides react with many nucleophiles under conditions where ordinary ethers are inert.

Key idea. An ordinary ether is unreactive because RO− is a very poor leaving group and there is no acidic hydrogen. Reactivity appears only when the oxygen is first protonated (strong acid) or when the ring is strained (epoxides).
