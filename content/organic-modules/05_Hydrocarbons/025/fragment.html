24. Catalytic hydrogenation of alkenes

H2 (1–4 atm) + a metal catalyst (Pt, Pd/C, Ni, Rh; PtO2 (Adams), Raney Ni) at room temperature (Pt, Pd) or 100–200 °C (Ni): alkene → alkane. Exothermic by about 120–137 kJ mol-1. Homogeneous catalyst: Wilkinson's catalyst RhCl(PPh3)3 (hydrogenates less hindered C=C selectively; it also leaves C=O alone). Mechanism (heterogeneous): H2 adsorbed dissociatively on the metal; alkene π complexes to the surface; two H atoms transfer in sequence to the same face: syn addition; the alkane desorbs. This explains:

  • cis-alkene + D2 → meso-dideuterioalkane; trans → racemic (for 2-butene).
  • 1,2-Dimethylcyclohexene + H2/Pt → cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane (both H's on the face that touches the catalyst).
  • Hydrogenation is sensitive to steric hindrance (more substituted alkenes react more slowly): 1-alkene > disubstituted > trisubstituted > tetrasubstituted, the reverse of stability.

Example 43 Advanced

H2/D2 stereochemistry

How many stereoisomers (and which) result when (a) cis-but-2-ene (b) trans-but-2-ene is treated with D2/Pt? (c) cyclohexene with D2/Pt?

Show solution
  1. Syn addition of two D atoms to C-2 and C-3 gives CH3CHD–CHDCH3.
  2. (a) cis → meso (2R,3S)-2,3-d2-butane: 1 product, achiral.
  3. (b) trans → racemic (2R,3R)/(2S,3S): 2 enantiomers.
  4. (c) cyclohexene → cis-1,2-dideuteriocyclohexane (meso, achiral).
Example 44 Foundation

Heat of hydrogenation reasoning

The heats of hydrogenation of cis- and trans-but-2-ene are 120 and 115 kJ mol-1. What is the energy difference between the isomers, and which is more stable? Why does the cis alkene release more energy?

Show solution
  1. ΔE = 120 − 115 = 5 kJ mol-1; trans is more stable. Both give butane, so the cis alkene starts from a higher energy level: the two CH3 groups on the same side suffer van der Waals (steric) repulsion (≈ 4–5 kJ), relieved by hydrogenation.
