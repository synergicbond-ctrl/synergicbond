29. The Knoevenagel condensation

A carbonyl compound reacts with a compound having an active methylene group Z–CH2–Z′ (Z, Z′ = COOR, CN, COR, NO2) in the presence of a weak base (piperidine, pyridine, ammonia, an amine salt) to give an alkylidene derivative after dehydration. The weak base is enough to deprotonate the active methylene (pKa 9–13) but not strong enough to cause self-condensation of the aldehyde.

+ piperidine, EtOH⟶
Benzaldehyde + diethyl malonate → diethyl benzylidenemalonate
+ pyridine or piperidine⟶
Benzaldehyde + malononitrile → benzylidenemalononitrile
+ pyridine, piperidine, Δ (Doebner)⟶
Doebner modification: malonic acid loses CO₂ during the reaction, giving cinnamic acid
Key idea Three ways to make cinnamic acid from benzaldehyde: Perkin (Ac2O/AcONa, 453 K), Knoevenagel–Doebner (malonic acid, pyridine), and Wittig/HWE (Ph3P=CHCOOEt, then hydrolysis). In each case two carbons are added to PhCHO (C7H6O) and the product is cinnamic acid, C9H8O2.
Mechanism. The base removes H from CH2(Z)2; the carbanion attacks C=O; the alkoxide is protonated; E1cB dehydration (the remaining C–H is acidic) gives the alkene. Amines (piperidine) can also work by forming an iminium ion from the aldehyde, which is more electrophilic.
