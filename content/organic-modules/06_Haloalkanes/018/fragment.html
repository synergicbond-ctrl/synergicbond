17. Reactions of alkyl halides with metals and reducing agents

17.1 Reduction to alkanes

R–X → R–H by: LiAlH4 (SN2 by H−; works for 1°, 2°), H2/Pd (or Raney Ni), Zn/HCl or Zn/CH3COOH, red P/HI at 150 °C (and also reduces many other groups), Bu3SnH (radical, AIBN), or Mg then H2O (via Grignard). Na/liquid NH3 also works.

LiAlH4, ether⟶
Hydride displaces bromide (SN2). Tertiary halides give mainly alkenes.

17.2 Wurtz reaction

2 R–X + 2 Na (dry ether) → R–R + 2 NaX. Two alkyl radicals (or an organosodium + RX) couple; the product has a carbon chain equal to the sum of the two alkyl groups. Best for symmetrical alkanes from 1° halides. A mixture of two different halides gives three alkanes (R–R, R′–R′, R–R′), so the reaction is not useful for unsymmetrical alkanes. Tertiary halides eliminate; methane cannot be made; odd-carbon alkanes cannot be formed from a single halide.

2 Na, dry ether⟶
Ethyl bromide to butane.
Zn or Na⟶
Intramolecular Wurtz: 1,3-dibromopropane gives cyclopropane (with Zn; Na also works). 1,4- and 1,5-dihalides give cyclobutane and cyclopentane.
  • Wurtz–Fittig: Ar–X + R–X + 2 Na (dry ether) → Ar–R (side product Ar–Ar and R–R). Fittig: 2 Ar–X + 2 Na → Ar–Ar (biphenyl).
+ 2 Na, ether⟶
Wurtz–Fittig: ethylbenzene from bromobenzene and ethyl bromide, together with biphenyl and butane.
  • Frankland: R–X + Zn → R2Zn (dialkylzinc), historical organometallic.
Trap Wurtz needs dry ether (no H2O or ROH: Na reacts violently). A typical 'products count' question: a mixture of CH3Cl and C2H5Cl with Na/ether gives three alkanes: ethane, propane, butane.
<h4 class="sub">17.3 Corey–House (Gilman) synthesis</h4>

R–X → R–Li (2 Li, ether) → R2CuLi (lithium dialkylcuprate, 0.5 CuI) → R2CuLi + R′–X → R–R′ + RCu + LiX. Couples two different alkyl groups cleanly (R′X primary or secondary alkyl, vinyl, aryl halides; R′ best methyl/primary).

1. 2 Li 2. CuI 3. CH3CH2Br⟶
Hexyl bromide + ethyl bromide: octane (an unsymmetrical alkane free of the mixture problem of the Wurtz).

17.4 Friedel–Crafts alkylation

ArH + R–X + AlCl3 → Ar–R + HX. Electrophile: R+ (or polarised R–X···AlCl3). Rearrangement of R is likely (n-propyl chloride gives isopropylbenzene (cumene) mainly), polyalkylation occurs (product more reactive than benzene), and the reaction fails on strongly deactivated rings (nitrobenzene, acylbenzenes) and on aryl or vinyl halides (no carbocation).

+ AlCl3⟶
n-Propyl chloride gives mainly isopropylbenzene (1,2-hydride shift in the incipient 1° cation/complex).

17.5 Halide tests

  • Alcoholic AgNO3: R–X + AgNO3 → AgX↓ + R–ONO2. For ionising substrates (SN1): tertiary, allylic, benzylic precipitate at once at room temperature; secondary on warming; primary on heating; vinyl/aryl halides give no precipitate. Colours: AgCl white (soluble in NH3), AgBr pale yellow (sparingly soluble in NH3), AgI yellow (insoluble in NH3).
  • Lassaigne (sodium fusion) test: the halogen is converted to NaX and tested with AgNO3 after acidification with HNO3. Works for aryl halides too.
  • Beilstein test: a green-blue flame when the compound is heated on a copper wire (CuX): qualitative test for halogen (false positives with some N compounds).
Shortcut Rates of precipitation with AgNO3/EtOH: benzyl/allyl/3° > 2° > 1° ≫ vinyl ≈ aryl; and for the halide: RI > RBr > RCl. Primary alkyl iodide may precipitate faster than a 2° chloride.
