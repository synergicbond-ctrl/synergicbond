5. Ring-chain isomerism

Ring-chain isomers have the same molecular formula, but one is acyclic and the other contains a ring. A ring removes two hydrogens relative to the acyclic alkane (one degree of unsaturation), so an alkene CnH2n is a ring-chain isomer of the corresponding cycloalkane. Typical pairs are propene and cyclopropane, but-1-ene and cyclobutane, and propanone with oxetane or methyloxirane.

Propene and cyclopropane, C3H6.

C4H8: but-1-ene (acyclic) alongside cyclobutane and methylcyclopropane (rings).

C3H6O: propanone (chain) with oxetane and 2-methyloxirane (rings).

Ring-chain isomerism also occurs among alkynes and cycloalkenes. Propyne and cyclopropene share the formula C3H4; but-1-yne, buta-1,3-diene, cyclobutene and methylcyclopropene are all C4H6. Chemical properties show the difference: the alkene adds bromine at once, whereas cyclobutane and, under ordinary conditions, methylcyclopropane are taken as not decolourising bromine in carbon tetrachloride in the dark (a strained cyclopropane ring can open under forcing conditions or with a catalyst).

Key idea. Count every ring as one degree of unsaturation. If a formula has one degree of unsaturation, it may be a C=C, a C=O, or a ring, and all three families are mutually isomeric.
Trap. Small strained rings (cyclopropane, oxirane) open with acids or hydrogen halides and can mimic alkene behaviour in some reactions; yet in the standard cold bromine test they are taken as unreactive, whereas alkenes decolourise bromine at once. That test is the usual way to tell them apart in this chapter.
