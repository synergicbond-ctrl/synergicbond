9. The EAS reactions one by one

9.1 Nitration

Mixed acid generates the nitronium ion: HNO3 + 2 H2SO4 ⇌ NO2+ + H3O+ + 2 HSO4−. Sulphuric acid is the stronger acid; it protonates HNO3, and loss of water gives NO2+. This is why nitration in conc. H2SO4 is fast and why dilute acid is ineffective.

conc. HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄, ≤ 330 K⟶ fuming HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄, 370 K⟶
Mononitration, then meta dinitration (deactivated ring needs forcing conditions)
  • Temperature control. Benzene is mono-nitrated at 323–333 K (50–60 °C); above this, dinitrobenzene forms. Further nitration of m-dinitrobenzene to 1,3,5-trinitrobenzene is very difficult (the ring is a million-fold or more less reactive than nitrobenzene, which itself is about 10-7 times as reactive as benzene).
  • Toluene is nitrated about 25 times faster than benzene, and at lower temperature (303 K); TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene) is made stepwise: toluene → o/p-nitrotoluene → 2,4-dinitrotoluene → TNT, each step requiring a hotter, stronger acid mixture.
  • Phenol with dilute HNO3 (room temperature) gives o- and p-nitrophenol; the ortho isomer is volatile in steam because of intramolecular hydrogen bonding and is separated by steam distillation; p-nitrophenol is non-volatile (intermolecular H-bonds). With conc. HNO3/H2SO4 phenol is oxidised and over-nitrated to picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol); a cleaner route sulphonates phenol first, then nitrates.
  • Aniline is not nitrated directly: HNO3 oxidises it (tar) and in H2SO4 it exists as anilinium (NH3+, meta-directing), giving a large meta product (≈ 47 %) alongside para (≈ 51 %) and ortho (≈ 2 %). Acetylate first (acetanilide), nitrate, then hydrolyse: p-nitroaniline.
Trap Nitration of aniline in mixed acid gives a significant meta product because the NH3+ form is the species that is attacked: do not write "only o- and p-". For N,N-dimethylaniline in strong acid, the same applies. Acetylation avoids it.

9.3 Halogenation

Benzene does not react with Br2 or Cl2 alone (no Br2/CCl4 addition, no decolourisation): a Lewis acid polarises the halogen to make an electrophilic halogen.

Br₂, FeBr₃ (or Fe)⟶ Br₂, FeBr₃, Δ⟶
Bromination; the second bromine goes ortho/para (o- and p-dibromobenzene, para major)
  • Chlorine requires AlCl3 or FeCl3; iron filings are sufficient because Fe + Cl2 → FeCl3 in situ.
  • Fluorine reacts explosively (too reactive; non-selective; also attack at C–H of side chains and C–C cleavage). Aryl fluorides are made by the Balz–Schiemann reaction: ArN2+BF4− → ArF + N2 + BF3 on heating.
  • Iodine is unreactive toward benzene (the reaction is reversible and HI reduces ArI back to ArH); an oxidising agent (HNO3, HIO3, Cu2+) removes HI, or use ICl, or Hg(OCOCF3)2/I2. Aryl iodides are readily prepared from diazonium salts with KI.
  • Activated rings need no catalyst. Phenol and aniline react with aqueous bromine at once to give 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white ppt) and 2,4,6-tribromoaniline; the reaction is used as a test. In CS2, at low temperature, phenol gives mainly p-bromophenol.
  • Radical conditions give different chemistry: benzene + Cl2 under UV light (no catalyst, in the cold) adds three Cl2 to give 1,2,3,4,5,6-hexachlorocyclohexane (benzene hexachloride, BHC; the γ-isomer is the insecticide lindane). Toluene + Cl2/hv substitutes in the side chain.

9.4 Friedel–Crafts alkylation

Benzene + R–X + AlCl3 (catalytic) → Ar–R + HX. The same reaction works with alkenes (H+, HF, H3PO4, AlCl3/HCl) and alcohols (BF3, H2SO4). Industrially, ethylbenzene (for styrene) and cumene (for phenol + acetone) are made this way.

+ AlCl₃⟶
Ethylbenzene by Friedel–Crafts alkylation (R–Cl + AlCl₃ → R⁺ AlCl₄⁻)
+ H₃PO₄, 520 K, pressure (industrial cumene)⟶
Cumene from propene: the secondary cation is formed by protonation (Markovnikov)
  • The electrophile is the carbocation or the polarised complex R–Cl···AlCl3. Reactivity of the halide: tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl (stability of cation); for the same alkyl group, RF > RCl > RBr > RI (strength of coordination to AlCl3).
  • Catalyst strength (approximate, strong → weak): AlCl3 > FeCl3 > SbCl5 > SnCl4 > BF3 > ZnCl2.
  • Aryl and vinyl halides do not work as alkylating agents (they do not give stable cations). Chlorobenzene cannot be used to alkylate benzene.
  • Benzene with excess CH2Cl2/AlCl3 → diphenylmethane; with CHCl3 → triphenylmethane; with CCl4 → triphenylchloromethane (trityl chloride). Benzene + ethylene oxide, AlCl3 → 2-phenylethanol.

Example 12 Adv

Which of these cannot be made by direct Friedel–Crafts alkylation of benzene?

(a) toluene (b) ethylbenzene (c) n-propylbenzene (d) tert-butylbenzene (e) isobutylbenzene (f) neopentylbenzene

Show solution
  1. (a), (b), (d) are made directly (methyl, ethyl have no rearrangement issue; the tertiary cation is formed).
  2. (c) n-Propylbenzene: not directly (gives cumene); make by acylation (propanoyl chloride) then Clemmensen.
  3. (e) Isobutylbenzene: not directly (isobutyl chloride gives t-butylbenzene). Make it by acylation with (CH3)2CHCOCl/AlCl3 to give PhCOCH(CH3)2 (isobutyrophenone), then Clemmensen reduction of C=O to CH2: PhCH2CH(CH3)2.

For an exam, the expected answer is simply "cannot be made by direct alkylation".

9.6 Friedel–Crafts acylation

Benzene + RCOCl (or (RCO)2O) + AlCl3 → ArCOR. The acyl chloride loses Cl− to AlCl3 to give the acylium ion R–C≡O+ (linear, resonance-stabilised: R–C+=O ↔ R–C≡O+). The acylium ion is a good electrophile but, unlike an alkyl cation, it does not rearrange, and the ketone product is deactivated, so the reaction stops at one acyl group.

+ AlCl₃ (≥ 1 equiv.), then H₂O⟶
Acetophenone. The first step gives CH₃–C≡O⁺ AlCl₄⁻, which attacks benzene; the product ketone forms a 1 : 1 complex with AlCl₃, so at least one full equivalent of AlCl₃ is consumed; aqueous work-up releases the ketone.

9.9 Reactions of phenols that are EAS in disguise

Reimer–Tiemann. Phenol + CHCl3 + aq. NaOH (≈ 340 K), then H+ → mainly salicylaldehyde (o-hydroxybenzaldehyde), a little p-isomer. The electrophile is dichlorocarbene, :CCl2, formed by α-elimination from CCl3−; the phenoxide ion (a much stronger nucleophile than phenol) attacks it through the ortho carbon (ion-pairing with Na+ directs ortho), giving ArCHCl2, which hydrolyses to ArCHO. With CCl4 in place of CHCl3 the product is salicylic acid.

1. CHCl₃, NaOH(aq), 340 K 2. H₃O⁺⟶
Reimer–Tiemann: salicylaldehyde (ortho major); p-hydroxybenzaldehyde is the minor product

Kolbe–Schmitt. Sodium phenoxide + CO2 (4–7 atm, ≈ 400 K) then H+ → salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid). CO2 is a weak electrophile, so only the phenoxide ion reacts. With potassium phenoxide at higher temperature (≈ 500 K) the para acid (4-hydroxybenzoic acid) predominates (thermodynamic). Salicylic acid + acetic anhydride → aspirin; + methanol (H+) → methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen).

1. CO₂, 400 K, 4–7 atm 2. H₃O⁺⟶
Kolbe–Schmitt: sodium phenoxide → salicylic acid

Fries rearrangement. A phenyl ester with AlCl3 rearranges to o- and p-hydroxyaryl ketones. The acyl group migrates through an acylium–phenoxide–AlCl3 pair (largely intermolecular). Low temperature (≈ 298 K or below) favours the para isomer (kinetic), high temperature (> 433 K) favours the ortho isomer (thermodynamic: chelate with AlCl3, intramolecular H-bond in the product, and ortho isomer volatile in steam).

9.10 Azo coupling

Aryldiazonium ions are the weakest of the common electrophiles and react only with strongly activated arenes: phenols (as phenoxide), anilines (free amine), naphthols, and N,N-dialkylanilines. Substitution occurs at para, or ortho if the para position is blocked. The pH matters:

  • Phenols: pH 9–10. The phenoxide ion is the reactive nucleophile; at pH > 11 the diazonium ion is converted to inert diazotate, ArN=N–O−; at low pH the concentration of phenoxide is too low.
  • Amines: pH 4–7 (weakly acidic). Free amine is needed; in strong acid it is protonated (–NH3+, no reaction); in base the diazonium ion is lost. At the correct pH coupling happens at the ring para carbon (or at N first to give a diazoamino compound, which rearranges).
+ NaOH, pH 9–10, 273–278 K⟶
Coupling with phenol: 4-hydroxyazobenzene (p-hydroxyazobenzene), an orange dye
+ pH 4–5⟶
Coupling with N,N-dimethylaniline: p-dimethylaminoazobenzene (butter yellow); methyl orange is the sulphonated analogue

9.11 Other EAS reactions worth knowing

  • Blanc chloromethylation: ArH + HCHO + HCl (ZnCl2) → ArCH2Cl. Used to attach a handle to polystyrene beads (Merrifield resin). Carcinogenic bis(chloromethyl) ether is a by-product.
  • Nitrosation: N,N-dimethylaniline + HNO2 (NaNO2/HCl, 273 K) → p-nitroso-N,N-dimethylaniline (green crystals); phenol + HNO2 → p-nitrosophenol (+ o). The electrophile is NO+, weak, so only highly activated rings react. Secondary aromatic amines give N-nitroso compounds (Liebermann test).
  • Mercuration: ArH + Hg(OAc)2 → ArHgOAc, a mild, highly para-selective electrophilic substitution; ArHg+ can then be converted to ArBr, ArI, or ArH.
  • Hydrogen exchange: D2SO4 or DCl in D2O replaces ring H by D at the same positions favoured by EAS (a quick experimental probe of the arenium ion stability); this is the simplest EAS, with E+ = D+.
  • Protodesilylation, ipso substitution: a silyl group (–SiMe3) is replaced by E at the same carbon (the ipso carbon) because the β-silicon effect stabilises the arenium ion; the same logic makes –SO3H, –COOH, –t-Bu groups replaceable (ipso substitution): p-bromo-tert-butylbenzene + Br+ may expel t-butyl cation.

Example 16 Adv

Mechanism-level question: why is Reimer–Tiemann ortho-selective?

Give two reasons that the aldehyde group enters ortho to the oxygen in the Reimer–Tiemann reaction, and say why it fails for anisole.

Show solution
  1. (1) The reactive species is the phenoxide ion, which carries most of the negative charge on O, ortho and para carbons; the Na+ ion associates with the phenoxide O and the electron-deficient dichlorocarbene, bringing :CCl2 close to the ortho carbon.
  2. (2) The ortho product is stabilised by an intramolecular O–H···O=C hydrogen bond (salicylaldehyde is a chelate), and is volatile in steam: it is separated from the para isomer by steam distillation.
  3. Anisole has no acidic OH, so no phenoxide can form in aqueous NaOH, and :CCl2 is too weak an electrophile to attack neutral anisole at an appreciable rate: no reaction.
