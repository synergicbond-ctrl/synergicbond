36. Mechanism: acylation of an amine by an acid chloride

Reaction: RNH2 + R'COCl → RNHCOR' + HCl (nucleophilic acyl substitution: addition–elimination).

  • Step 1 – addition. The lone pair of N attacks the carbonyl carbon along the Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory (≈ 107° to the C=O axis), the direction where the C=O π* (the LUMO) has its largest lobe and away from the repulsive filled π orbital. The π electrons move onto oxygen: tetrahedral intermediate (an alkoxide–ammonium zwitterion). Why: C=O carbon is electron-poor (Cl and O both withdraw), and π*(C=O) is low in energy.
  • Step 2 – collapse. The O− lone pair re-forms C=O and expels the best leaving group: Cl− (not R', not N). Why: Cl− is the weakest base of the three groups on carbon. This step is faster than the reverse (loss of the amine) because the amine is a worse leaving group.
  • Step 3 – deprotonation. The N–H+ of the protonated amide (acylammonium) is lost to a base (pyridine, NaOH, a second amine). Why: it pulls the equilibrium to the neutral amide; without a base half of the amine is wasted as RNH3+Cl−.

Acetyl chloride + methylamine: addition → tetrahedral intermediate → elimination → deprotonation

Why the reaction stops at the amide: the amide nitrogen lone pair is tied up in π-conjugation with C=O (Section 34.1), so the product is much less nucleophilic than the starting amine. Why anhydrides and esters are slower: the leaving group (carboxylate, alkoxide) is a stronger base than Cl−, and the carbonyl is less electrophilic because O-lone pairs donate into π*(C=O).
