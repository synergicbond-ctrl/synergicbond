10. Hyperconjugation

10.1 The idea

In hyperconjugation (also called σ–π conjugation or 'no-bond resonance') the electrons of a σ-bond, usually C–H of an alkyl group attached to an unsaturated carbon, are delocalised into the neighbouring empty or partially filled p-orbital or into the antibonding π* orbital. The σ-bond is said to 'conjugate' with the π-system without being a π-bond itself.

ethyl cation isopropyl cation tert-butyl cation propene toluene ethyl radical
The sp³ carbon next to the cationic, alkene or radical carbon bears the α-hydrogens
  • σ(C–H) → empty p-orbital (carbocation): stabilises the cation.
  • σ(C–H) → π\* (alkene): stabilises the alkene, makes the double bond a little longer and the adjacent C–C bond a little shorter.
  • σ(C–H) → half-filled p (radical): stabilises the radical.

For the C–H bond to overlap with the p-orbital, the bond must be roughly parallel to the axis of the p-orbital: free rotation of the CH₃ group provides it.

Alkene stability rises with the number of alkyl groups on the double bond: R₂C=CR₂ > R₂C=CHR > R₂C=CH₂ ≈ RCH=CHR (trans > cis) > RCH=CH₂ > CH₂=CH₂. This is the thermodynamic basis of the Saytzeff (Zaitsev) rule for elimination. The trans isomer is more stable than cis because of reduced steric repulsion (about 4 kJ mol⁻¹ in 2-butene), not because of hyperconjugation (both have six α-H).

  • Carbocations: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺ because of +I and hyperconjugation (9, 6, 3, 0 α-H).
  • Free radicals: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃• (the same reasoning; C–H BDE of ethane 423, propane (secondary) 413, isobutane (tertiary) 404 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • Bond lengths: C(sp³)–C(sp²) in propene (150 pm) is shorter than in ethane (154 pm) and the C=C bond is slightly longer than in ethene — partly s-character but also partial double-bond character in the C–CH₃ bond.
  • Dipole moments: propene 0.36 D, toluene 0.36 D with CH₃ as the positive end.
  • Orientation in toluene: the CH₃ group (+I and hyperconjugation) activates the ring at ortho and para. p-Toluic acid (pKₐ 4.34) is a weaker acid than benzoic acid (4.20), because the methyl group donates electrons.
  • Carbanions are destabilised by alkyl groups (+I effect, and no π-acceptor help): CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°.

Stability of alkenes

Arrange in increasing stability: 1-butene, trans-2-butene, 2-methyl-2-butene, cis-2-butene, 2,3-dimethyl-2-butene.

Illustration 10.3 Adv

Hyperconjugation or induction?

The relative rates of reaction of p-alkylbenzyl bromides with a nucleophile follow the order p-CH₃ > p-C₂H₅ > p-iPr > p-tBu in some solvents (Baker–Nathan order), which is the order of the number of α-C–H bonds. In other solvents the order is reversed. What does this show?

  1. If hyperconjugation alone controlled the rates, the order would be the same in all media: CH₃ (3 α-H) > C₂H₅ (2) > iPr (1) > tBu (0).
  2. The reversal in other media (Hughes–Ingold order, tBu > iPr > Et > Me, the order of the +I effect) shows that the order depends on solvation of the alkyl groups: C–H bonds near the reaction site are solvated best when there are more hydrogens.
  3. Therefore the 'Baker–Nathan effect' is not a clean proof of hyperconjugation; for JEE Advanced you simply use: hyperconjugation counts α-H, inductive effect counts size, and both favour t-butyl for carbocation stability.
Illustration 10.4 Adv

Which carbocation is more stable?

Compare (CH₃)₃C⁺ and CH₃CH₂⁺ on (i) α-H count, (ii) inductive effect, and give the conclusion.

  1. (i) (CH₃)₃C⁺ has 9 α-H, CH₃CH₂⁺ has 3: nine hyperconjugative structures against three.
  2. (ii) Three methyl groups donate electrons by +I, one methyl donates less.
  3. Both effects stabilise the tertiary cation: (CH₃)₃C⁺ ≫ CH₃CH₂⁺ (in the gas phase the tertiary cation is far more easily formed from the hydrocarbon than the ethyl cation; in solution only 3° halides ionise readily in SN1).
Illustration 10.5 Adv

Bond length with hyperconjugation

Why is the C–C single bond in CH₃–CH=CH₂ shorter (150 pm) than in CH₃–CH₃ (154 pm)?

  1. Two effects: (1) the carbon of the CH=CH₂ is sp² (33 % s) and bonds formed from sp²–sp³ overlap are shorter than sp³–sp³; (2) hyperconjugation gives the C–CH₃ bond partial double-bond character through the no-bond structures H⁺ ⁻CH₂–CH=CH₂ ↔ H⁺ CH₂=CH–CH₂⁻.
  2. Both effects shorten the bond.

10.3 Negative hyperconjugation and reverse hyperconjugation

  • Negative hyperconjugation: donation from a lone pair (or a filled p-orbital) into an adjacent σ* orbital such as σ*(C–F) or σ*(C–O). It underlies the gauche preference of 1,2-difluoroethane and the anomeric effect in sugars (axial OR on the anomeric carbon of a pyranose is preferred) and helps explain why carbanions β to fluorine are stabilised.
  • Reverse hyperconjugation (σ* acceptance): the C–X bonds of –CF₃ and –CCl₃ are low-energy acceptors; they accept π-electron density from an adjacent π-system. This is invoked, besides the very strong −I effect, for the meta-directing nature and the strong deactivation by –CF₃ and –CCl₃ (σ* C–X orbitals) – the groups behave as 'electron acceptors' without a π-bond.
Illustration 10.6 Adv

Direction of the dipole moment

In 3,3,3-trifluoropropene CF₃–CH=CH₂ the dipole is larger than that expected from the C–F bonds alone. Give one reason based on electronic effects.

  1. CF₃ is a very strong −I group and also an acceptor through σ*(C–F) (reverse hyperconjugation), withdrawing electron density from the double bond, so the C=C carbon next to CF₃ is δ⁺.
  2. The CF₃–C vector and the polarised C=C add, giving a larger moment than the C–F bonds alone suggest.
