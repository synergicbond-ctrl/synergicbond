16. Substitution versus elimination: the competition map

Every alkyl halide that can undergo SN2 can usually also undergo E2 (a nucleophile is a base), and every substrate that can ionise (SN1) can usually also lose a proton (E1). The product is decided by the substrate, the reagent (base strength, steric size, nucleophilicity), the solvent and the temperature. The rules below are enough for almost every JEE question.

16.1 Role of the substrate

Alkene percentage from alkyl bromides with sodium ethoxide in ethanol at 55 °C (classical data):

R–Br% elimination (alkene)% substitution (ether)
CH3CH2Br (ethyl)≈ 1≈ 99
CH3CH2CH2Br (n-propyl)≈ 9≈ 91
(CH3)2CHCH2Br (isobutyl, β-branched)≈ 60≈ 40
(CH3)2CHBr (isopropyl, 2°)≈ 80≈ 20
(CH3)3CBr (tert-butyl)≈ 100≈ 0
!key The more hindered the α- and β-carbons, the more elimination: SN2 is blocked by crowding at the back side of the α-carbon, while E2 only needs the base to reach a peripheral hydrogen on the outside of the molecule. - **Methyl, primary:** SN2 wins with all common reagents. Exceptions: bulky strong bases (t-BuOK) give E2 for 1° with a β-hydrogen; β-branched 1° halides (isobutyl) give a lot of E2. - **Secondary:** a //strong base// (EtO−, HO−) → mostly E2 (80 % elimination for isopropyl bromide); a //good nucleophile that is a weak base// (I−, Br−, CN− in DMSO/DMF, N3−, RS−, PhS−) → SN2; weak nucleophile in a protic solvent → SN1/E1 (heat → E1). - **Tertiary:** no SN2; strong base → E2; weak base/nucleophile in polar protic solvent → SN1 + E1. - **Allyl and benzyl (primary):** SN2/SN1 with good nucleophiles; elimination only for strongly hindered bases (benzyl has no β-H if CH2).

!tip **Base vs nucleophile** is the central question: basicity is a thermodynamic affinity for H+; nucleophilicity is the kinetic rate of attack at carbon. Bulky, strongly basic anions (t-BuO−, LDA) are only bases; large, soft, weakly basic anions (I−, RS−, N3−, CN−) are good nucleophiles but poor bases (pKa of conjugate acid: HI −10, HN3 4.7, HCN 9.2, RSH ≈ 10, vs ROH ≈ 16, H2O 15.7, NH3 38).

16.3 Role of the solvent and temperature

  • Temperature: high temperature favours elimination over substitution (elimination increases the number of molecules, ΔS > 0; its activation energy is slightly higher). "Heat" in a question is a strong hint for elimination.
  • Solvent: polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF) increase both SN2 and E2 by freeing the anion, but for weakly basic nucleophiles they favour substitution; protic solvents (H2O, EtOH) favour ionisation, so SN1/E1 for 3°/2° substrates. For a given base, ethanol rather than water favours E2.
  • Concentration: high concentration of strong base favours bimolecular pathways (E2/SN2) over SN1/E1.

16.4 Decision flow chart

  • Step 1. Classify the carbon: methyl, 1°, 2°, 3°, allylic/benzylic, vinyl/aryl (the last: no SN1/SN2/E2 under ordinary conditions).
  • Step 2. Classify the reagent: strong base? hindered? good Nu/weak base? neutral/weak?
  • Step 3. Methyl or 1°: good Nu or small strong base → SN2; hindered strong base (t-BuOK) → E2; 1° β-branched with strong base → E2 mostly (with substitution).
  • Step 4. 2°: strong base → E2 (Zaitsev with EtO−, Hofmann with t-BuOK); good Nu/weak base → SN2; weak Nu in protic solvent, heat → SN1 + E1 (check rearrangement).
  • Step 5. 3°: strong base → E2; weak base/neutral → SN1 + E1 (more E1 as T rises).
  • Step 6. Stereochemistry/regiochemistry: SN2 inversion; SN1 racemisation; E2 anti-periplanar, Zaitsev/Hofmann.

Example 30 Main

Predict the major product

Predict the major organic product: (a) CH3CH2CH2Br + NaOCH3/CH3OH (b) (CH3)2CHCH2CH2Br + t-BuOK (c) (CH3)3CBr + CH3OH, 25 °C (d) 2-bromopentane + NaI/acetone (e) 3-bromo-3-methylpentane + NaOEt/EtOH.

Show solution
  1. (a) 1°, small strong Nu: SN2: CH3CH2CH2OCH3 (1-methoxypropane).
  2. (b) 1° with bulky base: E2: 3-methylbut-1-ene.
  3. (c) 3°, neutral methanol: SN1/E1: tert-butyl methyl ether (MTBE) + isobutene (minor).
  4. (d) 2°, good Nu/weak base, aprotic: SN2 with inversion: 2-iodopentane.
  5. (e) 3° with EtO−: E2, Zaitsev: 3-methylpent-2-ene (E and Z; E major) and some 2-ethylbut-1-ene (Hofmann).
Example 31 Adv

Hofmann or Zaitsev?

Predict the major alkene: (a) 2-bromo-3-methylbutane + EtONa (b) 2-bromo-2,3-dimethylbutane + t-BuOK (c) 2-fluorobutane + EtONa (d) 2-bromobutane + EtONa; also give the trans/cis ratio idea for (d).

Show solution
  1. (a) Zaitsev with a rearranged cation not relevant (E2): lose H from C3 (tertiary C–H): 2-methylbut-2-ene (major) vs 3-methylbut-1-ene (minor).
  2. (b) Bulky base: Hofmann: 2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene (major) over tetrasubstituted 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene.
  3. (c) Poor LG fluoride: Hofmann: but-1-ene major.
  4. (d) trans-but-2-ene (major, ≈ 60 %), cis-but-2-ene (≈ 20 %), but-1-ene (≈ 19 %).
Example 32 Adv

Same substrate, different reagents

For 2-bromo-2-methylbutane write the major product with (i) H2O, warm (ii) NaOEt/EtOH (iii) t-BuOK/t-BuOH (iv) NaI/acetone.

Show solution
  1. (i) Weak Nu, protic solvent: SN1 + E1. 2-Methylbutan-2-ol is the major product at low temperature; the share of 2-methylbut-2-ene grows with temperature.
  2. (ii) E2, Zaitsev: 2-methylbut-2-ene.
  3. (iii) E2, bulky base, Hofmann: 2-methylbut-1-ene.
  4. (iv) Tertiary halide: SN2 is impossible, and iodide is a poor base for E2, so there is essentially no useful reaction (only slow SN1/E1 ionisation).
Example 33 Adv

Count alkenes

How many different alkenes, counting stereoisomers, can form by dehydrobromination of 2-bromo-3-methylpentane (taken as a mixture of all its stereoisomers)?

Show solution
  1. CH3–CHBr–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3. The β-hydrogens are on C1 (the CH3) and on C3 (one H).
  2. Loss from C1 gives 3-methylpent-1-ene. C3 is a stereocentre in this alkene (H, CH3, C2H5, vinyl) and is untouched by the reaction, so with all substrate stereoisomers present both R and S alkenes form: 2.
  3. Loss from C3 gives 3-methylpent-2-ene as E and Z: 2.
  4. Total 4 (2 constitutional alkenes).
