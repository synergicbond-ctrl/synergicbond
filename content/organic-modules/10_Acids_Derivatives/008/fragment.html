8. Nucleophilic acyl substitution (the central mechanism)

8.1 The mechanism: addition then elimination

Unlike the SN2 reaction of an alkyl halide (one step, inversion), substitution at an acyl carbon takes two steps through a tetrahedral intermediate:

+ Nu⁻ (addition)⟶ − Cl⁻ (elimination)⟶
General pattern shown with water/hydroxide as Nu; the C=O is regenerated and the leaving group expelled
  • Step 1 (usually rate-determining for acyl derivatives with poor leaving groups): the nucleophile adds to the carbonyl carbon (sp2 → sp3), pushing the π electrons onto oxygen: tetrahedral alkoxide.
  • Step 2: the C=O is rebuilt and the best leaving group among the three groups on carbon is expelled. (The nucleophile itself or the original group may leave; only if Y− is a better leaving group than Nu− does net substitution occur.)
  • Net change: Y replaced by Nu. Acid catalysis (protonation of C=O, or of the leaving group) accelerates both steps; base catalysis supplies a better nucleophile.

Evidence: (i) 18O exchange: ester hydrolysis in H218O/OH− incorporates 18O into the unreacted ester before it is consumed, which requires a reversible tetrahedral intermediate that can lose either OH− or OR−; (ii) the rate depends on the electrophilicity of the carbonyl and on the leaving group; (iii) rates are second order (first order in ester and in OH−).

Key idea Rule: a nucleophile attacks the acyl derivative only when it is a stronger base (poorer leaving group) than the group it replaces, so substitution always goes “downhill” in basicity of the leaving group: acid chloride → anhydride → ester ≈ acid → amide. The reverse (amide → acid chloride) cannot be done directly.

Two factors operate together and the leaving group ability is the more decisive for the order above: (1) resonance stabilisation of the starting derivative by donation from Y (strongest for NH2, weak for Cl) lowers the electrophilicity of C=O and costs energy to break; (2) inductive withdrawal from the carbonyl carbon (strongest for Cl, O in anhydride) raises electrophilicity; and (3) the stability of Y− as a leaving group (Cl− ≫ RCOO− > RO− > NH2−).

Trap Amides are less reactive than esters, but amines are better nucleophiles than alcohols. So converting an ester to an amide (aminolysis) is easy, while converting an amide to an ester needs harsh acid conditions. Another trap: the order for hydrolysis rate with acid is not identical to that with base for substituted benzoates; follow the general order unless data are given.

Example 18 Main

Which transformation works?

Which of these can be done in one step under normal conditions? (A) amide → acid chloride; (B) acid chloride → ester; (C) ester → anhydride; (D) anhydride → amide; (E) ester → amide.

Show solution
  1. Only “downhill” changes work. (B) yes (alcohol + pyridine). (D) yes (NH3). (E) yes (NH3; the amide is the more stable product, so aminolysis goes forward, though slowly).
  2. (A) no: Cl− is a poorer base than NH2−. (C) no.
  3. Answer: B, D, E.

8.4 Acid- and base-catalysed substitution

  • Base: converts the nucleophile into a stronger one (RO− instead of ROH, OH− instead of H2O). But HO− in saponification of an ester is a reagent, not just a catalyst, since the carboxylic acid product is deprotonated and the reaction becomes irreversible.
  • Acid: protonates the carbonyl oxygen (making C more electrophilic) and, at the tetrahedral stage, protonates the leaving group (OR′ → +HOR′). Typical for esters, amides and nitriles, where the leaving group is poor.
  • Pyridine or Et3N: with acyl chlorides and anhydrides as an HCl trap (and as a nucleophilic catalyst, via a reactive N-acylpyridinium ion). DMAP is a more powerful catalyst.
Example 19 Adv

Order of reactivity with hydroxide

Arrange in decreasing rate of hydrolysis by dilute NaOH (treat the amide as the slowest of all): CH3COCl, CH3COOCH3, CH3CONH2, (CH3CO)2O, C6H5COOCH3, CH3COOC(CH3)3.

Show solution
  1. Acyl chloride > anhydride > esters > amide. Among esters: the phenyl group of methyl benzoate conjugates with C=O (lowering electrophilicity) so it is slower than methyl acetate; tert-butyl acetate is much slower under basic conditions because of steric hindrance to attack at the carbonyl through the bulky alkoxy group.
  2. CH3COCl > (CH3CO)2O > CH3COOCH3 > C6H5COOCH3 > CH3COOC(CH3)3 > CH3CONH2 (by convention the amide is placed last).
