2.1 Crystallisation

The most common method for solid organic compounds. The impure solid is dissolved in the minimum quantity of a hot solvent in which it is sparingly soluble in the cold but very soluble when hot. The hot solution is filtered (to remove insoluble impurities), then cooled; the pure compound crystallises while the soluble impurities stay in the cold solvent (the mother liquor, because impurities are present in much smaller amount than the main compound and remain below their saturation limit). The steps, in order, with the reason for each:

Why It Happens Crystal growth is selective: a growing lattice incorporates its own molecules far more readily than foreign ones, so slow growth excludes impurities, whereas rapid precipitation traps mother liquor and impurity inside and between tiny crystals.
  • Choose the solvent: the compound should dissolve hot and not cold; impurities should dissolve in the cold solvent, or not dissolve at all; the solvent must not react with the compound and should be easily removed (low b.p.). Common solvents: water, ethanol, ethanol–water, benzene or toluene, petroleum ether, acetic acid.
  • Dissolve in the minimum amount of boiling solvent, so that the solution is saturated hot and most of the compound crystallises on cooling (excess solvent reduces the yield).
  • If the solution is coloured by resinous impurities add activated charcoal (animal charcoal), boil briefly and filter hot. Charcoal adsorbs coloured high-molecular-mass impurities.
  • Filter hot through fluted filter paper and a short-stem (or stemless) funnel, so that the solution does not cool in the stem and crystallise there.
  • Cool slowly; slow cooling gives large, pure crystals. A rapid chill gives tiny crystals that trap mother liquor. If crystals do not appear, scratch the glass or add a seed crystal.
  • Collect the crystals by suction filtration, wash with a little ice-cold solvent, and dry (in air or a desiccator, or a low-temperature oven).
Exam Trap Wrong answers about crystallisation: the solvent should NOT dissolve the compound at room temperature (yield would be lost); too much solvent lowers yield; the charcoal is used only for coloured impurities and is removed in the hot filtration, never added to a boiling solution suddenly (it makes the liquid froth over).

Solvent boiling point and "oiling out". The boiling point of the solvent should lie below the melting point of the compound. If the compound melts in the hot solvent instead of dissolving, it separates on cooling as an oil that traps impurities rather than as crystals. When no single solvent has a steep solubility curve, a solvent pair is used: dissolve the compound in a minimum of the hot solvent in which it is freely soluble (for example ethanol), then add hot the second, miscible solvent in which it is sparingly soluble (water) drop by drop until a faint turbidity just appears; clear the turbidity with a drop of the first solvent and cool. Fractional crystallisation separates two solids that have different solubilities in the same solvent (for example KClO3 and KCl, or two sugars): on cooling the hot saturated solution the less soluble component crystallises first; the filtrate is concentrated and cooled again to give the second. Recrystallisation is simply repeating the process until the melting point no longer changes. Percent recovery in a single crystallisation is always below 100 %, because some compound stays dissolved in the mother liquor.

Example 2 Main

Percentage recovery

A solid is purified by crystallisation from 50 mL of a solvent. 6.0 g of impure solid contains 5.0 g of the desired compound. At 273 K the solubility of the compound is 0.30 g per 50 mL. How many grams crystallise at best, and what is the maximum percentage recovery?

Show solution
  1. The mother liquor (50 mL) holds at most 0.30 g of the desired compound.
  2. Maximum crystals = 5.0 − 0.30 = 4.70 g.
  3. Maximum recovery = 4.70/5.0 × 100 = 94 %. Using more solvent would increase the loss.
