17. Tautomerism

17.1 What tautomers are

Tautomers are constitutional isomers that interconvert rapidly and spontaneously, usually by the migration of a hydrogen atom (as a proton) together with a shift of a double bond. Because the interconversion is fast (acid- or base-catalysed, or on mere standing), the isomers are in dynamic equilibrium and ordinarily cannot be separated. The commonest type is prototropic tautomerism.

IMP Tautomerism ≠ resonance. In resonance the same molecule is described by structures differing only in the positions of electrons (the hybrid is a single species, joined by ↔). In tautomerism two different molecules with different positions of an atom (H) are in equilibrium (joined by ⇌); they have different bond lengths, spectra and (often) reactivity.

17.2 Keto–enol tautomerism

A carbonyl compound with at least one α-hydrogen (H on the sp³ carbon next to C=O) is in equilibrium with its enol (C=C–OH) form.

⇌⟶
Acetone (keto) and its enol (prop-1-en-2-ol): the equilibrium lies far on the keto side
  • Mechanism, base-catalysed: (1) the base removes the α-H giving the enolate ion (resonance-stabilised); (2) the enolate is protonated on O to give the enol (or on C to return to the keto form).
  • Mechanism, acid-catalysed: (1) protonation of the carbonyl oxygen; (2) loss of the α-H to the solvent forms the C=C and the O–H.
B⁻, −BH⟶ BH, −B⁻⟶
Base-catalysed enolisation: enolate intermediate

Keto form is usually more stable for simple aldehydes and ketones, because the C=O bond (≈ 745 kJ mol⁻¹) plus a C–H bond is stronger than the C=C plus O–H: the enol content of acetone and of cyclohexanone is far below 0.01 % (of the order of 10⁻⁶ % for acetone). The enol content rises dramatically when the enol is specially stabilised.

Tautomer pairs
IMP Acetylacetone and ethyl acetoacetate: the 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds have an unusually acidic CH₂ (pKₐ 9 and 11) and a strongly stabilised enol (the C=C is conjugated with the second C=O and the OH···O=C chelate ring). Acetylacetone is about 80 % enol in the pure liquid; ethyl acetoacetate about 8 % (ester C=O is a poorer acceptor and a poorer H-bond acceptor than ketone C=O). Both give a violet/red colour with FeCl₃ (enol/phenol test), decolourise bromine water (enol C=C), and react with sodium, but also give oxime/2,4-DNP derivatives (keto C=O): both forms are present.
Exam Trap Not every carbonyl can enolise. Enolisation needs a hydrogen on an sp³ α-carbon. HCHO, PhCHO, Ph₂CO, (CH₃)₃C–CHO and HCOOH have no α-H. Also, an α-H at a bridgehead of a small bicyclic ketone cannot enolise: the enol would put a double bond at the bridgehead (anti-Bredt).
Illustration 17.1 Main

Can it enolise?

Which of the following can exhibit keto–enol tautomerism: acetaldehyde, benzaldehyde, acetone, benzophenone, 2,2-dimethylpropanal, cyclohexanone, formaldehyde?

  1. A carbonyl compound enolises if it has at least one α-H.
  2. Yes: acetaldehyde (CH₃), acetone (CH₃), cyclohexanone (ring CH₂). No α-H: benzaldehyde, benzophenone, (CH₃)₃CCHO (α-carbon is quaternary), formaldehyde.
  3. Three of the seven.
Illustration 17.2 Main

Draw the enol forms

How many enol forms (constitutional) can butan-2-one (CH₃COCH₂CH₃) give? Also count the geometrical isomers.

  1. The α-carbons are C-1 (CH₃) and C-3 (CH₂): two constitutional enols.
  2. The C-3 enol, CH₃–CH=C(OH)–CH₃, has two different substituents on each double-bond carbon and exists as E and Z isomers; the C-1 enol, CH₂=C(OH)CH₂CH₃, does not.
  3. Total: 2 constitutional (3 counting E and Z separately).

Illustration 17.3 Adv

Order of enol content

Arrange in increasing enol content: acetone, ethyl acetoacetate, acetylacetone, cyclohexanone.

  1. Simple ketones (acetone, cyclohexanone) have negligible enol; ethyl acetoacetate has about 8 %; acetylacetone about 80 %.
  2. acetone ≈ cyclohexanone (negligible) < ethyl acetoacetate < acetylacetone.
Illustration 17.4 Adv

Solvent effect on enol content

Acetylacetone is about 92 % enol in hexane and only about 15 % enol in water. Explain.

  1. In hexane the enol is stabilised by an intramolecular O–H···O hydrogen bond (chelate), which is the dominant intramolecular interaction in a non-polar medium. The enol's dipole moment is also smaller (the two C=O/C–O dipoles partly cancel in the chelate).
  2. In water the keto form (more polar, with two C=O groups free) is solvated by hydrogen bonds to water, and the enol's internal H-bond competes with H-bonds to the solvent; the equilibrium shifts toward the keto form.

HYF How to find the tautomers of any compound: look for the pattern H–X–Y=Z (X, Z = C, N, O, S) and write the isomer X=Y–Z–H. The H moves from the atom X to the atom Z; the double bond moves from Y=Z to X=Y. If X has no hydrogen (or Z is not electronegative enough to bear it) no tautomerism. For the keto form H–C–C=O: X = C(α), Y = C, Z = O.

Illustration 17.6 Adv

Tautomers of a heterocycle

Draw the two tautomers of 2-hydroxypyridine and say which predominates in water.

  1. The H moves between oxygen and ring nitrogen. 2-Pyridone retains a six-π-electron aromatic ring (the N lone pair is in the sextet and C=O is polarised: pyridinium-olate resonance) and has strong intermolecular H-bonding and a larger dipole moment, so it predominates in water and in the solid.
  2. In the gas phase and in non-polar solvents the two forms are of comparable energy and the hydroxy form is slightly favoured.
2-hydroxypyridine (lactim) 2-pyridone (lactam)
Illustration 17.7 Adv

Racemisation and H–D exchange via enolisation

Why does optically active (R)-3-methylpentan-2-one racemise in aqueous base or acid and exchange its α-hydrogens with D in D₂O?

  1. The α-carbon (C-3) is the stereocentre and carries an H. Enolisation removes this H and makes C-3 sp² (planar) in the enol or enolate: the stereo-information is lost. Reprotonation can occur from either face, giving a racemic mixture.
  2. In D₂O the enolate is protonated by D⁺ (deuterium): after repeated enolisation all α-H (C-1 and C-3) are replaced by D.

Illustration 17.8 Adv

Distinguish

How would you distinguish acetone from acetylacetone using a single reagent? Explain the result.

  1. Add neutral FeCl₃ solution: acetylacetone gives an intense red/violet colour (the enol chelates Fe³⁺); acetone gives no colour change because its enol content is negligible.
  2. Alternative: Br₂/CCl₄ is decolourised immediately by acetylacetone (rapid reaction with the enol), whereas acetone reacts only slowly through the acid- or base-catalysed enolisation.
