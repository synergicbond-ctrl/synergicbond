Exercise set 3: Integration

Questions that combine several concepts: stability data and orbital arguments, quantitative energetics, product prediction, multi-step synthesis and mechanism drawing. Hydride affinities are in kcal mol−1; a smaller value means a more stable cation.

A. Stability and structure (Questions 170 to 192)

Question 170 Basic

Ranking cations from data

The gas-phase hydride affinities are cumyl 220, tert-butyl 230, benzyl 233, isopropyl 247, allyl 256, ethyl 274. Rank the cations from most to least stable and say which of the conjugated cations are more stable than a saturated cation of the same degree of substitution.

Show answer

Cumyl > tert-butyl > benzyl > isopropyl > allyl > ethyl. The tertiary benzylic cation (cumyl) is more stable than tert-butyl, and the primary benzyl cation (233) is more stable than the secondary isopropyl cation, close to the tertiary alkyl cation. The primary allyl cation (256), however, is *less* stable than isopropyl (247), though more stable than ethyl (274), a primary alkyl cation. Conjugation with a phenyl group stabilises a primary centre more than conjugation with a vinyl group does.

Question 171 Advanced

Solution versus gas phase

The energy difference between tertiary and secondary alkyl cations is about 17 kcal mol−1 in the gas phase and about 9.5 kcal mol−1 for ionisation in a non-nucleophilic solvent. Convert both to kJ mol−1 and explain the smaller value in solution.

Show answer

17 kcal mol−1 = 71 kJ mol−1 and 9.5 kcal mol−1 = 40 kJ mol−1. In solution the solvent stabilises the positive charge; the less substituted, more localised secondary cation is stabilised by solvation more than the tertiary cation, whose charge is already dispersed over the alkyl groups, so solvation reduces (levels) the intrinsic difference.

Question 172 Basic

Using pKR+

The pKR+ of the tropylium ion is +4.7. Calculate the ratio [C7H7+]/[C7H7OH] in water at pH 2.0, and say what the ratio would be at pH 7.

Show answer

pKR+ is the pH at which the cation and carbinol are present in equal amounts, so [R+]/[ROH] = 10pKR+ − pH. At pH 2.0: 102.7 = about 500 : 1 (the cation is the main species). At pH 7: 10−2.3 = about 1 : 200 (mostly the alcohol).

Question 173 Advanced

Effect of a methoxy group

The pKR+ values of triphenylmethyl and 4-methoxytriphenylmethyl cations are −6.63 and −3.40. By what factor does the 4-methoxy group change the ratio [R+]/[ROH] at a given acidity? What does this show about the stabilisation?

Show answer

The ratio changes by 103.23 = about 1700. The oxygen lone pair delocalises into the ring and the cationic carbon, so the cation is stabilised by about 3.23 × 5.7 = 18 kJ mol−1 relative to the alcohol, compared with the unsubstituted triphenylmethyl cation.

Question 174 Advanced

Hückel delocalisation energy

Use Hückel energies to calculate the π delocalisation energy (in units of β) of (a) the allyl cation relative to ethene plus an empty p orbital, and (b) buta-1,3-diene relative to two isolated ethene π bonds (the Hückel energies are α + 1.414β, α, α − 1.414β for allyl and α + 1.618β, α + 0.618β, α − 0.618β, α − 1.618β for butadiene; an ethene π bond has α + β).

Show answer

(a) Allyl cation: 2(1.414) = 2.828β, against 2(1) = 2β for ethene with an empty p orbital: delocalisation energy 0.83β. The allyl radical and anion have the same value, since the extra electrons go into the nonbonding orbital. (b) Butadiene: 2(1.618) + 2(0.618) = 4.472β against 4β for two ethene units: 0.47β.

Question 175 Advanced

Aromatic or antiaromatic?

The Hückel total π energy of each ring and of the open chain with the same number of atoms and π electrons (in units of β) is: cyclopropenyl cation (2 e) 4.000 and 2.828; cyclopropenyl anion (4 e) 2.000 and 2.828; cyclopentadienyl cation (4 e) 5.236 and 5.464; cyclopentadienyl anion (6 e) 6.472 and 5.464; tropylium (6 e) 8.988 and 8.055. Classify each ring.

Show answer

A ring is aromatic if its π energy is greater (more stabilising, larger in units of β) than that of the open chain, antiaromatic if smaller. Aromatic: cyclopropenyl cation (+1.172), cyclopentadienyl anion (+1.008), tropylium (+0.933). Antiaromatic: cyclopropenyl anion (−0.828) and cyclopentadienyl cation (−0.228). The pattern follows 4n + 2 (aromatic) and 4n (antiaromatic) π electrons.

Question 176 Advanced

Where does the nucleophile attack?

In the Hückel treatment of the benzyl cation the squared coefficients of the LUMO are 4/7 at the benzylic carbon, 1/7 at each ortho carbon and at the para carbon, and 0 at the meta carbons. Predict the main site of attack of a nucleophile, and the relative spin density at the carbon atoms of the benzyl radical.

Show answer

The nucleophile attacks mainly at the benzylic carbon (57 % of the LUMO density), which also gives a product that retains the aromatic ring; attack at ortho or para carbons would disrupt aromaticity. In the benzyl radical the unpaired electron occupies the same nonbonding orbital, so the spin density is 0.571 at the benzylic carbon, 0.143 at each of the two ortho carbons and at the para carbon, and zero at meta (total 1.000). EPR hyperfine splittings measure this distribution.

Question 177 Advanced

Orbitals of the SN2 reaction

Describe the SN2 reaction of hydroxide with bromomethane in terms of the HOMO of the nucleophile and the LUMO of the substrate. Why is inversion of configuration observed?

Show answer

The HOMO of HO− is an oxygen lone pair; the LUMO of CH3Br is the C–Br antibonding orbital σ*, which has its large lobe at the back of carbon, opposite to bromine. Overlap of the lone pair with this lobe forms the C–O bond as the C–Br bond weakens. During the process carbon changes from tetrahedral (sp3) through a planar sp2-like transition state (the three hydrogens in a plane, with O and Br on opposite sides along the p orbital) to tetrahedral again, with the three substituents turned over: inversion. Front-side attack would meet only the small front lobe and would be repelled by the filled orbitals of bromine.

Question 178 Advanced

The angle of attack at C=O

Why do nucleophiles attack the carbonyl carbon at about 107° to the C=O bond and not at 90°?

Show answer

The LUMO is π*(C=O). It has a node between carbon and oxygen, so its two lobes on carbon are tilted away from the plane perpendicular to the bond rather than parallel. A nucleophile attacking at 90° would overlap with the π* orbital but would be repelled by the filled π bond; the angle of about 107° (close to the tetrahedral angle at which the new bond forms) is a compromise between maximum overlap with π* and minimum repulsion by the filled π orbital.

Question 179 Advanced

Anti-periplanar elimination

cis-4-tert-Butylcyclohexyl bromide reacts with a base in an E2 reaction about 500 times faster than the trans isomer. Explain with chair conformations and calculate the difference in free energy of activation at 298 K.

Show answer

The bulky tert-butyl group holds the ring with that group equatorial. In the cis isomer this places the bromine in the axial position, anti-periplanar to axial hydrogens on both neighbouring carbons, so σ(C–H) can donate into σ*(C–Br) and elimination is easy. In the trans isomer the bromine is equatorial, anti to ring C–C bonds and not to hydrogens, so elimination requires the ring to flip to a conformer with a diaxial tert-butyl group, which is very unfavourable. ΔΔG‡ = RT ln 500 = 8.314 × 298 × 6.21 = 15.4 kJ mol−1.

Question 180 Advanced

Norbornyl solvolysis

exo-2-Norbornyl brosylate acetolyses 350 times faster than the endo isomer, and both give only exo acetate. Calculate ΔΔG‡ at 298 K and explain the observations using σ-participation.

Show answer

ΔΔG‡ = RT ln 350 = 8.314 × 298 × 5.86 = 14.5 kJ mol−1. In the exo isomer the C1–C6 σ bond is anti to the leaving group, so its electrons assist ionisation (σ-participation) and a symmetrical bridged cation forms; the endo isomer has no anti σ bond, ionises unassisted at a normal rate (comparable to cyclohexyl brosylate), so its rate is lower. The nucleophile must attack the bridged ion from the side opposite the bridging bond, which gives exo acetate from either isomer, and an optically active starting material gives racemic product because the intermediate is achiral.

Question 181 Advanced

Bridgehead solvolysis

The relative rates of solvolysis in 80 % ethanol at 25 °C are 1-bromoadamantane 1, 1-bromobicyclo[2.2.2]octane 10−3 and 1-bromobicyclo[2.2.1]heptane 10−10. Calculate the difference in ΔG‡ between the first and last and explain the trend.

Show answer

ΔΔG‡ = RT ln 1010 = 8.314 × 298 × 23.0 = 57 kJ mol−1. The rate falls with the strain energy introduced when the bridgehead carbon must flatten to become an sp2 cation: the adamantyl skeleton can distort most easily, and the norbornane skeleton least. Backside attack (SN2) is impossible at a bridgehead.

Question 182 Advanced

Barriers for shifts in the 2-butyl cation

Hydride exchange between C-2 and C-3 of the 2-butyl cation has a barrier of at most about 2.5 kcal mol−1, scrambling of C-3 and C-4 about 7 to 8 kcal mol−1, and rearrangement to the tert-butyl cation about 18 kcal mol−1. Why is the last so much slower? Estimate the ratio of the rate constants of the hydride shift (2.5) and the rearrangement to tert-butyl (18) at 298 K.

Show answer

The conversion to tert-butyl must pass through a primary cation, which lies about 20 kcal mol−1 above the secondary cation, so the barrier is high; a hydride shift between two secondary carbons goes through a bridged structure and needs very little energy. Ratio = exp[(18 − 2.5) × 4184/(8.314 × 298)] = exp(26.2) ≈ 2 × 1011.

Question 183 Advanced

Product from superacid

Predict the cation observed when each alcohol is dissolved in FSO3H–SbF5–SO2 and the temperature is raised: (a) 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (b) butan-2-ol, (c) 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol (neopentyl alcohol).

Show answer

(a) The tert-butyl cation by ionisation of the protonated tertiary alcohol. (b) Butan-2-ol is protonated first and ionises at higher temperature with rearrangement to the tert-butyl cation. (c) The neopentyl cation rearranges (methyl shift) to the tert-pentyl (2-methyl-2-butyl) cation. In the absence of nucleophiles the cations are long-lived and accumulate as the most stable isomer.

Question 184 Basic

Singlet or triplet from data

Classify each carbene as singlet or triplet and give the reason: (a) the N-heterocyclic carbene with an X-ray bond angle of 102° and no ESR signal; (b) a carbene with a bond angle of 135° that gives an ESR signal; (c) :CCl2.

Show answer

(a) Singlet: small angle (100 to 110°), all electrons paired, no ESR signal. (b) Triplet: angle in the range 130 to 150° and unpaired electrons (ESR active). (c) Singlet: chlorine lone pairs donate into the empty p orbital and lower the energy of the singlet state.

Question 185 Advanced

Singlet–triplet energy gap

For most carbenes the triplet is the ground state and the singlet lies about 40 kJ mol−1 higher. If a mixture were at thermal equilibrium at 298 K, what fraction of excited singlet relative to triplet would be present? Why can a triplet carbene nevertheless react as a singlet when it is formed by loss of N2 from a diazoalkane?

Show answer

Ratio = exp(−40000/(8.314 × 298)) = 1.0 × 10−7, so the equilibrium population of the singlet is negligible. But the carbene is formed with all electrons paired, i.e. as a singlet, and interconversion of spin states requires collisions with other molecules (spin flip); if the carbene reacts faster than it flips, it reacts as a singlet, which is why methylene from diazomethane can add stereospecifically.

Question 186 Advanced

Orbital picture of cyclopropanation

Explain why a singlet carbene approaches an alkene "sideways" and not along the axis through the centre of the C=C bond.

Show answer

The carbene HOMO (filled sp2) must overlap with the alkene LUMO (π*), and the carbene LUMO (empty p) with the alkene HOMO (π). In a direct, head-on approach the filled π orbital meets the empty p orbital constructively on one side but the sp2 and π* orbitals have an antibonding overlap, so the two bonding interactions cannot both be achieved. Approaching sideways (the carbene axis roughly parallel to the C=C bond) gives bonding overlap in both interactions, and the carbene then rotates through about 90° as the two new C–C bonds form.

Question 187 Advanced

Enolate HOMO and charge

In an enolate the negative charge is larger on oxygen but the HOMO has the larger coefficient on carbon. Use this to predict the site of reaction of an enolate with (a) CH3I and (b) (CH3)3SiCl.

Show answer

(a) Iodomethane is a soft electrophile with a good leaving group on sp3 carbon; the reaction is controlled by overlap with the HOMO, so it occurs at carbon (C-methylation). (b) Chlorotrimethylsilane is a hard electrophile; the reaction is controlled by charge, so it occurs at oxygen and gives the silyl enol ether.

Question 188 Advanced

Why radicals do not undergo 1,2-alkyl shifts

Explain, in terms of orbital occupancy, why a 1,2-shift of a methyl group is easy in a carbocation but unusual in a radical.

Show answer

In the cation the migrating group moves through a three-centre transition state in which three atoms share two electrons, both in a bonding orbital. In a radical the same three-centre arrangement holds three electrons: two fill the bonding orbital and the third must occupy an antibonding orbital, which raises the energy of the transition state. Aryl and vinyl groups can migrate because they do so by addition to their π system to give a bridged intermediate (a cyclopropane-like radical) rather than through this three-centre transition state.

Question 189 Advanced

The LUMO of benzyne

(a) Describe the extra bond of benzyne. (b) Why is benzyne electrophilic? (c) Why must a nucleophile attack in the plane of the ring?

Show answer

(a) It is an abnormal π bond formed by weak overlap of two sp2 orbitals in the plane of the ring, perpendicular to the aromatic π system, which is left intact. (b) The bond is weak, so its antibonding orbital (the LUMO) is low in energy and accepts electrons from nucleophiles. (c) The LUMO lies in the plane of the ring, so the nucleophile HOMO can overlap with it only by approaching in that plane, alongside any ortho substituent, which is why the reaction is sensitive to steric hindrance.

Question 190 Advanced

Orbitals of the Diels–Alder reaction

The HOMO of butadiene has terminal coefficients +0.60 and −0.60, the LUMO of ethene +0.71 and −0.71. Explain, using the signs, why the reaction is allowed, and why the LUMO of the diene (terminal coefficients +0.60 and +0.60) would not react with the LUMO of ethene.

Show answer

For a suprafacial [4+2] reaction the two new σ bonds must form between termini of the same sign of the two interacting orbitals. HOMO(diene) has opposite signs at C-1 and C-4, and LUMO(ethene) has opposite signs at its two carbons, so both bonds form in phase simultaneously: the reaction is allowed. Two LUMOs are both empty, so there are no electrons to donate and no bonding stabilisation at all; the interaction of an empty with an empty orbital contributes nothing.

Question 191 Basic

Nucleophilic constants

Using the nucleophilic constants n toward methyl iodide in methanol (CH3O− 6.3, C6H5S− 9.9, I− 7.4, Cl− 4.4, (C2H5)3P 8.7, (C2H5)3N 6.7), calculate how many times faster (a) thiophenoxide reacts than methoxide, (b) iodide than chloride, (c) the phosphine than the amine.

Show answer

The ratio is 10Δn. (a) 109.9 − 6.3 = 103.6 ≈ 4000. (b) 107.4 − 4.4 = 103.0 = 1000. (c) 108.7 − 6.7 = 102.0 = 100. Large, polarisable atoms with high-lying HOMOs (S, I, P) are far better nucleophiles towards sp3 carbon than their basicity suggests.

Question 192 Basic

Leaving groups from pKa

Use the pKa values of the conjugate acids (HI −10, HBr −9, HCl −7, HF 3.2, H2O 15.7) to rank the leaving groups I−, Br−, Cl−, F−, HO− and explain why an alcohol is protonated before it undergoes SN1 or SN2 substitution.

Show answer

B. Acidity and carbanions: further problems (Questions 193 to 201)

Question 193 Basic

Ranking carbon acids in DMSO

Rank in decreasing acidity (pKa in DMSO in brackets): ethyl acetate (30.5), nitromethane (17.2), acetone (26.5), acetonitrile (31.3), pentane-2,4-dione (13.3), N,N-diethylacetamide (34.5).

Show answer

Pentane-2,4-dione (13.3) > nitromethane (17.2) > acetone (26.5) > ethyl acetate (30.5) > acetonitrile (31.3) > N,N-diethylacetamide (34.5). Two carbonyl groups share the charge in pentane-2,4-dione; nitro is the best single stabilising group; among carbonyl groups the ketone is more effective than the ester and the amide because in the ester and amide the heteroatom donates to the carbonyl group and reduces its ability to accept the enolate charge.

Question 194 Advanced

Solvent dependence of acidity

Nitromethane has pKa 10 in water and 17.2 in DMSO; diethyl malonate has 13 (estimated) in water and 16.4 in DMSO. Which is the stronger acid in each solvent, and why does the order change?

Show answer

In water nitromethane is the stronger acid (10 against 13); in DMSO diethyl malonate is stronger (16.4 against 17.2). The nitronate ion has its charge localised on two oxygen atoms and is strongly stabilised by hydrogen bonding in water; DMSO has no hydrogen-bond donors, so this extra stabilisation is lost and the more delocalised malonate enolate becomes more favourable. Comparisons of carbon-acid strength are valid only on one scale.

Question 195 Advanced

Equilibrium constant for deprotonation

Acetone (pKa 26.5 in DMSO) is treated with sodium hydride and with the conjugate base of diisopropylamine. Why can a base whose conjugate acid has a pKa below 26.5 never convert acetone completely into its enolate? Estimate K for acetone with a base whose conjugate acid has pKa 22.6 (the fluorenide anion) in DMSO.

Show answer

Complete deprotonation requires that the conjugate acid of the base be a much weaker acid than the ketone (pKa well above 26.5). K = 10pKa(BH) − pKa(AH) = 1022.6 − 26.5 = 10−3.9 ≈ 1 × 10−4: only a minute fraction of the acetone is converted into enolate at equilibrium.

Question 196 Basic

Why cyclopentadiene is acidic

(a) By how many pKa units (DMSO) is cyclopentadiene more acidic than diphenylmethane? (b) By how much more than propene (38 in DMF)?

Show answer

(a) 32.3 − 18.1 = 14.2 units. (b) 38 − 18.1 = about 20 units (taking values from different solvents, so approximate). The cyclopentadienide ion is a planar aromatic anion (6 π electrons), and diphenylmethane and propene give anions stabilised by delocalisation but not aromatic.

Question 197 Advanced

Cycloheptatriene and cyclopentadiene

Why is cycloheptatriene (pKa 39) barely more acidic than propene (43), although cyclopentadiene is 20 pKa units more acidic than propene?

Show answer

Cyclopentadiene gives a planar 6 π-electron anion (aromatic). Cycloheptatriene would give a planar anion with 8 π electrons (4n), which is antiaromatic and no more stable than an open-chain conjugated anion, so it is only slightly more acidic than propene. Cycloheptatriene prefers to lose hydride to give the aromatic 6 π-electron tropylium cation.

Question 198 Basic

Deuterium exchange

Acetone is dissolved in D2O containing NaOD. What is the final product and why?

Show answer

Hexadeuterioacetone, CD3COCD3. Each α-hydrogen is removed reversibly to give the enolate, which is deuterated by D2O; repetition exchanges all six α-hydrogens.

Question 199 Advanced

Stereochemistry of a carbanion

In the base-catalysed cleavage of a chiral ketone that gives 2-phenylbutane, the hydrocarbon has 93 % net retention with potassium t-butoxide in benzene, 48 % net inversion with potassium hydroxide in ethylene glycol, and is racemic in DMSO. Interpret.

Show answer

The carbanion is an intermediate whose fate depends on the medium. In benzene (non-polar) it exists as a tight ion pair that is protonated from the side where the proton donor is already bound, before the carbanion can be symmetrically solvated: retention. In ethylene glycol a good proton donor delivers the proton rapidly to an unsymmetrically solvated carbanion from the side opposite the departed group: inversion. DMSO separates the ion pair and the carbanion becomes symmetrically solvated before protonation: racemisation.

Question 201 Advanced

Claisen condensation: why a full equivalent of base?

Ethyl acetate with catalytic sodium ethoxide gives very little ethyl acetoacetate, but with one equivalent the yield is good. Explain using pKa values (ethyl acetate 30.5, ethyl acetoacetate 14.2 in DMSO).

Show answer

The condensation steps up to ethyl acetoacetate are reversible and the equilibrium lies on the side of the starting ester. The product has an acidic CH2 between two carbonyl groups (14.2 against 30.5 for the ester, a difference of 16 units); ethoxide deprotonates it essentially completely, which removes the product from the equilibrium and drives the reaction. This consumes the base, so a full equivalent is needed.

Question 202 Basic

Radical stability from BDE

Use C–H bond dissociation enthalpies (kcal mol−1): CH3–H 105, C2H5–H 101, (CH3)2CH–H 99, (CH3)3C–H 96, allyl–H 89, benzyl–H 90, vinyl–H 111, phenyl–H 113. Rank the radicals by stability and give the energy by which the tert-butyl radical is more stable than the methyl radical, in kJ mol−1.

Question 203 Advanced

Halogenation energetics

Using the bond dissociation enthalpies in section 7 (kcal mol−1: C–H of methane 105, H–Cl 103, H–Br 88, Cl–Cl 57, Br–Br 45, CH3–Cl 84, CH3–Br 72), calculate ΔH for (a) the overall chlorination and (b) the overall bromination of methane, and (c) the ΔH of each first propagation step.

(a) (105 + 57) − (84 + 103) = −25 kcal mol−1. (b) (105 + 45) − (72 + 88) = −10 kcal mol−1. (c) Cl• + CH4: 105 − 103 = +2; Br• + CH4: 105 − 88 = +17. Bromination is much less exothermic and its first step is endothermic; this is why it is slower and, through a late transition state, more selective.

Question 204 Advanced

Selectivity per hydrogen

In the chlorination of propane at 25 °C the products are 40 % 1-chloropropane and 60 % 2-chloropropane; in the bromination at 125 °C they are 3 % and 97 %. Calculate the reactivity of a secondary hydrogen relative to a primary hydrogen in each case.

Divide each percentage by the number of hydrogens (6 primary, 2 secondary). Chlorination: (60/2)/(40/6) = 30/6.67 = 4.5. Bromination: (97/2)/(3/6) = 48.5/0.5 = 97.

Question 205 Advanced

Predicting a product distribution

Assume that the secondary : primary reactivity per hydrogen is the same in butane as in propane. Predict the percentage of 2-halobutane in (a) the chlorination (4.5) and (b) the bromination (97) of butane.

Question 206 Advanced

Why is bromination selective?

The enthalpies of hydrogen abstraction by Br• are +16.5 (methane), +10.5 (ethane), +7.0 (propane, secondary C–H) and +3.5 (isobutane, tertiary C–H) kcal mol−1. How do these numbers explain the selectivity, and why is chlorination (abstraction exothermic) less selective?

Show answer

The abstraction is endothermic, so by the Hammond postulate the transition state is late and resembles the product radical: nearly the whole difference in C–H bond strength between the types of hydrogen (about 3 to 4 kcal mol−1 per step down the series) appears in the activation energy. For chlorine the abstraction is exothermic, the transition state is early and resembles the reactants (an alkane and a chlorine atom), so only a small part of the energy difference appears in the barrier.

Question 207 Advanced

Polar effect in chlorination

Explain why the radical chlorination of butanenitrile, CH3CH2CH2CN, occurs mainly at the β and γ carbons and not at the α carbon, although the α-radical is stabilised by the cyano group.

Show answer

The chlorine atom is an electrophilic radical and its transition state is early, so electrostatic and polar factors control the selectivity. The α-hydrogens are on a carbon made electron-poor by the cyano group, and attack there is disfavoured; the effect outweighs the stabilisation of the α-radical, which matters little in an early transition state.

Question 208 Advanced

Propagation steps of the peroxide effect

Using BDE values (C–H of a secondary carbon 99; H–Cl 103; H–Br 88; H–I 71 kcal mol−1), calculate ΔH for R• + HX → RH + X• for X = Cl, Br, I and explain which halogen acid can sustain a radical chain addition to alkenes.

Show answer

ΔH = BDE(H–X) − BDE(R–H) with sign reversed: X = Cl: 99 − 103 = +4 (endothermic); X = Br: 99 − 88 = −11 (exothermic); X = I: 99 − 71 = −28 (very exothermic). For HI the other propagation step, addition of I• to the alkene, is endothermic and too slow; for HCl the hydrogen-transfer step is endothermic; HBr is exothermic in both steps, so only HBr adds by a chain mechanism.

Question 209 Advanced

Stereochemistry of radical addition of HBr

The radical addition of HBr to a cyclic alkene gives mainly the anti addition product, and radical bromination of optically active 2-bromobutane to 2,3-dibromobutane gives mostly racemic product. What intermediate do these results suggest and why?

Show answer

A bridged bromine radical, in which bromine is bonded to both carbons of the former double bond (like a bromonium ion with an extra electron). A freely rotating or inverting planar radical would lose stereochemistry in addition (syn and anti would be mixed) and would give no preferred anti product; the bridged radical is opened by hydrogen transfer from HBr to the carbon on the side opposite to bromine, giving anti addition, and it explains the racemisation at the stereocentre of 2-bromobutane because bromine can bridge to either carbon.

Question 210 Advanced

Why NBS and not Br2?

Cyclohexene reacts with Br2 in the dark to give 1,2-dibromocyclohexane but with NBS in CCl4 under light to give 3-bromocyclohexene. Explain the role of NBS.

Show answer

Molecular bromine adds to a C=C bond by an ionic bromonium mechanism that is second order in Br2, so it is very concentration-dependent. NBS reacts with the HBr formed in the radical chain to regenerate Br2 at a very low steady concentration; at this low concentration the ionic addition is slow, and the radical abstraction of the weak allylic C–H bond (89 kcal mol−1) followed by capture of the allylic radical by Br2 wins.

Question 211 Basic

Autoxidation of ethers

Why must diethyl ether not be distilled to dryness after long storage in air?

Show answer

Oxygen oxidises the ether at the α-position in a radical chain (the α-radical is stabilised by the oxygen lone pair) to give hydroperoxides, which are less volatile than the ether and concentrate in the residue, which can explode.

Question 212 Advanced

Cumene process

Write the radical chain for the autoxidation of cumene and say what is formed when the hydroperoxide is treated with acid.

Show answer

Initiation gives the cumyl radical by abstraction of the tertiary benzylic hydrogen. Propagation: cumyl• + O2 → cumyl–OO•; cumyl–OO• + cumene → cumyl–OOH + cumyl•. Acid cleaves the hydroperoxide with migration of phenyl from carbon to oxygen to give phenol and acetone.

D. Rearrangements and mechanisms (Questions 213 to 215)

Question 213 Advanced

Which group leaves in an unsymmetrical pinacol?

Activation energy from two rate constants

Question 249 Mechanism

Acetal formation

Write the mechanism for the acid-catalysed reaction of ethanal with two molecules of methanol to give ethanal dimethyl acetal, and say which species is the electrophile in the second addition.

Show answer
  1. Protonation of the carbonyl oxygen gives an oxocarbenium ion CH3CH=OH+ (the LUMO is lowered, so a weak nucleophile can attack).
  2. Methanol adds to carbon; loss of H+ gives the hemiacetal CH3CH(OH)OCH3.
  3. Protonation of the hemiacetal OH and loss of water gives a second oxocarbenium ion CH3CH=O+CH3, which is the electrophile of the second addition.
  4. Methanol adds and loss of H+ gives CH3CH(OCH3)2. Every step is reversible; water is removed (or the alcohol is used in excess) to drive the reaction, and acid hydrolyses the acetal back.
Question 250 Mechanism

Imine formation

Write the mechanism for the reaction of cyclohexanone with methylamine to give the imine, and explain why the optimum pH is about 4 to 5.

Show answer
  1. The amine nitrogen attacks the carbonyl carbon (Bürgi–Dunitz approach); proton transfers give the carbinolamine (a hemiaminal).
  2. The OH of the carbinolamine is protonated and water leaves, assisted by the nitrogen lone pair, to give the iminium ion C=N+HCH3.
  3. Loss of H+ from nitrogen gives the imine.
  4. At low pH most of the amine is protonated and has no lone pair to attack; at high pH there is not enough acid to protonate the OH of the carbinolamine and convert it into a good leaving group (water). The rate is greatest at an intermediate pH.
Question 251 Mechanism

SN1 with arrows

Draw the mechanism for the hydrolysis of 2-bromo-2-methylpropane in water, with curved arrows, and identify the rate-determining step.

Show answer
  1. Arrow 1: the C–Br bonding pair moves to bromine; the carbon becomes the planar cation (CH3)3C+ (rate-determining ionisation).
  2. Arrow 2: a lone pair of H2O attacks the empty p orbital of the cation, forming a C–O bond and giving (CH3)3COH2+.

Question 252 Mechanism

E1 dehydration

Write the mechanism for the dehydration of 2-methylbutan-2-ol with concentrated H2SO4 and say which alkene is the major product and why.

Show answer
  1. A lone pair on oxygen takes a proton from H2SO4, giving the oxonium ion (CH3)2C(OH2+)CH2CH3.
  2. The C–O bond breaks (the pair goes to oxygen), giving water and the tertiary cation; this is the slow step.
  3. HSO4− or water removes a β-proton, and the C–H pair becomes the π bond.
  4. The major product is 2-methylbut-2-ene (trisubstituted, more stable alkene, Zaitsev); 2-methylbut-1-ene is the minor product.
Question 253 Mechanism

Saponification

Write the mechanism for the base hydrolysis of methyl benzoate and explain why the reaction is irreversible.

Show answer
  1. HO− attacks the carbonyl carbon (π electrons move to oxygen) to give the tetrahedral alkoxide.
  2. The C=O re-forms by pushing out methoxide, giving benzoic acid.
  3. Methoxide (or hydroxide) removes the acidic O–H proton of benzoic acid (pKa about 4) to give benzoate and methanol.
  4. The last step is essentially irreversible because the carboxylate is a very weak acceptor of nucleophiles and the acid–base equilibrium lies far on the product side, so the whole sequence goes to completion.
Question 254 Mechanism

Wittig reaction

Write the mechanism for the reaction of cyclohexanone with methylenetriphenylphosphorane, Ph3P=CH2, and say what the driving force is.

Show answer
  1. The nucleophilic carbon of the ylide attacks the carbonyl carbon, giving a betaine (or directly a four-membered oxaphosphetane).
  2. The oxygen anion bonds to phosphorus to give the oxaphosphetane ring.
  3. The ring collapses: the C–C and P–O bonds of the ring break and the C=C and P=O bonds form, giving methylenecyclohexane and Ph3P=O.
  4. The formation of the very strong P=O bond is the driving force.
Question 255 Mechanism

Nitration

Draw the mechanism for the nitration of benzene, including the formation of the electrophile, and explain why the proton loss is fast.

Show answer
  1. HNO3 + H2SO4 → H2NO3+ + HSO4−; H2NO3+ → H2O + NO2+ (linear nitronium ion).
  2. The π bond of benzene (HOMO) attacks the nitrogen of NO2+, giving the arenium ion with the positive charge delocalised over three carbons; aromaticity is lost (rate-determining step).
  3. HSO4− removes the proton from the sp3 carbon; the C–H pair becomes part of the π system and aromaticity is restored. This step is fast because it regains the aromatic stabilisation.
Question 256 Mechanism

Bromonium ion opening

Write the mechanism for the reaction of cyclohexene with bromine to give trans-1,2-dibromocyclohexane and explain why the cis isomer is not formed.

Show answer
  1. The π bond attacks one bromine atom of Br2; the Br–Br bond breaks; a lone pair of the bonded bromine moves to the other carbon to form the bridged bromonium ion (three-membered ring) with Br− as the leaving anion.
  2. Bromide attacks one carbon of the bromonium ion from the side opposite the C–Br bond (backside attack on the C–Br σ* orbital), breaking that bond.
  3. Because both bromine atoms end up on opposite faces, the product is the trans isomer (as a racemate: attack at either carbon is equally likely). The cis isomer would need front-side attack, which is not possible.
Question 257 Mechanism

Dichlorocarbene and phenoxide

Write the mechanism of the formation of 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde from phenol, chloroform and sodium hydroxide, showing the species that forms the new C–C bond.

Show answer
