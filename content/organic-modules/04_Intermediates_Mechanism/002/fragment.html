1. What a mechanism is, and the language used to write it

A reaction mechanism is the sequence of elementary steps by which reactants become products, together with the structure of every intermediate and the movement of electrons in each step. An elementary step is a single event with a single transition state. Its molecularity is the number of species that collide or fall apart in that event (one, two, and very rarely three). For an elementary step, and only for an elementary step, the rate law can be written directly from the equation: for A + B → products in one step, rate = k[A][B].

IMP Molecularity belongs to one elementary step and is always a small whole number. Order is an experimental property of the overall rate law and may be zero, fractional or negative. A multistep reaction should be called "second order overall", never "bimolecular", even if its rate law is second order.

A reactive intermediate is a short-lived, high-energy species that occupies a well between two transition states. The six treated in this chapter are the carbocation, carbanion, free radical, carbene, nitrene and aryne. Some members of these families can be stored as salts when substituents stabilise them strongly (triphenylmethyl tetrafluoroborate, for example) but they are still carbocations and react as such.

IMP A reaction with n elementary steps has n transition states and n − 1 intermediates. The rate-determining step is the step whose transition state lies highest above the preceding stable species. Steps before it are fast pre-equilibria, so the rate law contains only the species that appear up to and including the rate-determining step.
Why It Happens An intermediate exists only if it lies in a valley, so that it has a barrier to react in each direction. A species that falls apart or reacts without any barrier is not an intermediate but part of a concerted step, which is why a primary carbocation is not written as an intermediate in the SN2 reaction of a primary halide.

1.2 Bond fission

A covalent bond A–B can break in three ways.

  • Heterolytic fission. Both bonding electrons leave with one fragment: A–B → A+ + :B−. It is favoured by polar solvents that stabilise the ions, by good leaving groups, by Lewis acids, and by a stable cation, so it is the normal mode in solution.
  • Homolytic fission. One electron goes to each fragment: A–B → A• + •B. It needs a weak bond (O–O in peroxides, N=N in azo compounds, X–X in halogens) together with heat or light, and it is favoured in the gas phase and in non-polar solvents.
  • Concerted change. Bonds break and form together in one cyclic transition state with no intermediate: the Diels–Alder reaction, the Cope and Claisen rearrangements, and electrocyclic ring opening. The stereochemistry is governed by orbital symmetry.

The energy required for homolysis is the bond dissociation enthalpy (BDE); values are given in section 7. In the gas phase heterolysis costs far more energy than homolysis because opposite charges must be separated; in a polar solvent the ions are solvated and heterolysis becomes feasible. Hence ionic reactions are characteristic of polar solvents, and radical reactions of the gas phase and of non-polar liquids.

Why It Happens In a polar solvent the energy needed to separate charges in heterolysis is repaid largely by solvation of the ions (ion–dipole interaction and hydrogen bonding), whereas in the gas phase or in a hydrocarbon there is no such compensation and homolysis, which creates no charge, is the lower-energy route. The same reasoning explains why light or peroxide is needed for radical reactions and why they are run in non-polar media.
Compare & Distinguish Heterolysis: electron pair goes to one fragment; ions; favoured by polar solvent; arrows are full-headed. Homolysis: one electron to each fragment; radicals; favoured by heat, light, weak bonds; arrows are half-headed. Concerted pericyclic change: no intermediate; cyclic transition state; stereochemistry from orbital symmetry.

1.3 Rules for drawing curved arrows

Curved arrows are bookkeeping for electrons. Following the rules below makes it impossible to draw a step that violates valence or charge.

  • Rule 1. The tail starts at electrons. It begins at a lone pair or at the middle of a bond (σ or π), never on an atom as such or on a positive charge.
  • Rule 2. The head shows where the electrons end up. It points at an atom (where a new bond or lone pair forms) or at the middle of the region between two atoms (where a new π bond forms).
  • Rule 3. Electrons flow from a nucleophile to an electrophile. An arrow that appears to run from a positive to a negative centre is almost always wrong.
  • Rule 4. Valence is never exceeded. Carbon cannot have five bonds, nor nitrogen four neutral bonds, nor oxygen three bonds without charge. If an arrow arrives at a saturated atom, another arrow must leave it in the same step.
  • Rule 5. Atoms and charge are conserved. The total formal charge before the arrow equals the total after it. Check this after every step.
  • Rule 6. A double-headed arrow ↔ is not a reaction. It joins resonance contributors of one species whose nuclei do not move. An equilibrium is written ⇌.
  • Rule 7. A half-headed arrow moves one electron and is used only for radical steps.

Seven patterns account for nearly every ionic step: (1) a nucleophile attacks an electrophile, using a lone pair or a π bond; (2) a leaving group departs with its bonding pair; (3) a proton is transferred between an acid and a base; (4) a π bond attacks an electrophile (alkene, arene, enol); (5) a group migrates with its bonding pair to an adjacent electron-deficient atom (1,2-shift); (6) a lone pair or carbanion expels a leaving group from the neighbouring atom (E1cB, collapse of a tetrahedral intermediate); (7) a π bond is pushed onto an atom so that a σ bond breaks (β-scission, retro-addition).

Example 1 Basic

Reading arrows

In the reaction of hydroxide with bromomethane, name the nucleophile, the electrophile and the leaving group, give the number of curved arrows, and say what is wrong with an arrow that starts on the carbon atom.

Show solution
  1. Nucleophile: HO−, which donates a lone pair. Electrophile: the carbon of CH3Br, which carries a partial positive charge because bromine is more electronegative. Leaving group: Br−.
  2. Two arrows are needed (the first panel of the figure above shows the same pattern): one from an oxygen lone pair to carbon (forming the C–O bond) and one from the C–Br bond to bromine (turning the bonding pair into a bromide lone pair).
  3. An arrow whose tail is on the carbon atom is not allowed because arrows start at electrons. The carbon donates no electrons here; it is the electron-poor site.
  4. The products are methanol and bromide ion. In the transition state a planar CH3 group lies between oxygen and bromine, and the negative charge is shared between the two.
Example 2 Basic

Counting charges

Draw the arrows for the protonation of ethene by H3O+ and check that formal charge is conserved.

Show solution
  1. Arrow 1: from the middle of the C=C bond to the hydrogen of H3O+. The π pair becomes the new C–H σ bond, and the other carbon is left with only six electrons.
  2. Arrow 2: from the O–H bond to oxygen. The pair that held the proton becomes a lone pair on neutral H2O. A hydrogen atom cannot carry two bonds, so one bond must leave as another arrives (rule 4).
  3. Charge: one positive charge before (on oxygen) and one after (on carbon). Charge is conserved.

Validity Check Before accepting a drawn step, check five things: (i) every arrow starts at electrons; (ii) no atom exceeds its valence; (iii) the total charge is unchanged; (iv) the species are consistent with the medium (no strong base in strongly acidic solution, no strong acid in basic solution); (v) the step is unimolecular or bimolecular, not termolecular. A mechanism that passes all five can still be wrong, because a mechanism is a hypothesis that must also agree with the rate law, the stereochemistry and labelling experiments.
Concept check. An arrow is drawn from a carbocation to a nucleophile. What is wrong?
Show answer
The tail must start at electrons. The nucleophile donates its pair to the cation, so the arrow runs from the nucleophile lone pair to the cationic carbon.
Concept check. Why can a rate law never prove a mechanism?
Show answer
Different mechanisms can give the same rate law; a rate law can only exclude mechanisms that disagree with it.
