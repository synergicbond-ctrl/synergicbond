21. Acidity of α-hydrogens

The conjugate base of a carbonyl compound, the enolate, is stabilised by delocalisation of the negative charge onto oxygen: C−–C=O ↔ C=C–O−. The more stabilised the enolate, the more acidic the α-H.

Key idea Order of α-acidity: 1,3-dicarbonyl ≫ nitroalkane > aldehyde > ketone > ester ≈ nitrile > amide. Why is a ketone more acidic than an ester? The ester oxygen donates electrons into C=O (resonance), reducing the ability of C=O to accept the negative charge. Why is an aldehyde more acidic than a ketone? The alkyl group on the ketone carbonyl destabilises the enolate (+I).
21.1 Bases for enolate formation
  • NaOH, NaOEt (alkoxides) in the corresponding alcohol: pKa of alcohol about 16, ketone about 20, so only a small equilibrium fraction (about 10-4) of enolate is formed. Enough for aldol and halogenation, because the enolate reacts as it is formed. The reaction is under thermodynamic (equilibrium) control.
  • LDA (lithium diisopropylamide, pKa of diisopropylamine about 36), NaH, KH, NaNH2, KHMDS: convert a ketone essentially completely into its enolate; with the bulky LDA at −78 °C in THF, the proton removed is the more accessible one: kinetic control.
Trap NaOH does not convert a ketone completely to an enolate. Questions which say "treat acetone with one equivalent of NaOH to make the enolate quantitatively" are wrong: use LDA or NaH.
