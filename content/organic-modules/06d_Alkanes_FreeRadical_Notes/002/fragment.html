3. Preparation I: hydrogenation, reduction of halides and Grignard reagents

These three routes convert a functional group into C–H without changing the carbon skeleton, so the product alkane can be read off directly from the starting material.

Catalytic hydrogenation. Alkenes and alkynes add H2 over finely divided Ni (about 450 K, Sabatier–Senderens), Pt or Pd (room temperature). Both hydrogens add to the same face (syn addition) of the double bond adsorbed on the metal. An alkyne takes two molecules of H2 to give the alkane.

Reactant:

Reagent: H2, Ni (or Pd/C)

Product:

2-Methylbut-2-ene gives 2-methylbutane. The skeleton is unchanged, so all alkenes with the 2-methylbutane carbon chain converge to this single alkane.

Reduction of alkyl halides. R–X is reduced to R–H by Zn/dilute HCl (nascent hydrogen), by red P + HI, by LiAlH4 in ether, or by H2/Pd with a base. Iodides react most easily and fluorides least.

Reactant:

Reagent: Zn, HCl (or LiAlH4)

Product:

Grignard reagent and water. An alkylmagnesium halide is a strong base; any compound with an acidic hydrogen (water, alcohols, carboxylic acids, terminal alkynes, amines) protonates the carbanion-like carbon and gives the alkane.

Reactant:

Reagent: H2O (or R'OH)

Product:

Mg(OH)Br is the by-product. With D2O the deuterated alkane CH3CHDCH3 is obtained, a standard way of placing an isotope label.

Key idea. Count of C–H formed: each CH3MgX consumes one active hydrogen and releases one CH4. Measuring the methane evolved from a compound with CH3MgI (Zerewitinoff method) counts its OH/NH/C≡C–H groups.
Trap. In counting problems 'alkenes that give a given alkane on hydrogenation', a 2-ene counts twice (E and Z) when stereoisomers are included, and a chiral alkene such as 3-methylpent-1-ene counts twice (R and S): the two enantiomers are different compounds even though both give the same achiral alkane, 3-methylpentane.
