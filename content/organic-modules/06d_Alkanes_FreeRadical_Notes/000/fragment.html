1. Bonding and sp3 geometry

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons of general formula CnH2n+2 (acyclic) in which every carbon is sp3 hybridised. One 2s and three 2p orbitals mix to give four equivalent sp3 orbitals directed to the corners of a tetrahedron, so each carbon makes four σ bonds at about 109.5°. The C–C bond (about 154 pm) arises from sp3–sp3 overlap and the C–H bond (about 109 pm) from sp3–1s overlap. Because all bonds are σ and almost non-polar (the electronegativities of C and H are close), alkanes are inert towards acids, bases and most oxidants, and they react mainly by free-radical pathways.

Methane, ethane, propane and isobutane. Every carbon is tetrahedral; the drawings are only skeletal projections.

  1. Ground state carbon: 2s22px12py1. Only two unpaired electrons, so four bonds need promotion of one 2s electron to 2p.
  2. Promotion gives 2s12p3 and four unpaired electrons; mixing 2s with three 2p gives four equivalent sp3 hybrid orbitals (25% s character each).
  3. Each hybrid orbital overlaps head-on with an H 1s orbital or with another carbon sp3 orbital, giving σ bonds whose electron density lies along the internuclear axis.
Formula. For an acyclic alkane CnH2n+2: number of C–C bonds = n − 1, number of C–H bonds = 2n + 2, so the total number of σ bonds is 3n + 1 and the number of π bonds is zero. Propane therefore has 2 + 8 = 10 σ bonds.

Rotation about a C–C σ bond is not completely free: the molecule passes through staggered and eclipsed arrangements of different energy (Section 11).

Trap. Carbon in an alkane is never 'sp3 with lone pairs' and the molecule is not planar. A planar methyl radical (sp2, 120°) is a different species; do not confuse the geometry of the alkane with that of the radical derived from it.
