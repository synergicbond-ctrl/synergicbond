7. The SN1 reaction in detail

7.1 Mechanism

The substrate ionises slowly to a carbocation and halide ion (rate-determining); the carbocation then reacts rapidly with whatever nucleophile is available, usually the solvent (solvolysis).

slow, ionisation⟶ +
Step 1 (rate-determining): the planar sp2 tert-butyl cation forms.
+ fast⟶ −H+⟶
Steps 2 and 3: water captures the cation (either face), then the protonated alcohol loses H+ to the solvent.
Formula rate = k [R–X] (first order; independent of the nature and concentration of Nu)
Energy diagram: two maxima separated by a shallow valley (the cation is a real intermediate); the first barrier is much higher than the second, so the first step controls the rate. Rate-determining ionisation is helped by factors that stabilise the transition state resembling the carbocation: substituents that stabilise the cation, a good leaving group, and a solvent that stabilises ions.
Key idea If a reaction shows first-order kinetics, racemisation of a chiral centre, rearranged skeleton, rate increase on switching from ethanol to water, and no dependence on nucleophile concentration, it is SN1.

7.2 Effect of substrate: carbocation stability decides

The more stable the carbocation, the faster the SN1. Stabilisation comes from (i) hyperconjugation and +I effect of alkyl groups: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3+; (ii) resonance with π-bonds or lone pairs: trityl > diphenylmethyl > allyl ≈ benzyl > 2° alkyl; (iii) adjacent heteroatom lone pairs (R–O–CH2+ ↔ R–O+=CH2); (iv) ring geometry and p-orbital overlap (bridgehead cations cannot become planar and are very unstable).

7.3 Effect of the leaving group and solvent

  • Leaving group: the same order as before: OTf > OTs > I > Br > Cl ≫ F; protonated alcohols leave as H2O in acid. The first, slow step has the C–X bond almost broken in the transition state, so LG ability is critical, more so than in SN2.
  • Solvent: polar protic solvents (water, formic acid, alcohols) stabilise both the leaving group (hydrogen-bonding to X−, solvation by H–O dipoles) and the developing cation. The ionising power rises with the dielectric constant and hydrogen-bond donor ability: water > formic acid > methanol > ethanol > acetic acid ≫ acetone, ether. Approximate dielectric constants: water 80, formic acid 58, DMSO 47, CH3CN 37, DMF 37, CH3OH 33, C2H5OH 25, acetone 21. tert-Butyl chloride solvolyses about 105 times faster in water than in ethanol.
  • Nucleophile: does not enter the rate law. A weak nucleophile (H2O, ROH, RCOOH) is perfectly adequate. A high concentration of a strong nucleophile instead pushes the substrate toward SN2 (or E2).
  • Common-ion effect (mass-law effect): adding X− (e.g. NaBr to the solvolysis of R–Br) captures R+ back to RX, slowing the reaction; this is observed with stable cations (benzhydryl chloride). Added inert salt (NaClO4) often speeds up ionisation by raising solvent polarity (the normal salt effect).

7.4 Stereochemistry: racemisation with a tilt toward inversion

The planar cation can be captured on either face, so an optically active 3° halide gives the racemic alcohol. In practice there is usually partial net inversion (the alcohol is slightly optically active with the inverted configuration) because the leaving group shields the front face for a short time (ion pair). Winstein's scheme:

  • R–X ⇌ contact (intimate) ion pair R+X− ⇌ solvent-separated ion pair ⇌ free ions.
  • Capture of the contact ion pair by solvent occurs from the back face (front face shielded by X−): inversion.
  • Capture of the solvent-separated or free cation is non-selective: racemisation.
  • The more stable the cation (longer-lived), the more complete the racemisation: trityl, benzhydryl ≈ 100 % racemisation; secondary alkyl: 5–30 % excess inversion.
H2O, SN1⟶
3-Bromo-3-methylhexane (chiral, enantiopure) gives racemic 3-methylhexan-3-ol (the chiral tertiary alcohol, 50:50 R and S, with a slight excess of inversion).
Trap In an SN1 reaction at a chiral centre, "racemic mixture" is the answer unless the question explicitly mentions ion-pair effects or neighbouring-group participation. Complete racemisation also needs the product to retain a stereocentre. If the stereocentre ends up on a carbon with two identical groups, no stereoisomer arises at all.

7.5 Rearrangements: the hallmark of carbocations

A carbocation may rearrange by a 1,2-shift of H−, CH3− or another group (with its bonding pair) from the adjacent carbon, if this gives a more stable cation. Also by ring expansion/contraction. Rearrangement happens within the lifetime of the intermediate before it is captured; the product mixture of SN1/E1 reactions often contains rearranged products.

2-Bromo-3-methylbutane: initial secondary cation undergoes a 1,2-hydride shift to the tertiary cation, which is captured by ethanol to give 2-ethoxy-2-methylbutane (major). Unrearranged ether and alkenes minor.

The common allylic cation C=C–C+H–CH3 ↔ +CH2–CH=CH–CH3 gives both alcohols (the more substituted alkene, crotyl alcohol, usually predominates at equilibrium; kinetic ratio depends on conditions).

7.6 SN1 versus E1 products

The carbocation intermediate has two fates: capture by Nu (SN1) or loss of a β-hydrogen to the solvent/base (E1). Tert-butyl bromide in 80 % aqueous ethanol at 25 °C: roughly 80 % substitution (alcohol and ether), 20 % isobutene. Raising the temperature favours E1 (ΔS > 0 for elimination). The competition is not controlled by nucleophile strength (weak Nu throughout); it is controlled by temperature and by how hindered the cation is.
