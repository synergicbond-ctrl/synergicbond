12. Amides

12.1 Structure: why amides are so unreactive and so unbasic

The amide nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the C=O π system (resonance R–C(=O)–NH2 ↔ R–C(O−)=NH2+). Consequences:

  • The C–N bond has partial double-bond character (≈ 133 pm, against 147 pm for an amine C–N); the group R–C(=O)–N is planar, and rotation about C–N is hindered (barrier 15–20 kcal/mol). N,N-Dimethylformamide shows two separate N–CH3 signals at room temperature.
  • The C=O bond is longer and has a lower IR frequency than a ketone (1650–1690 cm−1).
  • The nitrogen lone pair is not available: amides are essentially neutral (conjugate acid pKa ≈ −0.5, protonation occurs on oxygen), far less basic than amines (pKaH ≈ 10.6) or even than water-solvated ammonia (9.25).
  • The carbonyl carbon is a poor electrophile (resonance donation from N), so amides are the least reactive acyl derivatives.
  • N–H of a primary amide is weakly acidic (pKa ≈ 15–17); in imides (two acyl groups on N) pKa falls to 8–10 (phthalimide 8.3, succinimide 9.6), allowing NaOH or KOH to form the N-anion (Gabriel reagent).

  • Hydrolysis needs vigorous conditions (hours of boiling with 6 M acid or base). In acid the tetrahedral intermediate loses the protonated amine; in base the tetrahedral intermediate loses NH2−, which is a very poor leaving group, and the reaction is driven by the final deprotonation of RCOOH.

Amide products

Give the product with (a) CH3CONH2 + LiAlH4, (b) CH3CONHCH3 + LiAlH4, (c) CH3CON(CH3)2 + LiAlH4, (d) CH3CONH2 + P2O5, (e) CH3CONH2 + NaOBr, (f) CH3CONH2 + HNO2.

Example 30 Adv

Hydrolysis stoichiometry

When 0.59 g of an amide RCONH2 was boiled with excess NaOH, the NH3 evolved was absorbed in 50 mL of 0.2 M H2SO4; the excess acid required 20 mL of 0.5 M NaOH. Find the molar mass and identify R.

Show solution
  1. Acid taken: 50 × 0.2 × 2 = 20 mmol H+. Excess = 20 mL × 0.5 M = 10 mmol (NaOH is monoacidic). H+ used by NH3 = 10 mmol → n(NH3) = 10 mmol = n(amide).
  2. M = 0.59 g/0.010 mol = 59 g mol−1. RCONH2 = 44 + R → R = 15: CH3CONH2 (acetamide, M = 59).

12.4 Imides and the Gabriel-type chemistry

Succinimide and phthalimide are cyclic imides. The acidic N–H (pKa ≈ 8–10) is removed by KOH; the N-anion alkylates with RX (SN2) to give N-alkylphthalimide; hydrazine (or acid/base hydrolysis) then releases the pure primary amine (Gabriel synthesis, Amines chapter). Phthalimide is made from phthalic anhydride and NH3 (or urea) on heating.

Example 31 Adv

Hofmann on cyclic imides

Predict the products of NaOBr (Br2/NaOH) on (a) succinimide, (b) phthalimide.

Show solution
  1. The imide first hydrolyses in NaOH to the monoamide-carboxylate (ring opening); then the primary amide undergoes Hofmann degradation, losing its C=O as CO32− while R keeps the other carboxylate.
  2. (a) succinimide → H2NCH2CH2COOH: β-alanine (3-aminopropanoic acid).
  3. (b) phthalimide → anthranilic acid (2-aminobenzoic acid): the aryl group migrates to N.

12.5 Urea (carbamide)

Urea H2N–CO–NH2 is the diamide of carbonic acid. Industrial route: CO2 + 2 NH3 ⇌ H2NCOONH4 (ammonium carbamate) → H2NCONH2 + H2O at about 180 °C and 150 atm; Wöhler’s synthesis (1828): heating ammonium cyanate NH4OCN → urea (first organic compound made from inorganic starting materials, ending vitalism). Properties and reactions:

  • Basic character: weakly basic (monoacidic, pKaH 0.1); with conc. HNO3 gives the sparingly soluble urea nitrate, with oxalic acid urea oxalate. Protonation on oxygen.
  • Hydrolysis: boiling water, acid, alkali or urease: CO(NH2)2 + H2O → 2 NH3 + CO2.
  • HNO2: CO(NH2)2 + 2 HNO2 → CO2 + 2 N2 + 3 H2O. NaOBr: CO(NH2)2 + 3 NaOBr + 2 NaOH → N2 + Na2CO3 + 3 NaBr + 3 H2O (hypobromite method for estimating urea from N2 volume).
  • Heating (≈ 150 °C): 2 NH2CONH2 → H2NCONHCONH2 (biuret) + NH3; biuret with dilute CuSO4 and NaOH gives a violet colour (the biuret test, also given by peptides and proteins, not by urea itself).
  • With diethyl malonate and NaOEt: barbituric acid (a cyclic ureide; the parent of barbiturates). With HCHO: urea–formaldehyde resin. With chloral, acyl chlorides: ureides.

Urea numericals

How many grams of N2 are obtained (and what volume at STP) when 6.0 g of urea reacts with excess nitrous acid?

Show solution
  1. Urea M = 60: 6.0 g = 0.10 mol. CO(NH2)2 + 2 HNO2 → CO2 + 2 N2 + 3 H2O: 0.20 mol N2 = 5.6 g, volume at STP = 0.20 × 22.4 = 4.48 L. (Together with 2.24 L of CO2.)
Example 33 Adv

Identify

Compound A (C2H5NO) gives NH3 on warming with NaOH, N2 with HNO2, and the nitrile C2H3N on heating with P2O5. What is it? What would the isomer CH3NHCHO give?

Show solution
  1. A is ethanamide (acetamide): a primary amide (NH3 with NaOH, loses water to CH3CN, gives N2 with nitrous acid with formation of CH3COOH).
  2. N-methylmethanamide (HCONHCH3) would give methylamine (not NH3) with hot NaOH, and no nitrile.

12.6 Carbamates (urethanes) and carbamic acid

R–NH–COOR′ (urethane) arises from RNCO + R′OH (also in the Hofmann/Curtius reactions when run in alcohol). Carbamic acids RNHCOOH are unstable and lose CO2 to give RNH2, which is why the Hofmann product is the amine. Carbamates are used as insecticides (carbaryl) and polyurethanes (diisocyanate + diol).
