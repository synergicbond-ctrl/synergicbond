Each paragraph is followed by several questions; solutions assume only facts developed in the chapter.

Paragraph A: an aldohexose puzzle

An aldohexose X (D-series) is oxidised by hot HNO3 to an optically inactive dicarboxylic acid. Ruff degradation of X gives an aldopentose Y, whose HNO3 oxidation product is optically active. Kiliani–Fischer lengthening of Y gives X and a C-2 epimer X′.

Practice 223 L3 · paragraph A

Identify X.

Show answer

D-Galactose. Meso aldaric acid → X is D-allose (RRRR) or D-galactose (RLLR). Ruff degradation removes C-1: allose → D-ribose (RRR), whose pentaric acid is meso, contradicting "active". Galactose → D-lyxose (LLR), whose acid is active ✓.

Practice 224 L3 · paragraph A

Identify Y.

Show answer

D-Lyxose (LLR): the pentose lyxose gives an active diacid (not a palindrome) and Kiliani–Fischer on lyxose gives galactose and talose.

Practice 225 L3 · paragraph A

Identify X′ and say whether its aldaric acid is optically active.

Show answer

X′ is the C-2 epimer of galactose, D-talose (LLLR). Code LLLR is not a palindrome: its aldaric acid is optically active. (Talose and altrose give the same acid because rotating LLLR by 180° gives LRRR.)

Practice 226 L3 · paragraph A

What is the relationship between X and glucose?

Show answer

C-4 epimers (RLLR vs RLRR). Each of them gives an aldaric acid with different optical activity: glucaric acid active, galactaric acid meso.

Paragraph B: a disaccharide

A disaccharide D (C12H22O11) reduces Tollens' reagent. It is hydrolysed by emulsin (a β-glucosidase) to glucose only, but is not affected by maltase. Exhaustive methylation followed by acid hydrolysis gives 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-methyl-D-glucose and 2,3,6-tri-O-methyl-D-glucose in equal amounts.

Practice 227 L3 · paragraph B

Identify D and state the glycosidic link.

Show answer

Cellobiose, β-D-Glc-(1→4)-D-Glc. Emulsin shows the β-link; the two methyl ethers show a 1→4 link (the free OH at C-4 of the trimethyl sugar is the linkage oxygen); the Tollens test shows a free anomeric OH on the second unit.

Practice 228 L3 · paragraph B

Which methylated sugar comes from the non-reducing unit?

Show answer

2,3,4,6-Tetra-O-methyl-D-glucose: its only free OH is the former glycosidic C-1 OH created by hydrolysis; all other OH, including C-6, were methylated.

Practice 229 L3 · paragraph B

How would the methylation products differ if D were gentiobiose (β-1,6)?

Show answer

Gentiobiose gives 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-methylglucose (non-reducing end) and 2,3,4-tri-O-methylglucose (the second unit has OH at C-1 (hemiacetal after hydrolysis) and C-6; C-5 is the ring oxygen).

Paragraph C: sequencing a pentapeptide

A pentapeptide contains Ala, Arg, Gly, Met and Phe (one each). (i) Sanger's reagent labels Phe. (ii) Carboxypeptidase releases Ala first. (iii) Trypsin gives a dipeptide (which contains the N-terminal residue) and a tripeptide. (iv) Chymotrypsin gives free Phe and a tetrapeptide. (v) CNBr gives a tetrapeptide and free Ala.

Practice 230 L3 · paragraph C

Deduce the sequence.

Show answer

Sanger: N-terminal Phe. Carboxypeptidase: C-terminal Ala. Trypsin cuts after Arg and gives a dipeptide + tripeptide, so Arg is residue 2: Phe–Arg | (Gly, Met)–Ala. CNBr cuts after Met and leaves free Ala, so Met is residue 4: Phe–Arg–Gly–Met–Ala. Chymotrypsin cuts after Phe: Phe + a tetrapeptide ✓.

Practice 231 L3 · paragraph C

What is the tetrapeptide formed by CNBr cleavage?

Show answer

Cleavage at the C-side of Met converts Met to homoserine lactone: Phe–Arg–Gly–Hse lactone (plus free Ala).

Practice 232 L3 · paragraph C

Calculate the molar mass of the pentapeptide (Phe 165.19, Arg 174.20, Gly 75.07, Met 149.22, Ala 89.09).

Show answer

Σ = 652.77; minus 4 H2O (72.06) → 580.7 g mol−1.

Practice 233 L3 · paragraph C

How many fragments are obtained if the pentapeptide is cleaved with both trypsin and chymotrypsin?

Show answer

Trypsin cuts after Arg-2; chymotrypsin after Phe-1. Fragments: Phe | Arg | Gly–Met–Ala: 3 fragments.

Practice 234 L3 · paragraph C

How many moles of water are required for complete hydrolysis of the pentapeptide, and what is the mass of free amino acids obtained from 0.100 mol?

Show answer

4 mol H2O per mol. Σ amino acids = 652.77 g mol−1: 0.100 mol → 65.3 g (peptide mass 58.07 g + 7.21 g water).

Paragraph D: an unknown amino acid

An α-amino acid Z shows three pKa values: 2.1, 4.1 and 9.5 in its titration with NaOH.

Practice 235 L3 · paragraph D

What kind of side chain does Z have, and what is its pI?

Show answer

The middle pK (4.1) is a side-chain group of acidic type (a second –COOH; Z resembles glutamic acid). pI = ½(2.1 + 4.1) = 3.1.

Practice 236 L3 · paragraph D

Give the net charge of Z at pH 1, 3.1, 7 and 12.

Show answer

pH 1: +1; pH 3.1: 0; pH 7: −1 (both carboxyls ionised, amino group protonated); pH 12: −2.

Practice 237 L3 · paragraph D

How many moles of NaOH are needed to take 0.10 mol of the hydrochloride (the +1 cation) of Z to its pI and to the fully deprotonated dianion?

Show answer

To the pI (net 0): one equivalent, 0.10 mol. To the dianion (net −2): three equivalents, 0.30 mol.

Practice 238 L3 · paragraph D

In which direction does Z move at pH 7 in an electric field?

Show answer

Net charge −1 → toward the anode.

Paragraph E: reading a titration

0.0100 mol of an amino acid hydrochloride is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. The pH is 2.30 after 50.0 mL base and 9.70 after 150 mL.

Practice 239 L3 · paragraph E

Find the pK values and the pI.

Show answer

The hydrochloride is the cation, so the first 100 mL (10.0 mmol) converts it to the zwitterion. 50.0 mL is the half-equivalence point of this stage: pK1 = 2.30. The second stage (100–200 mL) converts the zwitterion to the anion; 150 mL is its half-equivalence point: pK2 = 9.70. pI = ½(2.30 + 9.70) = 6.00.

Practice 240 L3 · paragraph E

Which amino acid is it, and what is the volume of NaOH at the pI?

Show answer

pI 6.00, pK 2.3 and 9.7 fits alanine (2.34/9.69) or glycine/valine nearby. The pI is reached after exactly one equivalent: 100 mL.

Practice 241 L3 · paragraph E

What volume of NaOH gives the maximum buffering capacity in the second buffer region?

Show answer

At half-equivalence of the second stage: 150 mL (pH = pK2 = 9.70).

Paragraph F: from DNA to protein

The coding strand of a gene segment reads 5′-ATGGCCAAGCCTGAATAA-3′. Codons: AUG Met, GCC Ala, AAG Lys, CCU Pro, GAA Glu, UAA stop, GUA Val, UAG stop.

Practice 242 L3 · paragraph F

Write the template strand and the mRNA.

Show answer

Template (3′→5′): 3′-TACCGGTTCGGACTTATT-5′. mRNA: 5′-AUGGCCAAGCCUGAAUAA-3′.

Practice 243 L3 · paragraph F

Give the peptide encoded.

Show answer

AUG GCC AAG CCU GAA UAA → Met–Ala–Lys–Pro–Glu then stop.

Practice 244 L3 · paragraph F

How many hydrogen bonds hold the duplex together?

Show answer

The coding strand has 4 G (positions 3, 4, 9, 13) and 4 C (5, 6, 10, 11). Every G or C pairs with a partner on the other strand, so there are 8 G–C pairs and 18 − 8 = 10 A–T pairs. H-bonds = 8 × 3 + 10 × 2 = 44.

Practice 245 L3 · paragraph F

A mutation changes GAA → GUA in the mRNA. What is the effect, and what is the DNA change?

Show answer

Glu → Val (missense). Coding strand GAA → GTA: an A → T transversion at the middle base (the sickle-cell type change).

Paragraph H: separating proteins

Three proteins have isoelectric points: P1 (pI 4.5), P2 (pI 7.0), P3 (pI 10.0). A mixture in pH 7.0 buffer is passed through (i) a cation-exchange column (negatively charged resin) and (ii) an anion-exchange column (DEAE, positively charged).

Practice 249 L3 · paragraph H

What is the net charge of each protein at pH 7.0?

Show answer

Practice 250 L3 · paragraph H

Which proteins bind to the cation-exchange column?

Show answer

P3 (positive) binds strongly; P2 (neutral) does not bind; P1 (negative) is repelled. Only P3.

Practice 251 L3 · paragraph H

On the DEAE column the bound proteins are eluted by increasing [NaCl]. State the order of elution.

Show answer

P3 (positive) is repelled and passes through; P2 (neutral) is essentially unbound, elutes next; P1 (negative) binds and elutes last at the highest salt. P3, P2, P1 (with P3 and P2 emerging in the flow-through).

Paragraph I: an enzyme and an inhibitor

An enzyme gives these initial rates (µM min−1): v = 16.7, 28.6, 50.0, 66.7, 80.0 at [S] = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 mM. In the presence of 2.0 mM inhibitor I, KM rises to 10 mM without change in Vmax.

Practice 252 L3 · paragraph I

Find Vmax and KM without inhibitor.

Show answer

From v = VmaxS/(KM + S): at S = 5 mM, v = 50.0 = Vmax/2 → KM = 5 mM. Check S = 20: v = Vmax × 20/25 = 80 → Vmax = 100 µM min−1, KM = 5 mM. (Check S = 1: 100/6 = 16.7 ✓.)

Practice 253 L3 · paragraph I

Classify the inhibitor.

Show answer

KM up, Vmax unchanged: competitive.

Practice 254 L3 · paragraph I

Find Ki from KM,app = KM(1 + [I]/Ki).

Show answer

10 = 5(1 + 2.0/Ki) → 2 = 1 + 2.0/Ki → Ki = 2.0 mM.

Practice 255 L3 · paragraph I

Find the rate at [S] = 10 mM in the presence of the inhibitor.

Show answer

v = 100 × 10/(10 + 10) = 50 µM min−1 (without inhibitor 66.7).

Practice 290 L3 · multi-step

D-Glucose is reduced with NaBH4 and the product is treated with excess periodic acid. Give the products and the moles of HIO4 consumed per mole.

Show answer

NaBH4 gives D-glucitol, HOCH2–(CHOH)4–CH2OH. Every one of the five C–C bonds joins two carbons that each carry an OH, so 5 HIO4 are consumed. The two terminal CH2OH carbons become 2 HCHO; the four internal CHOH carbons (C-2 to C-5) each lose both neighbours and become 4 HCOOH.

Practice 291 L3 · multi-step

Glucose itself also consumes 5 HIO4. Compare its small-molecule products with those of glucitol.

Show answer

Glucose: C-1 (aldehyde carbon) leaves as HCOOH, C-2 to C-5 as 4 HCOOH, and C-6 as HCHO: 5 HCOOH + 1 HCHO. Glucitol: 4 HCOOH + 2 HCHO. The same five bonds are cleaved, but C-1 ends as formic acid in glucose (an aldehyde carbon is oxidised) and as formaldehyde in glucitol (a CH2OH carbon). The ratio HCHO : HCOOH therefore tells an aldose from its alditol.

Write the mechanism-level reason why the anomeric effect makes the α-glycoside more stable than expected from sterics alone. Which orbital interaction is responsible?

The ring oxygen lone pair (nO, antiperiplanar to the axial C-1–X bond) donates into the σ*(C-1–X) antibonding orbital: nO → σ*C–X stabilisation. It only works when X is axial, so the axial (α) anomer gains stabilisation of ≈ 4–8 kJ mol−1 that offsets the 1,3-diaxial strain.

Practice 303 L3 · multi-step

In acidic methanol D-glucose forms methyl glucosides. Explain why the product is a mixture, why the α-pyranoside predominates at equilibrium, and why the glycosides are stable to base.

Show answer

The oxocarbenium ion intermediate (formed after protonation and loss of water) is planar at C-1, so methanol attacks from either face: α/β mixture. At equilibrium the α anomer wins through the anomeric effect. Under basic conditions there is no good leaving group at C-1 (OR− is poor) and no acid to form the oxocarbenium, so acetals are base-stable.

Practice 306 L3 · mechanism

Write the steps for the acid-catalysed hydrolysis of methyl α-D-glucopyranoside. Which oxygen is protonated first, and which intermediate makes the reaction go?

methyl α-D-glucopyranoside oxocarbenium ion
Show answer
  1. 1. The exocyclic (glycosidic) OMe oxygen is protonated: a good leaving group (methanol) is made.
  2. 2. The ring oxygen lone pair pushes into C-1 as CH3OH leaves: the oxocarbenium ion, stabilised by nO → p(C+) donation (a C=O+ double-bond resonance form).
  3. 3. Water adds to C-1 (HOMO of water's lone pair → empty p orbital on carbon) from either face; loss of H+ gives the hemiacetal (α/β glucopyranose).
  4. The reaction is first order in H+: the rate-determining step is formation of the oxocarbenium ion.
Practice 307 L3 · mechanism

2-Deoxyglycosides hydrolyse much faster than the corresponding glucosides. Explain using the oxocarbenium ion.

Show answer

The rate-determining step creates a positive charge at C-1. In glucosides the C-2 oxygen is an electron-withdrawing (σ-acceptor) group beside the developing cation, which destabilises it; in 2-deoxyglycosides C-2 has only H, so the oxocarbenium ion is more stable and forms faster (by roughly three orders of magnitude). This is why deoxysugars in DNA and in antibiotics are acid-labile.

Practice 308 L3 · mechanism

The mutarotation of tetramethylglucose in benzene is catalysed by 2-pyridone (2-hydroxypyridine) far better than by a mixture of pyridine and phenol. Why?

Show answer

2-Pyridone is a bifunctional acid–base catalyst: its N–H hydrogen-bonds to the ring O-5 while its C=O accepts the hemiacetal O–H proton in the same cyclic transition state. Proton donation and proton acceptance occur simultaneously (a concerted, eight-membered cyclic array), so no charged intermediates are formed in the non-polar solvent. A separate acid and base would need to find each other at low concentration and would require charge separation.

Practice 309 L3 · mechanism

Explain why an N-acyl amino acid racemises when its carboxyl group is activated, but a Boc-amino acid does not.

Show answer

The activated N-acyl derivative cyclises (the amide O attacks the activated carbonyl) to a 5(4H)-oxazolone. Its C-4–H is acidic: loss of H+ gives an aromatic (6π-electron) oxazolone anion, which is planar and reprotonates from either face: racemisation. In a Boc-amino acid the carbamate C=O oxygen is less nucleophilic (the second O donates into the carbonyl), so cyclisation is much slower; coupling is complete before racemisation.

Practice 310 L3 · mechanism

Show with orbitals why acetamide protonates on oxygen rather than nitrogen.

Show answer

Protonation on O places the positive charge in the delocalised system: the cation CH3–C(OH)=NH2+ keeps the three-orbital (O–C–N) π system and is stabilised by resonance between O–H and N+. Protonation on N would use the nitrogen lone pair (which is part of ψ1/ψ2) and destroy the delocalisation; the resulting ammonium is much less stable. The conjugate acid pKa of an amide is ≈ −0.5, which also shows that amides are very weak bases.

Practice 311 L3 · mechanism

The Boc group is removed by CF3COOH to give the amine, CO2 and isobutene. Write the steps.

Show answer
  1. 1. Protonation of the carbamate carbonyl oxygen (acid on the most basic site).
  2. 2. The C–O bond to the tert-butyl group breaks heterolytically (the tertiary cation is stabilised by hyperconjugation): tert-butyl cation + carbamic acid R–NH–COOH.
  3. 3. The carbamic acid loses CO2 spontaneously (a carbamic acid is unstable): the free amine (as its trifluoroacetate salt).
  4. 4. The tert-butyl cation loses H+ to give isobutene (or is trapped by a scavenger). No nucleophile is needed: that is why Boc cleavage is clean.
Practice 312 L3 · mechanism

Describe the steps and the product of hydrogenolysis of Cbz–NH–R with H2/Pd-C.

Show answer
  1. 1. H2 and the benzylic C–O bond are both adsorbed on the Pd surface.
  2. 2. The benzylic C–O bond (weakened because the σ*(C–O) is conjugated with the aromatic ring) is cleaved by H atoms: toluene + carbamic acid R–NH–COOH.
  3. 3. The carbamic acid loses CO2 to give R–NH2. The by-products toluene and CO2 are volatile.
Practice 313 L3 · mechanism

In the Edman degradation the cleavage uses anhydrous acid but the conversion to PTH uses aqueous acid. Why?

Show answer

In anhydrous acid the thiourea sulfur attacks the first C=O intramolecularly and the shortened peptide is released intact. Water would hydrolyse the other peptide bonds (acid hydrolysis) and destroy the remaining chain, so anhydrous conditions are essential for the cleavage step. The cleaved thiazolinone is then removed from the peptide and treated with aqueous acid, where water-assisted ring opening and re-closure rearranges it to the stable PTH, which does not involve the peptide any more.

Practice 314 L3 · mechanism

Why is the Strecker synthesis carried out with NH4Cl/KCN instead of NH3 and HCN gas, and why does cyanide attack the iminium ion rather than the aldehyde?

Show answer

NH4Cl supplies NH3 and the mildly acidic NH4+ that protonates the carbinolamine OH; KCN is a safe solid source of CN−, and the buffer keeps enough free CN− (pKa(HCN) = 9.2). Aldehyde + NH3 are in equilibrium with the iminium ion; the iminium carbon carries a formal positive charge and its π* (LUMO) is lower in energy than that of the aldehyde, so it reacts with the nucleophile faster, and the C–C bond formed to the nitrile is not reversed. (Competing direct cyanohydrin formation is reversible and ends up funnelled into the aminonitrile.)

Practice 315 L3 · mechanism

Why does enolisation of glucose in base involve only C-2, and what orbital alignment is needed?

Show answer

Only C-2–H is α to the carbonyl. For deprotonation the C2–H σ bond must lie parallel to the p orbitals of the C=O π system (stereoelectronic alignment), so that the developing carbanion lone pair overlaps with π*(C=O) and is delocalised onto oxygen (enolate). The hydrogens at C-3 and beyond are not α to a carbonyl and are not acidified. The planar sp2 C-2 loses its configuration: reprotonation gives glucose or mannose; transfer of the C-1 proton to C-2 / tautomerisation of the enediol gives fructose.

Practice 316 L3 · mechanism

In the Ruff degradation, what is the formal change at C-1 and C-2 when calcium gluconate is treated with H2O2/Fe3+?

Show answer

C-2 (CHOH next to the carboxylate) is oxidised (formally to the α-keto acid level) while C-1 is lost as CO2; C-2 becomes the new aldehyde carbon of the pentose (D-arabinose). Mechanistically the Fe3+/H2O2 system forms an Fe(III) carboxylate chelate; one-electron oxidation/decarboxylation (oxidative decarboxylation of an α-hydroxy acid) then yields the aldehyde. The configurations at C-3 to C-5 are untouched, so glucose and mannose both give arabinose.

Practice 317 L3 · mechanism

Hydrolysis of a ribonucleoside 3′-phosphodiester gives a mixture of 2′- and 3′-phosphates. Explain using the intermediate.

Show answer

The 2′-OH (as 2′-O−) attacks P from the side opposite the 5′-O leaving group, forming a five-membered 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate (apical in, apical out, through a trigonal bipyramid). The cyclic phosphate is then opened by water at P from either side: breaking P–O3′ gives the 2′-phosphate, breaking P–O2′ gives the 3′-phosphate. Both opening directions are possible, so a mixture results.

Practice 318 L3 · mechanism

In chymotrypsin, what is the role of (a) Ser-195, (b) His-57, (c) Asp-102, (d) the oxyanion hole? What happens when Ser-195 is mutated to Ala?

Show answer

(a) Ser-195 is the nucleophile (its alkoxide attacks the peptide C=O). (b) His-57 is general base (takes the Ser proton) and then general acid (gives it to the leaving N). (c) Asp-102 orients His-57 and stabilises its protonated imidazolium (charge relay). (d) The oxyanion hole (two backbone N–H) stabilises the negative charge on the tetrahedral intermediate: the O− is H-bonded, so the transition state is lower in energy than the ground state. Ser → Ala removes the nucleophile: activity drops by many orders of magnitude.

Practice 319 L3 · mechanism

Cis-cyclohexane-1,2-diol reacts with HIO4 faster than trans-cyclohexane-1,2-diol. Why?

Show answer

Cleavage goes through a five-membered cyclic periodate ester. In the cis diol an axial–equatorial pair of OH groups has the ≈ 60° O–C–C–O dihedral needed for a five-membered ring. In the trans diol the OH groups are either diequatorial (≈ 60° but they point away: strained ring) or diaxial (180°): the ring cannot form without distortion, so the rate drops (but trans-diequatorial diols still react slowly; rigid trans-decalin diols that cannot reach the geometry do not react).

Practice 320 L3 · mechanism

Why does a 2-methoxytetrahydropyran prefer the axial OMe group in non-polar solvents, though an axial methoxy is larger than hydrogen?

Show answer

The anomeric effect: in the axial conformer a lone pair on the ring oxygen is antiperiplanar to the C-2–OMe bond; nO → σ*(C–O) donation lowers the energy by about 4 kJ mol−1 (≈ 1 kcal) in non-polar solvents. In the equatorial conformer the C–OMe bond is antiperiplanar to a ring C–C bond, a poor acceptor. Also the dipoles of the ring C–O and the exocyclic C–O partly cancel in the axial form. In water the dipole effect is damped and the preference decreases.

Practice 321 L3 · mechanism

Why is the N-terminal amino group of a peptide (pKa ≈ 8) less basic than the amino group of the free amino acid (pK2 ≈ 9.6)?

Show answer

In a free amino acid the NH3+ sits next to a negatively charged carboxylate (zwitterion), whose charge stabilises the ammonium ion and keeps it protonated up to pH ≈ 9.6. In a peptide that neighbour is a neutral amide carbonyl, which withdraws electron density inductively and offers no stabilising negative charge, so the ammonium is a stronger acid (pKa ≈ 7.8–8.2). The C-terminal COOH is correspondingly less acidic than in the free amino acid (pKa ≈ 3.1–3.5 versus 2.3), because it no longer has a cationic ammonium two atoms away to stabilise its carboxylate.
