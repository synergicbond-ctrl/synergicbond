1. Why purify, and what "pure" means

An organic compound made in the laboratory or isolated from a natural source is almost never pure. The product of a reaction contains unreacted starting material, by-products, catalyst, solvent and tar. Before its melting point, spectrum or percentage composition means anything it must be purified, and the purity must be demonstrated. The method of purification is chosen from the physical state and the physical properties of the compound and of its impurities: solid or liquid, volatility, thermal stability, solubility in a solvent, acidic or basic character, polarity.

IMP A pure organic solid has a sharp melting point (melts over 0.5 to 1 K); a pure liquid has a constant boiling point at a given pressure. A trace of impurity lowers and broadens the melting range, and raises (non-volatile impurity) or changes the boiling point.

Criteria of purity used in practice:

  • Sharp melting point of a solid; sharp, constant boiling point of a liquid. (Exception: azeotropes boil at a constant temperature although they are mixtures.)
  • Mixed melting point. Mix the sample with an authentic specimen of the supposed compound. If the melting point is unchanged and sharp the two are identical; if it is lowered and the range is wide, they are different compounds (the second compound acts as an impurity).
Why It Happens A soluble impurity behaves as a solute in the molten sample, so the freezing (melting) point is depressed, as in any colligative effect; because the impurity concentration in the melt changes as the solid melts, melting spreads over a range instead of occurring at one temperature. A eutectic mixture is the exception: it melts sharply, at a temperature below both components.
  • A single spot in thin layer chromatography in at least two different solvent systems; a single peak in GC or HPLC.
  • Constant physical constants after repeated purification: refractive index, density, specific rotation, spectra.
  • Elemental analysis that agrees with the calculated percentages within experimental error (about ±0.4 %).
Exam Trap A constant boiling point does NOT prove purity: ethanol–water (95.6 % ethanol, b.p. 351.3 K) boils at a constant temperature as a mixture. Likewise a single TLC spot in one solvent may hide two compounds that happen to have the same Rf there, which is why two solvents are used.
Example 1 Main

Mixed melting point

Two samples, each melting sharply at 395 K, are mixed. The mixture melts over the range 380 to 390 K. What is concluded?

Show solution
  1. A sharp m.p. of each sample alone means each is a pure compound.
  2. The mixture melts lower and over a range: each sample behaves as an impurity for the other, so the two are different compounds that merely have the same melting point.
  3. If the mixture had melted sharply at 395 K, the two would be identical.
Concept check

A sample melts sharply at 395 K. Does this alone prove it is pure?

Show answer

No. A eutectic mixture also melts sharply, and a coincidence of melting points of two compounds is possible; the mixed melting point and TLC in two solvents are needed.
