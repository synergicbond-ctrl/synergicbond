1.5 Carbon classes and the alkyl groups

A carbon is primary (1°) if bonded to one other carbon, secondary (2°) to two, tertiary (3°) to three and quaternary (4°) to four. Hydrogens take the class of their carbon. The classification is used in names (sec-butyl, tert-butyl) and again in reactivity (stability of carbocations and radicals).

2,2,4-trimethylpentane
All four classes in one molecule: five CH₃ carbons are 1°; the CH₂ is 2°; C-4 (CH) is 3°; C-2 (no hydrogen) is 4°

An alkyl group is an alkane minus one hydrogen; the ending –ane becomes –yl. The carbon that carries the free valence is the attachment point and is always numbered 1 in the systematic name of a branched group.

Exam Trap The prefix iso means a terminal –CH(CH₃)₂ on an otherwise straight chain. Isobutyl is (CH₃)₂CHCH₂–, whereas isopropyl is (CH₃)₂CH–; isopentyl is 3-methylbutyl, not 1-methylbutyl. A typical slip is to confuse isobutyl with sec-butyl: the isobutyl attachment carbon is a CH₂ (primary), the sec-butyl attachment carbon is a CH (secondary).
Special Case A branched alkyl group is named systematically by numbering it from the attachment carbon as C-1 and choosing the longest chain that starts there. Its locants refer to that group, not to the parent chain, and the whole group goes in brackets.
Concept Link The class of the attachment carbon (1°, 2°, 3°) decides how readily the group is lost as a carbocation or radical. That is why the same four butyl groups reappear in substitution and elimination reactions.
