6. Leaving-group ability

A good leaving group is a weak base that is stable as the free anion. The better the group departs, the lower the transition-state energy for both SN1 (ionisation) and SN2 (bond breaking), so a better leaving group accelerates both mechanisms. Among the halides, I– &gt; Br– &gt; Cl– &gt;&gt; F–, which follows the acidity of HX; alkyl fluorides are essentially unreactive in substitution because the C–F bond is strong and F– is a relatively strong base.

Hydroxide, alkoxide and amide ions are strong bases and very poor leaving groups, so alcohols and ethers do not react directly with nucleophiles. They are activated either by protonation (ROH2+ leaves as neutral water, as in the reaction of an alcohol with HBr) or by conversion into a sulfonate ester (ROTs, ROMs) or alkyl halide with SOCl2 or PBr3. Sulfonate anions are weak bases, being conjugate bases of strong sulfonic acids, and are excellent leaving groups.

Reactant:

Reagent: 1. TsCl, pyridine; 2. NaCN, DMSO

Product:

Poor leaving group (OH) → excellent leaving group (OTs) → SN2 by cyanide. Butan-1-ol is converted into pentanenitrile, a chain extended by one carbon. In a chiral alcohol the tosylation step would not change the configuration, because the C–O bond is not broken.

Key idea. Leaving-group ability follows the weakness of the base: I– &gt; Br– &gt; Cl– &gt;&gt; F–; TsO– and H2O (from ROH2+) are good; HO–, RO–, NH2– are not. Use the leaving-group trend within a series of halides when comparing rates.
