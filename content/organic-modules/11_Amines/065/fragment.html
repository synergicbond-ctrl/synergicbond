Practice 1 L1 Naming

Give the IUPAC name of CH3CH2CH(NH2)CH2CH3.

Show answer
  1. The longest chain containing the C–N carbon has 5 carbons; N sits on C3 (the middle carbon), so the locant is 3.
  2. Name: pentan-3-amine (also 3-aminopentane; a primary amine).

Pentan-3-amine.

Practice 2 L1 Class

Classify as 1°, 2° or 3°: (a) (CH3)3CNH2, (b) C6H5CH2NH2, (c) (CH3)2CHNHCH3, (d) C6H5N(CH3)2.

Show answer
  1. The class depends on the number of carbon groups attached to N, not on the type of carbon: (a) one group (tert-butyl) → 1°; (b) one (benzyl) → 1°; (c) isopropyl + methyl → 2°; (d) phenyl + two methyls → 3°.

(a) 1°, (b) 1°, (c) 2°, (d) 3°.

Practice 4 L1 Isomers

How many primary amines have the molecular formula C5H13N?

Show answer
  1. Primary amines are R–NH2 with R = C5H11. Count the pentyl groups by carbon skeleton.
  2. Straight chain (pentane): pentan-1-amine, pentan-2-amine, pentan-3-amine = 3.
  3. 2-Methylbutane skeleton: 2-methylbutan-1-amine, 3-methylbutan-1-amine, 3-methylbutan-2-amine, 2-methylbutan-2-amine = 4.
  4. Neopentane skeleton: 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-amine = 1.
  5. Total 3 + 4 + 1 = 8.

Practice 5 L1 Isomers

Of the eight primary amines C5H13N, how many are chiral?

Show answer
  1. A chiral primary amine needs a carbon bearing four different groups (NH2, H and two different alkyls). The candidates: pentan-2-amine (C2: NH2, H, CH3, propyl) ✓; 2-methylbutan-1-amine (C2: CH2NH2, H, CH3, C2H5) ✓; 3-methylbutan-2-amine (C2: NH2, H, CH3, isopropyl) ✓. Pentan-3-amine, 3-methylbutan-1-amine, 2-methylbutan-2-amine, pentan-1-amine and neopentylamine have no stereocentre.

Practice 6 L1 Boiling points

Arrange in increasing boiling point: (a) propan-1-amine, (b) N,N-dimethylmethanamine (trimethylamine), (c) N-methylethanamine.

Show answer
  1. All three are C3H9N (M = 59). Association by N–H···N bonds: 1° (two N–H) > 2° (one N–H) > 3° (none). Hence b.p. 1° > 2° > 3°.

(b) < (c) < (a): trimethylamine (3 °C) < N-methylethanamine (37 °C) < propan-1-amine (48 °C).

Practice 7 L1 Boiling points

Explain why ethylamine (b.p. 17 °C) boils lower than ethanol (78 °C) although both have nearly equal molar masses, and why both boil higher than propane (−42 °C).

Show answer
  1. Propane (M = 44) is non-polar, with only weak London forces. Ethylamine (M = 45) has N–H···N hydrogen bonds. Ethanol (M = 46) has stronger O–H···O hydrogen bonds because O is more electronegative than N. Order of b.p.: propane < ethylamine < ethanol.

Because O–H···O hydrogen bonds are stronger than N–H···N bonds (oxygen is more electronegative); propane has only London forces.

Practice 8 L1 Solubility

Why is methylamine very soluble in water whereas aniline is only sparingly soluble? Which dissolves readily in dilute HCl?

Show answer
  1. Solubility in water depends on the balance between hydrogen bonding of N with water and the size of the hydrophobic hydrocarbon part. CH3NH2: tiny alkyl group → highly soluble. C6H5NH2: large phenyl → about 3.6 g in 100 mL.
  2. In dilute HCl: RNH2 + HCl → RNH3+Cl− (ionic, soluble). Aniline gives anilinium chloride.

Methylamine forms hydrogen bonds with water and has a small hydrophobic part; aniline has a large non-polar phenyl group. Both dissolve in dilute HCl because they form soluble ammonium salts.

Practice 10 L1 Structure

Why cannot an amine R1R2R3N with three different groups be resolved into enantiomers, while a quaternary ammonium salt with four different groups can?

Show answer
  1. The lone pair, N and its three substituents form a pyramid that flips through a planar transition state millions of times per second at room temperature, so a racemic mixture is obtained at once. In R4N+ there is no lone pair and the tetrahedral geometry is fixed.

Rapid pyramidal inversion at nitrogen (barrier ≈ 25 kJ mol-1) interconverts the enantiomers; a quaternary ion has no lone pair and cannot invert.
