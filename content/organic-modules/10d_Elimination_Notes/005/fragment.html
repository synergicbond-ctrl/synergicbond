6. Cyclohexane systems: the trans-diaxial requirement

In a cyclohexane chair, a hydrogen and a leaving group on adjacent carbons are anti-periplanar only when both are axial and on opposite faces of the ring (trans-diaxial). Equatorial leaving groups have no anti-periplanar hydrogens on the ring and cannot eliminate until the ring flips. Consequently, the stereochemistry of ring substrates decides both the rate and the regiochemistry.

Menthyl chloride and neomenthyl chloride illustrate the point. In the stable chair of menthyl chloride, the chlorine, isopropyl and methyl groups are all equatorial. To place chlorine axial, the ring must flip, which also places the isopropyl and methyl axial and costs conformational energy; in that flipped chair the hydrogen on the isopropyl-bearing carbon is equatorial, not axial, so only the hydrogen on the CH2 side is anti-periplanar. Menthyl chloride therefore reacts slowly and gives mainly (in classic work almost exclusively) the less substituted p-menth-2-ene. Neomenthyl chloride has an axial chlorine in its stable chair, with the isopropyl-bearing carbon's hydrogen axial and anti, so it eliminates quickly and gives mainly the more substituted p-menth-3-ene (Zaitsev), together with some p-menth-2-ene.

A tert-butyl group is so large that it holds the ring in the chair with the tert-butyl group equatorial. In cis-4-tert-butylcyclohexyl bromide the bromine is therefore axial and can be eliminated at once; in the trans isomer the bromine is equatorial, and elimination would require the ring to flip to an unfavourable chair with the tert-butyl group axial. The cis isomer reacts much faster than the trans isomer with a strong base, and both give 4-tert-butylcyclohexene.

Key idea. Draw the chair, put the leaving group axial, and check whether a trans-axial hydrogen exists on each neighbour. Only those hydrogens can leave in an E2 reaction.
