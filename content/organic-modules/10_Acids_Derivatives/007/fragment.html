7. Reactions of carboxylic acids

Think of the –COOH group as having four reactive features: (1) the acidic O–H (salts); (2) the electrophilic C=O carbon (acyl substitution: –OH replaced); (3) the acidic α-C–H (HVZ, via the enol); (4) the C–COOH bond (decarboxylation). Everything below belongs to one of these.

(1) O–H: salts, Na, NaHCO₃ (2) C=O: acyl substitution (3) α-C–H: HVZ (4) C–C: decarboxylation

7.1 Acid–base reactions

  • With NaOH, Na2CO3, NaHCO3, NH3: salts. Effervescence of CO2 with NaHCO3 is the test for –COOH (and for stronger acids such as sulfonic acids and 2,4,6-trinitrophenol); ordinary phenols do not react.
  • With active metals: H2 (also given by phenols and alcohols; so this test is not specific).
  • With a Grignard reagent or RLi: the first equivalent merely deprotonates the acid (RH + RCOOMgX). With 2 equivalents of RLi the carboxylate is attacked to give, after hydrolysis of the dianion, a ketone: RCOOH + 2 R′Li → RCOR′.

<h4>7.3 Fischer esterification</h4>

RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H2O, catalysed by strong acid (conc. H2SO4, dry HCl, TsOH). The reaction is reversible and slow without acid.

H⁺ (protonate C=O)⟶ + EtOH (attack at C)⟶ proton transfer⟶ − H₂O⟶ − H⁺⟶
Fischer esterification mechanism: PADPED (Protonation, Addition, Deprotonation, Protonation, Elimination, Deprotonation)
  • Protonation of the carbonyl oxygen makes the carbon far more electrophilic.
  • The alcohol oxygen adds to the carbonyl carbon (rate-determining), forming the tetrahedral intermediate.
  • Proton transfer converts one OH into the good leaving group –OH2+; water leaves and the carbonyl is regenerated.
  • Acyl–oxygen cleavage (the C–OH bond of the acid is broken). Proof: RCOOH + R′18OH gives RCO18OR′; the labelled oxygen stays in the ester.

Equilibrium and yield

For CH3COOH + C2H5OH ⇌ CH3COOC2H5 + H2O at 25 °C, Kc ≈ 4. With 1 mol each, the equilibrium yield is 67 % (x²/(1−x)² = 4 gives x = 2/3). Raising the yield by Le Chatelier: use excess alcohol (3 mol alcohol → 90 %; 10 mol → 97 %), remove water (Dean–Stark trap, conc. H2SO4, molecular sieves) or distil out the ester.

Example 10 Main

Equilibrium yield

Acetic acid (1 mol) and ethanol (3 mol) react to equilibrium, Kc = 4. What fraction of the acid is esterified?

Show solution
  1. Kc = x²/[(1 − x)(3 − x)] = 4 (volume cancels because Δn = 0).
  2. x² = 4(3 − 4x + x²) → 3x² − 16x + 12 = 0 → x = [16 − √(256 − 144)]/6 = (16 − 10.58)/6 = 0.90.
  3. So 90 % of the acid is converted; the other root (4.4) is impossible since x ≤ 1.

Reactivity trends in esterification

  • Alcohol: CH3OH > 1° > 2° ≫ 3° (steric hindrance at the nucleophile). Tertiary alcohols do not esterify satisfactorily by Fischer’s method because under acid they ionise to carbocations and give alkenes (or ethers); use the acid chloride or anhydride with pyridine instead.
  • Acid: HCOOH > CH3COOH > RCH2COOH > R2CHCOOH > R3CCOOH. Pivalic acid and 2,6-disubstituted benzoic acids esterify very slowly (steric hindrance to the tetrahedral intermediate). Newman’s “rule of six”: atoms in the sixth position from the carbonyl oxygen (counting that O as 1) shield the carbonyl carbon and hinder esterification, e.g. 2,6-disubstituted benzoic acids.
  • Substituent electronic effects matter less than steric ones (cf. acidity): the tetrahedral intermediate is little affected by polar effects.
Key idea The 2,6-disubstituted benzoic acids (e.g. 2,6-dimethylbenzoic acid) are very hard to esterify by Fischer’s method but are readily esterified by a different mechanism in cold conc. H2SO4 (via the acylium ion RCO+, AAC1), then pouring into the alcohol. Also, the ester of such an acid is hydrolysed only with great difficulty under normal conditions.
<h4>Other esterification methods</h4>
  • Acyl chloride or anhydride + alcohol (pyridine or base): fast, irreversible; works for 3° alcohols and phenols. Phenols do not esterify with acids directly (the phenoxide O is a poor nucleophile), but acetylate readily with acetic anhydride (aspirin).
  • Diazomethane (CH2N2, ether): RCOOH + CH2N2 → RCOOCH3 + N2; the acid protonates the diazomethane carbon, then the carboxylate attacks the methyldiazonium ion (SN2), N2 leaving. Quantitative, cold, no acid catalyst; only methyl esters.
  • Alkylation of the carboxylate: RCOO−Na+ (or the silver salt) + R′X → RCOOR′ (SN2: works with 1° and methyl halides).
CH₂N₂, ether⟶ +
Benzoic acid + diazomethane → methyl benzoate + N₂
Example 11 Adv

Mechanistic label

In the esterification of benzoic acid with methanol-18O (CH318OH) under H2SO4, and in the base hydrolysis of methyl benzoate in H218O/OH−, where does the 18O end up in each case? What does the result tell about the mechanism?

Show solution
  1. Esterification: ester C6H5CO18OCH3 (labelled), water unlabelled: the alcohol oxygen is retained in the ester, so the C–OH (acyl–oxygen) bond of the acid breaks.
  2. Base hydrolysis in H218O: the label appears in the carboxylate C6H5CO18O−, and the methanol is CH3OH (unlabelled): the acyl–oxygen bond of the ester breaks (BAC2), not the alkyl–oxygen bond.
  3. Both reactions proceed through a tetrahedral intermediate (attack on C=O), not by an SN2 or SN1 at the alkyl carbon.

Example 12 Adv

Counting esters

How many different esters (not counting stereoisomers) can form from a mixture of two acids (CH3COOH, HCOOH) and two alcohols (CH3OH, C2H5OH)? How many if the alcohol is the chiral butan-2-ol (racemic) with acetic acid only, counting stereoisomers?

Show solution
  1. Each acid can combine with each alcohol: 2 × 2 = 4 esters: HCOOCH3, HCOOC2H5, CH3COOCH3, CH3COOC2H5.
  2. Racemic butan-2-ol with acetic acid gives the ester CH3COOCH(CH3)C2H5: a racemic mixture of two enantiomers; 2 stereoisomers (one compound as a racemate), and the Fischer reaction does not touch the stereocentre because the C–O bond of the alcohol is not broken.

7.4 Anhydrides, amides and reduction

  • Anhydrides: two RCOOH + P2O5 (or heat for 1,4- and 1,5-diacids) → (RCO)2O; the industrial route to acetic anhydride is by ketene + acetic acid. In the laboratory use RCOCl + RCOO−Na+, which also makes mixed anhydrides.
  • Amides: RCOOH + NH3 → RCOO−NH4+ (salt, at room temperature) → heat to 150–200 °C → RCONH2 + H2O. The salt is the first product because the acid–base step is much faster than acyl substitution. Amine salts give N-substituted amides on heating; DCC (dicyclohexylcarbodiimide) couples acids and amines at room temperature (peptide chemistry).
  • Reduction: LiAlH4 (then H3O+) reduces RCOOH to RCH2OH (the aldehyde cannot be isolated, being reduced faster than the acid). NaBH4 does not reduce carboxylic acids (the carboxylate is a poor electrophile). B2H6/THF reduces –COOH selectively, even in the presence of ester, nitro and (slowly) ketone groups, because the acid first forms an acyloxyborane (RCOO–BH2) that is reduced rapidly by intramolecular hydride delivery. Catalytic hydrogenation of the acid is difficult.
  • Complete reduction to alkane: HI/red P, 200 °C, RCOOH → RCH3 (and Clemmensen/Wolff–Kishner on keto acids).
B₂H₆, then H₃O⁺⟶
Borane reduces –COOH selectively in the presence of a ketone

7.5 Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) α-halogenation

RCH2COOH + Br2/red P (or PBr3 catalytic) → RCHBrCOOH. The carboxylic acid itself enolises too poorly to halogenate; phosphorus tribromide converts a little of it into the acyl bromide, which enolises readily; the enol is brominated at the α-carbon, and the α-bromo acyl bromide then exchanges with fresh RCOOH to give the α-bromo acid and regenerate RCH2COBr.

PBr₃ (from red P + Br₂)⟶ enolisation⟶ Br₂⟶ RCOOH exchange or H₂O⟶
HVZ mechanism: the acyl bromide is the species that enolises and brominates
  • Requirement: at least one α-hydrogen. Benzoic acid, pivalic acid, formic acid, HCOOH and (CH3)3CCOOH do not undergo HVZ. Only the α-position is halogenated (contrast with free-radical halogenation of the chain).
  • Further steps: α-Br is an excellent leaving group (alpha to C=O, fast SN2): NH3 (excess) gives α-amino acids (alanine from propanoic acid); aqueous KOH then H+ gives α-hydroxy acids; alcoholic KOH gives the α,β-unsaturated acid (E2: possible only when a β-hydrogen exists); CN− gives the malonic acid derivative (after hydrolysis).
  • Chlorination of acetic acid (Cl2, red P or sunlight/I2): CH3COOH → ClCH2COOH → Cl2CHCOOH → Cl3CCOOH (trichloroacetic acid), one Cl at a time, each stage stronger than the previous.
1. Br₂, red P 2. NH₃ (excess)⟶
Propanoic acid → alanine (a racemate)
1. Br₂, red P 2. alc. KOH, Δ 3. H⁺⟶
Butanoic acid → crotonic acid via the α-bromo acid
Trap In HVZ the product is the α-bromo acid, not the β. Also the starting acid must have α-H: HVZ of CH3COOH goes all the way to CBr3COOH if enough Br2 is supplied.

HVZ-based synthesis

Starting from propanoic acid, give reagents for (a) lactic acid, (b) acrylic acid, (c) alanine, (d) methylmalonic acid.

Show solution

  2. (a) aq. KOH (SN2), then H+: CH3CH(OH)COOH, lactic acid.

Soda-lime decarboxylation

CH3COONa + NaOH → CH4 + Na2CO3 (CaO keeps the NaOH dry and stops it attacking the glass). The same works for benzoate (→ benzene) and for salicylate (→ phenol). Rule: each COONa group is replaced by H, so a dicarboxylic acid loses two carbons (phthalic acid gives benzene).

Kolbe electrolysis

Electrolysis of aqueous sodium or potassium carboxylate: at the anode RCOO− → RCOO· + e−; RCOO· → R· + CO2; 2R· → R–R. The cathode gives H2. A mixture of two salts gives three alkanes (R–R, R–R′, R′–R′). Potassium succinate gives ethene (CH2=CH2) because the radical formed after loss of the first CO2 loses the second to give the alkene; potassium fumarate or maleate gives ethyne. Sodium acetate gives ethane; formate gives H2 and CO2.

Example 14 Main

Kolbe products

Give the alkanes formed when a mixture of sodium acetate and sodium propanoate is electrolysed. What is the maximum proportion of the unsymmetrical product?

Show solution
  1. Radicals CH3· and CH3CH2· dimerise in all combinations: ethane (CH3CH3), propane (CH3CH2CH3) and butane (CH3CH2CH2CH3).

<h4>β-Keto acids, malonic acids and related substrates</h4>

Acids with a carbonyl (or C≡N, NO2, another COOH) at the β-position lose CO2 on gentle warming through a six-membered cyclic transition state in which the carboxyl O–H hydrogen is transferred to the carbonyl oxygen while the C–C bond to CO2 breaks: an enol is formed directly (no carbanion), which tautomerises to the ketone or acid.

Δ, − CO₂ (6-membered TS)⟶ tautomerise⟶
Acetoacetic acid → acetone
Δ, 140 °C⟶ +
Malonic acid → acetic acid
Trap Succinic and glutaric acids do not decarboxylate on heating: the cyclic transition state would need a 7- or 8-membered ring or a β-carbonyl. They form the cyclic anhydride instead (heating products of diacids are treated in Part G). Oxalic acid is special: its carboxyl groups are directly bonded and it decomposes (see later). Only β-carbonyl-type systems decarboxylate readily. Also benzoic acid needs soda lime and high temperature; 2,4,6-trinitrobenzoic acid and trichloroacetic acid lose CO2 easily because the leaving carbanion is stabilised.
Example 16 Main

Which decarboxylate on warming?

Which of the following lose CO2 on gentle warming of the free acid? (A) CH3COCH2COOH (B) HOOCCH2COOH (C) HOOCCH2CH2COOH (D) CH3COCOOH (E) CCl3COOH

Show solution
  1. (A) β-keto acid: yes (cyclic TS). (B) malonic acid: β-carboxy: yes. (C) succinic acid: no, forms anhydride. (E) trichloroacetic acid: yes (CCl3− is a reasonably stable carbanion/leaving group) at about 100 °C in water.
  2. (D) pyruvic acid is an α-keto acid, not β: it does not lose CO2 on mere warming (with conc. H2SO4 it loses CO to give acetic acid instead).
  3. Answer: A, B, E.

7.7 Ketones and aldehydes from salts

  • Dry distillation of the calcium (or barium) salt gives a ketone: (CH3COO)2Ca → CH3COCH3 + CaCO3. Calcium formate alone gives HCHO; a mixture of calcium formate and calcium acetate gives CH3CHO (and HCHO, CH3COCH3 as by-products); calcium formate + calcium benzoate gives PhCHO.
  • Calcium adipate gives cyclopentanone, calcium pimelate gives cyclohexanone (see diacids).
  • Industrially, acetic acid vapour over ThO2 or MnO at 300–400 °C gives acetone: 2CH3COOH → (CH3)2CO + CO2 + H2O.

7.8 Electrophilic substitution of benzoic acid

The COOH group is deactivating and meta-directing (–I, –M). Nitration gives m-nitrobenzoic acid; bromination (Br2/Fe) gives m-bromobenzoic acid; sulfonation gives m-sulfobenzoic acid. Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation fail on benzoic acid (the ring is deactivated and the acid complexes the AlCl3).

7.9 Formic acid: the acid that is also an aldehyde

HCOOH has the structure H–C(=O)–OH, so it has an aldehyde-like C–H. It is therefore a reducing agent: it reduces Tollens’ reagent (silver mirror), Fehling’s solution (red Cu2O), HgCl2 (Hg2Cl2 then Hg, white to grey), acidified KMnO4 (decolourised) and itself goes to CO2 + H2O. No other simple monocarboxylic acid does this (but oxalic acid also reduces KMnO4, and tartaric reduces Tollens). With conc. H2SO4 it dehydrates to CO: HCOOH → CO + H2O (a laboratory route to carbon monoxide). It decarboxylates on heating with some metals to H2 + CO2. It is the strongest of the simple unsubstituted monocarboxylic acids (pKa 3.75), and has no alkyl group to lower its acidity. In Cannizzaro-type chemistry its salt is the by-product of HCHO.

Example 17 Main

Distinguishing tests

Name one reagent that distinguishes: (a) formic from acetic acid, (b) benzoic acid from phenol, (c) acetic acid from ethyl acetate, (d) oxalic from malonic acid, (e) acetamide from ammonium acetate.

Show solution

(d) Warm acidified KMnO4 (decolourised by oxalic acid only); malonic acid gives no pink-to-colourless change at room temperature.
