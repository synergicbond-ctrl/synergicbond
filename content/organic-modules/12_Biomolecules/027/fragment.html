5.3 Lipids

Lipids are biomolecules soluble in non-polar organic solvents and sparingly soluble in water. Classes: fats and oils (triacylglycerols), waxes, phospholipids, glycolipids, steroids (cholesterol), terpenes and fat-soluble vitamins.

Fatty acids

palmitic acid (16:0) oleic acid (18:1, cis-9) linoleic acid (18:2, cis-9,12) α-linolenic acid (18:3) arachidonic acid (20:4)
Fatty acids are long, unbranched, almost always even-numbered (biosynthesis by two-carbon units) and the natural double bonds are cis and non-conjugated (methylene-interrupted)
  • Saturated (palmitic C16, stearic C18) pack tightly: solid fats. cis double bonds introduce a bend: oils (liquid). Trans fats (from partial hydrogenation) behave like saturated ones.
  • Essential fatty acids: linoleic (ω-6) and α-linolenic (ω-3); arachidonic acid (C20:4) is made from linoleic acid and is the precursor of prostaglandins.
  • Notation Cx:y: x carbons, y double bonds; ω-n means the first double bond is n carbons from the methyl end.

Triglycerides (fats and oils)

Glycerol esterified with three fatty acids: C3H5(OCOR)3. Simple triglycerides carry one acid (tripalmitin C51H98O6, M = 807.3; triolein C57H104O6, M = 885.5); natural fats are mixed.

<h4>Soaps and detergents</h4>

Soap: sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain fatty acid (C12–C18). A soap molecule has a hydrophilic carboxylate head and a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail; above the critical micelle concentration the tails cluster inside micelles that trap grease, and the charged heads face water.

  • Hard water (Ca2+, Mg2+) precipitates insoluble calcium or magnesium soaps (scum): 2 C17H35COO− + Ca2+ → Ca(C17H35COO)2↓. Synthetic detergents (sodium alkylbenzenesulfonates, sodium lauryl sulfate) do not form scum because their calcium salts are soluble.
  • Biodegradability: straight-chain alkyl (LAS) detergents are degraded by bacteria; branched alkylbenzene sulfonates were not and caused foam pollution.
  • Types: anionic (soaps, LAS), cationic (quaternary ammonium, e.g. cetyltrimethylammonium bromide: germicidal, fabric softener), non-ionic (polyethylene-glycol esters/ethers).

Phospholipids, waxes, steroids

  • Phospholipids: glycerol 1,2-diester of fatty acids with a 3-phosphate bearing choline (lecithin, phosphatidylcholine), ethanolamine (cephalin) or serine; amphipathic, they form the lipid bilayer of cell membranes.
  • Waxes: esters of a long-chain fatty acid with a long-chain monohydric alcohol, e.g. beeswax (myricyl palmitate, C15H31COOC30H61), spermaceti (cetyl palmitate); water-repellent; not hydrolysed by lipase.
  • Steroids: cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene skeleton (three six-membered rings A, B, C and one five-membered ring D, 17 carbons). Cholesterol (C27H46O; 3β-OH, Δ5 double bond, branched C8 side chain) has 8 stereocentres (28 = 256 stereoisomers, only one natural). It is a membrane component and the precursor of bile acids, steroid hormones and vitamin D3.

Example 77 Adv

Saponification value

Calculate the saponification value of (a) tripalmitin (M 807.3) and (b) triolein (M 885.5), and the iodine value of triolein.

Show solution
  1. SV = 3 × 56.1 × 1000/M: (a) 168 300/807.3 = 208.5; (b) 168 300/885.5 = 190.1 mg KOH g−1.
  2. Triolein has 3 C=C: iodine value = 3 × 253.8 × 100/885.5 = 86.0 g I2/100 g.
Example 78 Main

Soap formation

Write the equation for saponification of tristearin with NaOH, and calculate the mass of soap (sodium stearate, M 306.5) from 1.00 kg of tristearin (M 891.5).

Show solution
  1. (C17H35COO)3C3H5 + 3 NaOH → 3 C17H35COONa + C3H5(OH)3.
  2. 1000/891.5 = 1.122 mol → 3.365 mol soap × 306.5 = 1031 g (≈ 1.03 kg; glycerol 103 g by-product).
Example 79 Adv

Hydrogenation volume

How many litres of H2 (STP) are needed to fully hydrogenate 100 g of triolein? Compare with the iodine value.

Show solution
  1. 100 g = 0.1129 mol triolein → 3 C=C → 0.3388 mol H2 → 0.3388 × 22.4 = 7.59 L. Equivalent iodine uptake 0.3388 × 253.8 = 86.0 g, matching the iodine value.
Example 80 Adv

Fat or oil?

Rank by melting point: tristearin, triolein, trilinolein, and explain.

Show solution
  1. Tristearin (saturated, packs well; mp ≈ 72 °C) > triolein (one cis C=C per chain; mp ≈ 5 °C) > trilinolein (two cis C=C per chain; mp ≈ −13 °C). Each cis double bond kinks the chain and lowers packing efficiency.
Example 81 Main

Steroid facts

How many stereocentres does cholesterol have and what is its molecular formula?

Show solution
  1. 8 stereocentres (C-3, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14, 17, 20); formula C27H46O, M = 386.7.
Example 82 Adv

Soap action

Why does a soap fail in hard water but a sulfonate detergent works?

Show solution
  1. Ca2+ and Mg2+ form insoluble salts with the carboxylate of soap (calcium stearate scum). The calcium salts of alkylbenzenesulfonates are soluble, so the surfactant stays in solution.
Part VI — Mechanisms, one step at a time, with an orbital view

Every mechanism below is split into single arrow-pushing steps. After each step the why is given in two languages: the ordinary one (charge, leaving group, stability) and the molecular-orbital (MO) one (which filled orbital donates into which empty orbital). Read the MO lines as the reason the step has the geometry and the rate it has.

Key idea Three MO ideas used throughout. (1) A nucleophile attacks a carbonyl by donating its HOMO (a lone pair) into the C=O π* orbital (the LUMO), approaching at ≈ 107° to the C=O axis (the Bürgi–Dunitz angle) so that overlap with the larger carbon lobe of π* is maximal and repulsion with the filled π orbital is minimal. (2) A lone pair next to an σ* orbital stabilises the molecule if the two are antiperiplanar (n → σ* hyperconjugation): this is the anomeric effect and the reason amides and esters have their shapes. (3) Leaving groups leave from the orbital that is lowest in energy when the bond breaks: a good leaving group has a low-lying σ* (C–X antibonding) orbital, which is easy for a nucleophile or a lone pair to fill.
