14. Thioethers and thiols compared with ethers and alcohols

Sulfur lies below oxygen in the periodic table and its compounds differ from the oxygen analogues in several systematic ways. Thioethers (sulfides, R–S–R′) are named as alkylsulfanyl derivatives (CH3SCH2CH3 is (methylsulfanyl)ethane) or by the older name ethyl methyl sulfide. Thiols (R–SH) replace the –OH of alcohols.

  • Acidity: thiols are considerably more acidic than alcohols (pKa about 10 versus about 16), so they dissolve in aqueous NaOH. The S–H bond is longer and weaker and the thiolate ion is better stabilised.
  • Hydrogen bonding: S–H···S bonds are weak, so thiols boil much lower than alcohols of similar mass. Thioethers do not accept hydrogen bonds well.
  • Nucleophilicity: sulfur is large and polarisable, so thiolates and sulfides are excellent nucleophiles but weak bases. Thiolates displace halides even from secondary halides with little elimination, whereas alkoxides give much alkene.
  • Oxidation: sulfur is oxidised easily, whereas the oxygen of an ether is not.
  • Odour: volatile thiols and sulfides have strong, unpleasant smells.

Reactant:

Reagent: H2O2 (1 equivalent)

Product:

Sulfide to sulfoxide (DMSO). A second equivalent of oxidant gives the sulfone, CH3S(O)2CH3.

Reactant:

Reagent: CH3I

Product:

A sulfide reacts with iodomethane to give a trimethylsulfonium salt; an ether does not give a stable oxonium salt in this way.

Trap. Do not explain the higher acidity of thiols by saying that sulfur is more electronegative. Oxygen is more electronegative; thiols are more acidic because the larger sulfur atom stabilises the negative charge better and the S–H bond is weaker.
