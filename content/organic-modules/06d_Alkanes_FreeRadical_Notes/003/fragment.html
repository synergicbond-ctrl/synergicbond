4. Preparation II: Wurtz, Kolbe, soda lime, Corey–House and carbonyl reduction

Wurtz reaction. Two molecules of alkyl halide couple with sodium in dry ether: 2 R–X + 2 Na → R–R + 2 NaX. The product has twice the carbon number of the halide. The limits are important. (i) A mixture of n different halides gives n(n+1)/2 alkanes, difficult to separate, so the method is useful only for symmetrical alkanes. (ii) Tertiary halides (and often secondary ones) mainly eliminate to alkenes. (iii) Methane cannot be made this way, and a single halide always gives an alkane with an even number of carbons.

Reactant:

Reagent: Na, dry ether

Product:

2,3-Dimethylbutane from 2-bromopropane. Dihalides with Na or Zn close rings: 1,4-dibromobutane gives cyclobutane.

Kolbe electrolysis. Electrolysis of a concentrated aqueous sodium or potassium carboxylate produces a symmetrical alkane at the anode.

  1. At the anode: RCOO− → RCOO• + e−, followed by loss of CO2 to give R•.
  2. Two alkyl radicals couple: 2 R• → R–R. Overall 2 RCOO− → R–R + 2 CO2 + 2 e−.
  3. At the cathode: 2 H2O + 2 e− → H2 + 2 OH−. A mixture of two carboxylates gives three alkanes (R–R, R–R', R'–R').

Decarboxylation with soda lime. Heating a sodium carboxylate with NaOH/CaO removes the carboxyl carbon: RCOONa + NaOH → R–H + Na2CO3. The alkane has one carbon fewer than the acid.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOH, CaO, heat

Product:

Corey–House synthesis. A lithium dialkylcuprate couples with an alkyl halide to give an unsymmetrical alkane cleanly: R2CuLi + R'–X → R–R' + RCu + LiX. R'X should be methyl or primary (secondary is poor, tertiary fails by elimination); R may be any alkyl.

Reactant:

Reagent: (CH3)2CuLi, ether

Product:

Carbonyl to methylene. Clemmensen (Zn–Hg/conc. HCl) and Wolff–Kishner (N2H4, KOH, ethylene glycol, about 470 K) both convert C=O into CH2, again keeping the skeleton.

Reactant:

Reagent: Zn(Hg)/HCl or N2H4/KOH

Product:

Trap. Do not apply Wurtz to prepare an alkane with an odd number of carbons from a single halide; the product always has an even carbon number. Kolbe fails for unsymmetrical targets unless a statistical mixture is acceptable.
