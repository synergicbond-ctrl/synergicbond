6.10 Base hydrolysis of RNA (and why DNA survives)

phosphodiester at 3′-O, HO−⟶
The 2′-OH attacks the adjacent phosphorus to form a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate (uridine model)
  • Step 1. Hydroxide removes the 2′-OH proton (pKa ≈ 12.5); the 2′-O− is placed 2 atoms from the phosphorus.
  • Step 2. The 2′-O− attacks P in line with the leaving 5′-O (an SN2-type displacement at phosphorus through a trigonal-bipyramidal transition state). MO view: the nucleophile's lone pair attacks the σ*(P–O) of the P–O5′ bond from the side opposite the leaving group (apical entry, apical exit): the lobes of σ* are largest along the axis.
  • Step 3. The 5′-O leaves as an alkoxide (it takes a proton from water), giving a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate, which then hydrolyses to a mixture of 2′- and 3′-phosphates.
  • DNA has no 2′-OH, so there is no internal nucleophile and the phosphodiester is stable to base (the DNA phosphodiester is stable on a geological time scale at neutral pH).
