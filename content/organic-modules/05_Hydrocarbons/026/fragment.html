25. Allylic substitution: NBS and high-temperature halogenation

The C–H bond adjacent to a C=C (allylic) is weak (BDE ≈ 88 kcal mol-1 against 98 for a 2° and 101 for a 1° C–H) because the allylic radical is resonance stabilised. A radical halogenation can therefore be steered to the allylic position provided the halogen concentration is kept very low, so that Br2 addition (an ionic reaction that needs a high Br2 concentration) cannot compete. N-Bromosuccinimide (NBS), CCl4, hν or peroxide: NBS reacts with the HBr that is released and supplies a trickle of Br2.

  • Initiation: Br2 → 2 Br• (light).
  • Propagation 1: Br• + allylic C–H → HBr + allylic radical.
  • Supply of Br2: HBr + NBS → Br2 + succinimide (keeps [Br2] tiny).
  • Propagation 2: allylic radical + Br2 → allylic bromide + Br•.

25.1 Allylic rearrangement and product counting

The allylic radical has spin density on both terminal carbons (resonance), so Br2 can attack either end: two constitutional products when the allyl system is unsymmetrical (allylic shift). The product with the more substituted double bond usually predominates.

Trap NBS does not brominate the benzylic position and the allylic position differently in a way you can ignore: both are brominated (benzylic radical is the most stable). NBS also gives no addition across C=C. But if the alkene is treated with NBS in aqueous DMSO (not CCl4/hν), a bromohydrin forms by the ionic pathway.
