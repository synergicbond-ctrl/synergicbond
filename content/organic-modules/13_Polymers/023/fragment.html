19. Physical properties of polymers

  • Crystallinity. Regular chains (HDPE, isotactic PP, nylon, PET) pack into ordered crystallites (lamellae) embedded in amorphous regions, giving strength and a sharp melting point Tm. Irregular chains (atactic PS, branched LDPE, random copolymers) stay amorphous.
  • Glass transition temperature Tg. Below Tg an amorphous polymer is a hard, brittle glass (chain segments frozen); above Tg segmental motion sets in and it becomes leathery or rubbery. Rubbers have Tg far below room temperature (natural rubber about −70 °C), glassy plastics above (PS ≈ 100 °C, PMMA ≈ 105 °C, PVC ≈ 80 °C). Plasticisers (small molecules between chains) lower Tg (flexible PVC).
  • Elasticity of rubber is mainly entropic: in the unstretched state the chains are random coils with many conformations (high entropy); stretching straightens them (lower entropy), and the restoring force is the drive back to disorder. As a consequence a stretched rubber band heats up on rapid stretching and contracts on heating under load (unlike metals).
  • Thermoplastic vs thermoset: thermoplastics are linear/branched (melt and can be remoulded); thermosets are networks (decompose before melting).
  • Additives: plasticisers, fillers (carbon black in tyres), stabilisers, pigments, flame retardants.
Experimental Connection The “rubber heat engine”. A rubber band under constant load shortens when warmed gently (for example with a hair dryer), unlike a metal wire which lengthens. Reason: the entropic restoring force grows in proportion to temperature, so at higher T the coiled state is favoured more strongly.
