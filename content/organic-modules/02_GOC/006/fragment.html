5. Dipole moment

5.1 Definition and units

When two atoms of different electronegativity share a bonding pair, the pair is displaced toward the more electronegative atom, creating partial charges +δ and −δ separated by a distance d. The dipole moment is μ = q × d. It is a vector, drawn here from the positive end to the negative end (the physics convention) with a cross at the positive end: +→. (Some texts draw the arrow from negative to positive; JEE solutions use the symbol with the head at the negative end.) The unit is the debye: 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C m = 10⁻¹⁸ esu cm.

Formula μ = q × d ; q ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and d ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m give μ ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻²⁹ C m ≈ 4.8 D

% ionic character of HCl

Calculate the percentage ionic character of HCl: μ = 1.08 D, bond length = 127.4 pm. (e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C m)

  1. μ(full charges) = e × d = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C × 127.4 × 10⁻¹² m = 2.041 × 10⁻²⁹ C m = 2.041 × 10⁻²⁹ / 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ D = 6.12 D.
  2. % ionic = 1.08 / 6.12 × 100 = 17.6 % (about 18 %).

5.2 The vector method

The dipole moment of a molecule is the vector sum of all the bond moments (and of the lone-pair contributions, which point from the nucleus outward along the lone-pair axis). The vector sum of two moments of magnitudes μ₁ and μ₂ with the angle θ between the two vectors, both drawn outward from a common origin, is

Formula μ² = μ₁² + μ₂² + 2 μ₁ μ₂ cos θ ; for equal moments μ₁ = μ₂ = μ : resultant = 2 μ cos(θ/2)

  • θ = 0° (vectors parallel): resultant = μ₁ + μ₂ — maximum.
  • θ = 180° (vectors opposite): resultant = |μ₁ − μ₂|; zero if equal.
  • θ = 90°: resultant = √(μ₁² + μ₂²).

Two quick rules follow: a molecule whose bond moments cancel by symmetry (CO₂, CS₂, BF₃, CCl₄, SF₆, trans-1,2-dichloroethene, p-dichlorobenzene, benzene, ethene, ethane in the staggered form…) has μ = 0; and a molecule with a lone pair or a missing substituent is almost always polar.

Illustration 5.2 Main

Bond moment of O–H from the molecular dipole

Water has μ = 1.85 D and the H–O–H angle is 104.5°. Find the O–H bond moment (ignore lone-pair contribution).

  1. The two O–H vectors have equal magnitude m and make an angle of 104.5° with each other. The resultant is 2 m cos(104.5°/2) = 2 m cos 52.25°.
  2. cos 52.25° = 0.6122, so 1.85 = 2 m × 0.6122 ⟹ m = 1.51 D.
  3. Because lone pairs also contribute along the same direction as the vector sum, the real O–H bond moment is smaller (about 1.5 D is the textbook result when lone pairs are ignored).
Illustration 5.3 Main

Two dipoles at an angle

Two bond moments of 1.5 D and 2.0 D make an angle of 60°. What is the resultant?

  1. μ² = 1.5² + 2.0² + 2 × 1.5 × 2.0 × cos 60° = 2.25 + 4.00 + 3.00 = 9.25.
  2. μ = √9.25 = 3.04 D.

5.3 Disubstituted benzenes

For a monosubstituted benzene C₆H₅X the group moment points along the C–X axis. In disubstituted benzenes the two axes are separated by 60° (ortho), 120° (meta) and 180° (para), so the ortho isomer has the largest resultant (smallest angle) and the para isomer the smallest. Illustration 5.4 Adv

Dipole moments of the three nitrochlorobenzenes

Using μ(C₆H₅NO₂) = 4.0 D and μ(C₆H₅Cl) = 1.6 D (both directed ring → substituent), calculate the dipole moments of the ortho, meta and para isomers of chloronitrobenzene by the vector method.

  1. Ortho (θ = 60°): μ² = 4.0² + 1.6² + 2(4.0)(1.6) cos 60° = 16 + 2.56 + 6.4 = 24.96 ⟹ μ = 5.0 D.
  2. Meta (θ = 120°): μ² = 16 + 2.56 + 12.8 × (−0.5) = 12.16 ⟹ μ = 3.5 D.
  3. Para (θ = 180°): μ = 4.0 − 1.6 = 2.4 D.
  4. Order ortho > meta > para. (Measured: 4.3, 3.4, 2.5 D, so the pattern is confirmed.)

Illustration 5.5 Adv

Order of dipole moments

Arrange in increasing order of dipole moment: p-dichlorobenzene, m-dichlorobenzene, o-dichlorobenzene, chlorobenzene.

  1. p-Dichlorobenzene: bond moments cancel, μ = 0.
  2. m-Dichlorobenzene: 2 × 1.7 × cos 60° = 1.7 D (θ = 120°, resultant equals μ).
  3. Chlorobenzene: 1.7 D; the meta isomer has the same value as the monosubstituted compound by the vector calculation (experimental 1.7 for both).
  4. o-Dichlorobenzene: 2 × 1.7 × cos 30° = 2.9 D calculated (2.3 measured) — the largest.
  5. p-dichlorobenzene < chlorobenzene ≈ m-dichlorobenzene < o-dichlorobenzene.

A rotating dipole

Suppose the anti conformer of 1,2-dichloroethane has μ = 0 and the gauche conformer (dihedral 60°) has μ = 2.6 D, and the measured moment of the equilibrium mixture at 25 °C is 1.1 D. Estimate the total mole fraction of gauche conformer using μ²(observed) = x μ²(gauche) + (1 − x) μ²(anti).

  1. The measured value is a weighted average of squares of the moments (the dipole-moment-squared averaging that applies to a rapidly interconverting mixture). μ²(observed) = 1.21; μ²(gauche) = 6.76; μ²(anti) = 0.
  2. 1.21 = x × 6.76 ⟹ x = 0.18, i.e. about 18 % of the molecules are gauche at that temperature (the two equivalent gauche forms together).

5.5 Applications of the dipole moment

  • Shape of molecules: μ = 0 for CO₂ shows it is linear; μ ≠ 0 for SO₂ and H₂O shows they are bent; NH₃ μ = 1.47 D shows pyramidal rather than planar.
  • Separating geometric isomers: the cis isomer has the larger dipole moment than the trans in 1,2-disubstituted ethenes; the more polar isomer has higher boiling point, but the less polar (more symmetrical) trans isomer usually has the higher melting point.
  • Solubility and miscibility: like dissolves like; polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents; non-polar solutes in non-polar solvents.
  • Position of substituents in aromatic rings: o-, m-, p- isomers distinguished by μ (para lowest).
  • Percent ionic character of a polar covalent bond.
  • Intermolecular forces: dipole–dipole attraction raises boiling points (e.g. propanone 56 °C vs butane −0.5 °C, similar masses).
IMP Summary sheet — Dipole moment. (1) μ = q × d, a vector from δ+ to δ−. (2) Resultant of two vectors: √(μ₁² + μ₂² + 2μ₁μ₂ cos θ). (3) Lone pairs contribute. (4) Symmetry gives zero. (5) o > m > p for identical polar groups. (6) Donor (NH₂, CH₃) and acceptor (NO₂) at para: moments add. (7) CH₃F, CH₃Cl order is a charge × length effect, not just electronegativity. (8) NH₃ > NF₃ because the lone-pair moment opposes the N–F moments.
