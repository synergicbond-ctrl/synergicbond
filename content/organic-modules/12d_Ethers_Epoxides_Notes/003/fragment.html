4. Preparation of ethers

Four general laboratory routes are used. The Williamson synthesis is the most versatile: an alkoxide (made from an alcohol with Na or NaH, or a phenoxide made with NaOH) displaces a halide from an alkyl halide by an SN2 reaction. Alkoxymercuration–demercuration adds an alcohol across a double bond with Markovnikov orientation and no carbocation rearrangement. Acid-catalysed dehydration of a primary alcohol at moderate temperature gives a symmetrical ether. Diazomethane methylates acidic hydroxyl groups, particularly phenols.

Diazomethane gives anisole and nitrogen; simple alcohols need a Lewis acid catalyst.

Acid dehydration fails for unsymmetrical ethers. A mixture of two different primary alcohols ROH and R′OH produces R–O–R, R′–O–R′ and R–O–R′ in a statistical mixture, which is hard to separate. Secondary and tertiary alcohols eliminate to alkenes under these conditions because their carbocations lose a proton faster than they are trapped by a second alcohol.

Key idea. Mixture of n different primary alcohols with acid at 140 °C gives n(n+1)/2 different ethers: n symmetrical and n(n−1)/2 unsymmetrical.
