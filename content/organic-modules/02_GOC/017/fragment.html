16. Reactive intermediates and their stability

Most organic reactions proceed through short-lived species (carbocations, carbanions, radicals, carbenes, nitrenes, arynes) formed by bond fission. The stability of the intermediate decides the rate of its formation and therefore the product (Hammond postulate: the more stable the intermediate, the lower the energy of the transition state leading to it).

16.1 Bond fission and curved arrows

  • Homolytic fission: the bonding pair is split equally; one electron goes to each atom (radicals). Occurs in non-polar solvents, in the gas phase, with heat or light, or with radical initiators (peroxides). Written with fish-hook (half-headed) arrows.
  • Heterolytic fission: both electrons go to the more electronegative atom: cation + anion. Occurs in polar solvents and with acid/base catalysis. Written with full-headed arrows.

16.2 Carbocations

A carbocation has a positively charged carbon with six electrons, an empty p-orbital perpendicular to the plane of the three σ-bonds (sp², 120°). Its stability depends on how well the positive charge is dispersed.

Carbocations from the most to the least stable (conventional order)
IMP Order of carbocation stability (JEE convention): Ph₃C⁺ > Ph₂CH⁺ > PhCH₂⁺ ≈ CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺ > 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺ > vinyl⁺ > aryl⁺. Remember also: a cation next to O or N with a lone pair (CH₃OCH₂⁺, R₂NCH₂⁺) is more stable than a comparable 3° alkyl cation; a 3° benzylic or 2° allylic cation is more stable than the corresponding primary benzylic/allylic cation (add the alkyl stabilisation on top of the resonance). Vinyl and aryl cations (the cationic carbon is sp-hybridised, the empty orbital in the plane) are extremely unstable.
Exam Trap Allyl vs benzyl vs tertiary. Resonance is generally a stronger stabiliser than hyperconjugation, so primary benzyl and primary allyl cations are placed above the 3° alkyl cation in the convention. (Real gas-phase and solvolysis data show the t-butyl cation is, in fact, comparable to the benzyl cation.) If a question lists both, follow the convention; if it compares a secondary allyl with a primary allyl, or a tertiary benzylic with a primary benzylic, the more substituted one is always more stable.

Diphenylmethyl and an oxocarbenium

Which is more stable, CH₃O–CH₂⁺ or (CH₃)₃C⁺? Explain with resonance.

  1. In CH₃–O⁺=CH₂ every atom has an octet and there is a real C=O⁺ π-bond: strong stabilisation. (CH₃)₃C⁺ is stabilised only by hyperconjugation (9 C–H bonds). The oxocarbenium ion is the more stable.

Para-substituted benzyl cations

Rearrangements of carbocations

A carbocation rearranges when a 1,2-shift of H⁻ (hydride) or R⁻ (alkyl or aryl, with its bonding pair) can convert it into a more stable cation. The migrating group moves with its electron pair to the positive carbon.

Product of a rearrangement

When 3-methylbutan-2-ol is heated with concentrated HBr the main product is 2-bromo-2-methylbutane. Explain.

  1. Protonation of OH and loss of water gives the secondary cation; the tertiary hydrogen on the neighbouring carbon shifts with its electron pair to the cationic carbon (1,2-hydride shift), forming the more stable tertiary cation; Br⁻ captures it.

Illustration 16.6 Adv

Bridgehead cations

Why is the 1-norbornyl (bridgehead) carbocation extremely unstable although it is tertiary?

  1. A carbocation wants to be planar (sp²) so that the empty p-orbital can be stabilised by hyperconjugation with three σ-bonds. At a bridgehead of a small bicyclic skeleton the carbon cannot flatten: the cage holds it pyramidal.
  2. The empty orbital therefore points away from the bonds and no effective hyperconjugation is possible: the cation is far less stable than an acyclic tertiary cation (1-bromonorbornane does not ionise under SN1 conditions).

16.3 Carbanions

A carbanion has a carbon with three bonds and a lone pair (eight electrons), usually sp³ and pyramidal (like NH₃); when the lone pair is conjugated with a π-system or an electron-withdrawing group the carbon becomes sp² and planar. Carbanion stability is the reverse of the order for carbocations and parallels the acidity of the parent C–H (stronger acid = more stable carbanion).

16.4 Free radicals

A free radical has an unpaired electron. Alkyl radicals are nearly planar (sp²) with the odd electron in a p-orbital, or very shallowly pyramidal; they are stabilised by hyperconjugation and by conjugation. The best guide to relative stability is the C–H bond dissociation enthalpy (BDE): the weaker the C–H bond, the more stable the radical produced.

Major product of bromination

2-Methylpropane (isobutane) is brominated at 125 °C with Br₂/light. Which monobromide is the main product and why? What would chlorination give?

  1. Isobutane has 9 primary H and 1 tertiary H. For bromination the selectivity is about 1640 : 1 per H in favour of the tertiary H: ratio 1640 × 1 : 9 × 1, so the tertiary bromide is more than 99 % of the product: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.
  2. For chlorination 5 × 1 : 9 × 1 gives about 36 % tertiary and 64 % primary chloride (1 : 3.8 : 5 per H): a mixture.

16.5 Carbenes, nitrenes and arynes

  • Carbenes (:CR₂) have a neutral divalent carbon with six electrons. Singlet carbenes (paired electrons in one sp² orbital, an empty p-orbital) are electrophilic and add stereospecifically to alkenes to give cyclopropanes; triplet carbenes (two unpaired electrons, bent or linear) react in two steps as diradicals. Dichlorocarbene :CCl₂ (from CHCl₃ + KOH) is stabilised by the lone pairs on chlorine and is the electrophile in the Reimer–Tiemann reaction.
  • Stability of carbenes: stabilised by donor lone pairs and aromatic character: :CCl₂ and :CF₂ are more stable than :CH₂; N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) are isolable.
  • Nitrenes (R–N:) are the nitrogen analogues with a sextet at N (generated from azides RN₃ by heat or light, with N₂ loss, and in the Hofmann, Curtius and Lossen rearrangements as the transient species).
  • Arynes (benzyne, C₆H₄): the 'triple bond' of benzyne is formed by sideways overlap of two sp² orbitals in the plane of the ring; it is highly strained and reactive; it is generated from o-halo-aryl halides with strong bases (NaNH₂), or by diazotisation of anthranilic acid, and is trapped by dienes (furan) and nucleophiles.

Illustration 16.12 Adv

Hybridisation of reactive intermediates

Give the hybridisation and shape of (a) CH₃⁺, (b) CH₃⁻, (c) •CH₃, (d) singlet :CH₂.

  1. (a) 3 σ-bonds, no lone pair, empty p: sp², trigonal planar (H–C–H 120°).
  2. (b) 3 σ-bonds + lone pair: sp³, pyramidal (like NH₃).
  3. (c) 3 σ-bonds + one electron in p: sp², planar (very slightly pyramidal in some radicals).
  4. (d) 2 σ-bonds + lone pair: sp², bent (H–C–H ≈ 102°).
