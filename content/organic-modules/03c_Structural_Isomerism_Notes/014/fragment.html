15. The boundary between structural isomerism and stereoisomerism

Isomers are divided into two broad classes. Structural (constitutional) isomers have different connectivity of atoms: chain, position, functional group, metamers, ring-chain and tautomers all belong here. Stereoisomers have identical connectivity but differ in the arrangement of atoms in space: geometrical isomers (cis-trans, E-Z) and optical isomers (enantiomers and diastereomers), and conformers.

trans- and cis-but-2-ene are stereoisomers (geometrical).

(S)- and (R)-butan-2-ol are stereoisomers (enantiomers).

But-2-ene and but-1-ene are structural (position) isomers.

A quick test: write the name or the bond list of each atom (who is bonded to whom), ignoring wedges and dashes. If the connectivity differs, the pair is structural; if it is identical but the compounds are not superimposable, the pair is stereoisomeric. In counting problems the wording matters: 'structural isomers' means ignore stereo; 'all isomers' or 'including stereoisomers' means add geometrical and optical forms. For C4H8 there are 5 structural isomers (3 alkenes, 2 cycloalkanes); including geometrical isomerism the total is 6 (cis- and trans-but-2-ene); no other C4H8 isomer adds optical forms.

Key idea. Same connectivity: stereoisomers. Different connectivity: structural isomers. Tautomers and conformers are not the same: tautomers are structural isomers (different atom attachments), conformers are stereo-relationships interconverted by rotation about single bonds.
Trap. Counting problems on alkenes: C6H12 has 13 acyclic alkene structures, but 18 when cis/trans and optical forms are included. State the convention first, then count.
