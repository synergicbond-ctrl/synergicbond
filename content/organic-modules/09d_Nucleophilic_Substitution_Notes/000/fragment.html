1. The SN2 mechanism: backside attack and inversion

In a bimolecular nucleophilic substitution (SN2) the nucleophile attacks the carbon that carries the leaving group from the side directly opposite the leaving group. Bond formation and bond breaking occur in a single concerted step, so no intermediate is formed. The carbon passes through a trigonal-bipyramidal transition state in which the entering and leaving groups lie on one axis (each partly bonded) and the other three substituents lie in a plane perpendicular to it.

  1. The nucleophile approaches along the C–X axis, 180° from the leaving group, where the antibonding σ*(C–X) orbital has its large lobe.
  2. At the transition state the carbon is sp2-like and planar with respect to its three remaining groups; the Nu···C···X unit is nearly linear.
  3. As X– leaves, the three remaining groups flip to the other side like an umbrella in a gale, so the configuration is inverted (Walden inversion).

Reagent: NaCN in DMSO (SN2)

Product:

(S)-2-Bromobutane gives (R)-2-methylbutanenitrile. The new group takes the place opposite to Br, so the geometry is inverted; here the CIP priority order is unchanged (Br or CN first, then ethyl, methyl, H), hence the descriptor changes from S to R.

Because one molecule of substrate and one nucleophile meet in the rate-determining (and only) step, the rate law is second order overall: rate = k[RX][Nu–]. Doubling either concentration doubles the rate; doubling both quadruples it.

Key idea. SN2 = one step, no intermediate, rate = k[RX][Nu], 100% inversion of geometry at the reacting carbon. A change in R/S label follows only if the priority order of the groups is unchanged (see Section 10).
