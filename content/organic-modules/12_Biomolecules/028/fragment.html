6.1 Mutarotation: how the ring opens and recloses

Step 1: base removes the anomeric O–H⟶ Step 2: C1–O5 breaks; C=O forms⟶
α-D-glucopyranose → alkoxide → open-chain aldehyde with an alkoxide at O-5
  • Step 1 (deprotonation). A base (water or hydroxide) removes the hemiacetal O–H. Why: the hemiacetal OH of glucose is a slightly stronger acid than an ordinary alcohol (pKa ≈ 12.3 for the sugar) because the adjacent ring oxygen withdraws electron density inductively; the anion is the species that can push electrons.
  • Step 2 (ring opening). The O− lone pair (HOMO) pushes into the C-1–O-5 bond: the C=O π bond forms and O-5 leaves as an alkoxide. MO view: the O− lone pair is aligned antiperiplanar to the C1–O5 σ* orbital (the same n → σ* alignment as in the anomeric effect). Donation fills σ* and breaks the bond while forming the new C=O π.
  • Step 3 (proton transfer). The alkoxide at C-5 takes a proton from water. Why: alkoxides are strongly basic; the open-chain hydroxy-aldehyde is the neutral intermediate (≈ 0.002 % of the mixture).
Step 4: O-5 attacks C-1 from the opposite face⟶ Step 5: protonation⟶
Reclosure from the other face gives β-D-glucopyranose
  • Step 4 (ring closure). The C-5 oxygen attacks the aldehyde carbon (6-exo-trig). MO view: the nucleophile's lone pair (HOMO) overlaps with the large carbon lobe of the C=O π* (LUMO) along the Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory. Either face of the planar carbonyl is open, giving α or β.
  • Step 5 (protonation). The new alkoxide picks up a proton. At equilibrium β predominates (≈ 64 %) because all substituents are equatorial.
Trap Acid catalysis works the other way: protonation of the ring oxygen makes it a better leaving group (the C1–O5 σ* is lowered), and the aldehyde appears as the protonated oxocarbenium. Mutarotation is catalysed by both acid and base, and it is slowest near pH 5.
