1.8 Disaccharides

Two monosaccharide units joined by a glycosidic bond from the anomeric carbon of one unit (the glycosyl donor) to an –OH (or the anomeric –OH) of the other. A disaccharide is reducing if at least one anomeric carbon is free (an OH there); non-reducing if both anomeric carbons take part in the link (sucrose, trehalose).

Key idea Naming. α-D-Glucopyranosyl-(1→4)-D-glucopyranose = maltose: the first unit is the glycosyl (the one using its anomeric carbon), the second is the glucose carrying the free OH at C-4. A disaccharide whose second unit keeps its anomeric –OH shows mutarotation and gives an osazone and Fehling's test; the free end is called the reducing end.
Formula sucrose + H₂O → glucose + fructose ; 342 g → 180 g + 180 g (mass of products = 360 g)
<h4>Sucrose and invert sugar</h4>

Sucrose is dextrorotatory ([α] +66.5°). Acid or invertase hydrolysis gives equimolar D-glucose ([α] +52.5°) and D-fructose ([α] −92.4°). The mixture is laevorotatory, so the sign of rotation is inverted, and the mixture is called invert sugar. It is sweeter than sucrose because of the fructose, and does not crystallise easily (used in honey-like confectionery).

Trap Do not say "sucrose is hydrolysed to glucose + glucose". Maltose gives 2 glucose; lactose gives glucose + galactose; cellobiose gives 2 glucose; sucrose gives glucose + fructose.

Structure proof of maltose by methylation

Methylate fully; hydrolyse the acetal link: from the non-reducing (glycosyl) unit you get 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-methyl-D-glucose; from the reducing (aglycone) unit 2,3,6-tri-O-methyl-D-glucose. The free OH at C-4 of the second product marks the glycosidic oxygen. The α-link in maltose (versus β in cellobiose) was established from the enzymes that cleave them.

Example 22 Adv

Invert sugar calculation

Calculate the specific rotation of the invert sugar formed from 171 g of sucrose, and the mass of invert sugar.

Show solution
  1. 171 g sucrose = 0.50 mol → 0.50 mol glucose + 0.50 mol fructose.
  2. Mass of products = 0.5 × 180 + 0.5 × 180 = 180 g (the extra 9 g is water consumed).
  3. For equal masses of glucose and fructose, the mean is (+52.5 − 92.4)/2 = −19.95° ≈ −20° (laevorotatory).
Example 23 Adv

Is it reducing?

Classify as reducing/non-reducing and give the hydrolysis products: (a) maltose, (b) lactose, (c) sucrose, (d) trehalose, (e) cellobiose.

Show solution
  1. (a) Reducing; 2 D-glucose. (b) Reducing; D-galactose + D-glucose. (c) Non-reducing; D-glucose + D-fructose. (d) Non-reducing; 2 D-glucose. (e) Reducing; 2 D-glucose.
Example 24 Adv

Linkage from methylation data

A disaccharide X (C12H22O11) reduces Fehling's solution. Complete methylation followed by hydrolysis gives equal amounts of 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-methyl-D-glucose and 2,3,4-tri-O-methyl-D-glucose. What is the linkage?

Show solution
  1. The tetra-O-methyl compound is the non-reducing end (anomeric OH only; the ring O at C-5). The tri-O-methyl compound lacks OMe at C-1 (hemiacetal OH, free after hydrolysis) and at C-6.
  2. So the second unit used O-6 to bind the first: a (1→6) link, like isomaltose or gentiobiose. Fehling reduction shows the second unit has a free anomeric OH.
Example 25 Adv

Hydrolysis stoichiometry

How many grams of water are needed to hydrolyse 3.42 g of maltose completely, and how many grams of glucose result?

Show solution
  1. M(maltose) = 342; 3.42 g = 0.0100 mol. Each mol needs 1 mol H2O: 0.0100 × 18 = 0.18 g.
  2. Glucose = 0.0200 mol × 180 = 3.60 g.
