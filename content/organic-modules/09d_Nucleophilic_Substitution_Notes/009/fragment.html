10. Stereochemical bookkeeping: inversion, retention and labels

Stereochemistry in substitution has two separate layers. The first is geometric: SN2 inverts the spatial arrangement, SN1 racemises (with some net inversion), SNi and double inversion retain. The second is the CIP descriptor, which must be recalculated for the product from the priorities of its substituents. Inversion of geometry changes R to S only if the new group takes the same priority rank as the leaving group did. If the nucleophile ranks differently relative to the other substituents the descriptor may stay the same even though the molecule inverted.

(S)-2-Bromo-1-fluoropropane gives the nitrile with inversion of geometry, but both compounds are S. In the substrate the order is Br &gt; CH2F &gt; CH3 &gt; H; in the product CH2F (F,H,H) outranks CN (N,N,N), so the two highest ranks have exchanged.

  1. Draw the substrate and place the leaving group and the entering group at the same position of the SMILES or drawing.
  2. Flip the geometry for SN2 (retain it for SNi or double inversion; make a racemic mixture for SN1).
  3. Assign CIP priorities in the product anew, using the first point of difference outward from the stereocentre.
  4. Only then state R or S.

Sequences of substitutions are handled one step at a time: (S)-2-bromobutane + I– → (R)-2-iodobutane (inversion), then + CN– → (S)-2-methylbutanenitrile (second inversion, net retention overall). Net configuration is retained after an even number of SN2 steps and inverted after an odd number. Exchange of I– for I– on a pure enantiomer inverts each substrate molecule that reacts, so the loss of optical activity is twice as fast as the isotopic exchange: after half of the molecules have exchanged, none of the optical activity is left.

Key idea. Geometry first, label second. Ring systems: SN2 on a cis-1,4-disubstituted cyclohexane gives the trans product, and vice versa.
