1. The carboxylic acid family at a glance

Every compound in this chapter contains the acyl group R–C(=O)– joined to a heteroatom (or to a carbon, in the case of nitriles after hydrolysis). Because the carbonyl carbon is already at the carboxylic oxidation level (three bonds to heteroatoms: one C=O counts as two, plus one C–X), all the derivatives interconvert by substitution (no net oxidation or reduction), and they all hydrolyse to the same parent acid. This one idea organises the whole chapter.

Key idea Carboxylic acids, acyl halides, anhydrides, esters, amides and nitriles are all at the same oxidation level. Interconverting them needs only water, an alcohol or an amine (substitution), never an oxidant or a reducing agent. Going to aldehyde, alcohol or amine (by LiAlH4 etc.) is reduction; going from alcohol or aldehyde up to the acid is oxidation.

Cyclic esters and amides, imides, hydroxamic acid, acyl azide, urea and carbamate
  • Lactone = cyclic ester; lactam = cyclic amide; imide = two acyl groups on one nitrogen; anhydride = two acyl groups on one oxygen.
  • Hydroxamic acid RCONHOH (the substrate of the Lossen rearrangement); acyl azide RCON3 (Curtius rearrangement); urea and carbamates are derivatives of carbonic acid, not of a carboxylic acid, but behave like amides.
  • Ketene CH2=C=O (from pyrolysis of acetone or acetic acid at 700 °C) is the internal anhydride of acetic acid; isocyanate R–N=C=O is the key intermediate of all the “degradation” rearrangements.
