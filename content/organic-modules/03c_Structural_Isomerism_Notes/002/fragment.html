3. Functional-group isomerism

Functional isomers have the same molecular formula but different functional groups, so their chemistry is quite different. This is the richest source of isomers in problems. Familiar pairs are: alcohols and ethers (CnH2n+2O), aldehydes and ketones, together with unsaturated alcohols, enols and cyclic ethers (CnH2nO), carboxylic acids and esters (CnH2nO2), nitroalkanes and alkyl nitrites (CnH2n+1NO2), and alkynes, allenes and dienes (CnH2n-2). Primary, secondary and tertiary amines with the same formula are often grouped here too, since they react differently with nitrous acid and benzenesulfonyl chloride.

Ethanol and dimethyl ether, C2H6O: alcohol versus ether.

C3H6O: propanal (aldehyde), propanone (ketone), prop-2-en-1-ol (unsaturated alcohol).

C2H4O2: ethanoic acid, methyl methanoate, hydroxyethanal (2-hydroxyacetaldehyde).

Isomers of different classes differ greatly in physical properties as well. Ethanol, which forms hydrogen bonds, boils at 78 °C, whereas dimethyl ether, which cannot, is a gas at room temperature (boiling point about -24 °C). Ethanoic acid dimerises through two hydrogen bonds and boils at 118 °C, while methyl methanoate boils near 32 °C.

Key idea. Functional isomers must have an identical molecular formula and therefore identical degrees of unsaturation. Use the formula to decide which groups are possible before drawing anything.
Trap. Do not confuse functional isomerism with homology. Ethanol (C2H6O) and propanol (C3H8O) are different compounds with different formulas; they are homologues, not isomers.
