28. The Perkin reaction

An aromatic aldehyde (no α-H) is heated (about 453 K, 180 °C) with an acid anhydride that has α-H and the sodium or potassium salt of the same acid to give an α,β-unsaturated carboxylic acid (trans-cinnamic acid from PhCHO + (CH3CO)2O + CH3COONa).

+ CH₃COONa, 453 K; then H₂O/H⁺⟶
Perkin: benzaldehyde → (E)-cinnamic acid
  • Step 1: acetate removes an α-H of the anhydride to give the anhydride enolate (the anhydride is the more acidic partner; acetate is the base).
  • Step 2: the enolate attacks the aldehyde C=O (aldol-type step).
  • Step 3: intramolecular acyl transfer, then loss of acetate (E1cB-like elimination of the β-acyloxy group) gives the unsaturated mixed anhydride.
  • Step 4: hydrolysis gives the unsaturated acid.
  • The carboxylate salt must be the salt of the same acid as the anhydride (otherwise mixed anhydrides form).
  • With propanoic anhydride and sodium propanoate, PhCHO gives α-methylcinnamic acid.
  • Salicylaldehyde gives coumarin (after lactonisation of the cis-isomer).
+ CH₃CH₂COONa, Δ⟶
With the propanoic system: α-methylcinnamic acid
+ CH₃COONa, Δ⟶
Salicylaldehyde → coumarin (2H-chromen-2-one): the Perkin product (E-acid) isomerises and lactonises through the cis-isomer
Trap The Perkin reaction needs an aromatic aldehyde (non-enolisable) for a good yield; an aliphatic aldehyde would self-condense. The anhydride must have at least two α-H: after the aldol step one α-H is needed for the elimination of water. An anhydride with a single α-H, such as isobutyric anhydride ((CH3)2CHCO)2O, stops at the β-hydroxy acid.
