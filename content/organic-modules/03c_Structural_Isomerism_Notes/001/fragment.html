2. Position isomerism

Position isomers have the same carbon skeleton and the same functional group (or substituent, or multiple bond), but the group sits at a different carbon. Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol are position isomers; so are but-1-ene and but-2-ene (the double bond moves), 1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane, and the three xylenes (the two methyl groups are placed ortho, meta or para on the ring).

Position isomers: propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol.

Position isomers: but-1-ene and but-2-ene (cis/trans of but-2-ene is a separate, stereochemical matter).

The idea is the same for every substituent: keep the skeleton fixed, then walk the group along all non-equivalent carbons. On a symmetrical skeleton, positions related by symmetry give the same compound. In butane, carbons 1 and 4 are equivalent and carbons 2 and 3 are equivalent, so only two positions exist and only two alcohols result from the unbranched skeleton, butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol. In propane there are two non-equivalent positions (end and middle), so there are two monochloropropanes.

Position isomers on the butane skeleton: butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol.

Position isomerism is common on benzene rings. Chlorotoluene exists as ortho, meta and para isomers; positions 2 and 6 of the ring are equivalent, as are 3 and 5, so a ring bearing a single group and one more group has only three distinct placements.

o-, m- and p-chlorotoluene.

Key idea. Position isomerism moves the group or the multiple bond along a fixed skeleton. Count only positions that are not related by a symmetry operation of the skeleton.
Trap. Propene has only one possible position for its double bond: CH2=CH-CH3 and CH3-CH=CH2 are the same molecule written from opposite ends. Always check that two drawings are not just the same structure read from the other end.
