16. Specific nucleophilic additions

16.1 Addition of water: hydrates Covered above (15.3). Catalysed by acid and by base. Methanal in water is 99.9 % methanediol; formalin contains the hydrate and its oligomers. 16.2 Addition of HCN: cyanohydrins Aldehydes and most ketones add HCN to give cyanohydrins (2-hydroxynitriles). Pure HCN is toxic and weakly acidic (pKa 9.2), so the reaction is run with NaCN or KCN plus a mineral acid (or with a trace of base catalyst). The nucleophile is CN−: the rate is maximal when a small amount of base is present (to raise [CN−]) and falls in strong acid (all cyanide is HCN) and in strong base (no H+ to protonate the alkoxide).

+ NaCN, H⁺⟶ H₃O⁺, Δ⟶
Ethanal → lactonitrile → lactic acid (2-hydroxypropanoic acid)
HCN, trace OH⁻⟶ conc. H₂SO₄, Δ⟶

KCN, H⁺⟶ H₃O⁺⟶

Products and uses: acid hydrolysis of the CN gives an α-hydroxy acid (one more carbon than the original carbonyl compound); reduction (LiAlH4) gives a β-amino alcohol; the cyanohydrin of an aldehyde is a protected acyl anion equivalent after O-protection (umpolung). The cyanohydrin product is chiral from an aldehyde or unsymmetrical ketone and is racemic unless an enzyme (hydroxynitrile lyase) or chiral catalyst is used.

Trap Hindered ketones (di-isopropyl ketone, diaryl ketones, benzophenone) do not give cyanohydrins in good yield; equilibrium lies to the left. Aryl ketones such as acetophenone add HCN poorly and benzophenone not at all.
16.3 Addition of sodium hydrogensulphite (bisulphite)
Aldehydes, methyl ketones and unhindered cyclic ketones (up to about C8) add NaHSO3 to give a crystalline α-hydroxysulphonate (bisulphite addition compound). The nucleophile is sulphur of HSO3− (S has the lone pair).
NaHSO₃ (saturated, aq.)⟶ Na₂CO₃ or dil. HCl⟶
Bisulphite adduct: solid, water soluble; reversible

Uses: (i) separation and purification – the adduct is a water-soluble crystalline salt; non-carbonyl impurities are extracted away; the carbonyl compound is regenerated with dilute acid or aqueous carbonate; (ii) conversion into cyanohydrin without free HCN (adduct + KCN gives cyanohydrin plus Na2SO3).

Key idea Bisulphite adducts form with: all aldehydes; methyl ketones (CH3COR) and cyclic ketones of small ring size; they do not form with diaryl ketones, with di-isopropyl or higher hindered ketones, or with acetophenone (aryl ketone hindered).
16.4 Addition of alcohols: hemiacetals, acetals, ketals
In the presence of dry HCl (or p-toluenesulphonic acid), an aldehyde adds one alcohol to give a hemiacetal (also called hemiketal for ketones), which is usually unstable and cannot be isolated, and then a second alcohol to give an acetal with loss of water. The second step goes through an oxocarbenium ion:
> Step 1: the carbonyl O is protonated; ROH attacks carbon; loss of H+ gives the hemiacetal.
> Step 2: the hemiacetal OH is protonated and leaves as H2O, assisted by the lone pair of the OR oxygen, which forms the resonance-stabilised oxocarbenium ion R–CH=O+R′.
> Step 3: a second ROH attacks the oxocarbenium ion; loss of H+ gives the acetal.
CH₃OH, H⁺⟶ CH₃OH, H⁺, −H₂O⟶
Ethanal → hemiacetal → dimethyl acetal (1,1-dimethoxyethane)
+ H⁺ (p-TsOH), −H₂O, Dean–Stark⟶
Cyclic ketal from propanone + ethane-1,2-diol (a 1,3-dioxolane)

For ketones the equilibrium is much less favourable than for aldehydes, so water is removed (Dean–Stark trap, molecular sieves, or an orthoester HC(OEt)3) to drive it. Cyclic acetals and ketals from 1,2- and 1,3-diols are formed more readily than open-chain ones because the second step is intramolecular (entropy).

Key idea Acetals and ketals are ethers of a gem-diol. They are stable to bases, nucleophiles (RMgX, RLi, LiAlH4, NaBH4, enolates), and to oxidising agents of the chromate/PCC type. They are hydrolysed to the carbonyl compound by aqueous acid. That makes them the standard protecting group for C=O.

Protecting the ketone while the ester is reduced: methyl 4-oxopentanoate → 5-hydroxypentan-2-one

16.5 Addition of thiols: thioacetals Thiols and dithiols (e.g. propane-1,3-dithiol, with BF3 or HCl) convert C=O into thioacetals (1,3-dithianes). Raney nickel then removes sulphur and hydrogenolyses the C–S bonds, giving CH2: this is a neutral way to reduce C=O to CH2 (the Mozingo reduction). The C–H of the aldehyde-derived dithiane is acidic (pKa about 31), so deprotonation with BuLi gives a nucleophilic acyl-anion equivalent (Corey–Seebach umpolung).

+ BF₃⟶ Raney Ni, H₂⟶
Ethanal → 1,3-dithiane → propane (CO → CH₂ under neutral conditions)

Also, Grignard reagents add to the C=O of a ketone in the presence of an ester more readily than to the ester (ketone > ester in reactivity).
16.7 Addition of acetylides
Terminal alkynes are acidic (pKa 25): NaNH2 gives the acetylide, which adds to C=O to give a propargyl alcohol. With ethyne and methanal in the presence of copper acetylide, the industrial Reppe process gives but-2-yne-1,4-diol.

16.10 Catalytic hydrogenation and the MPV reduction
H2/Ni (or Pt, Pd) reduces C=O to CH–OH, but also C=C, so in an enone H2/Pd gives the saturated ketone first. Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley (MPV): Al(OiPr)3 in propan-2-ol transfers hydride from the isopropoxide to the carbonyl through a six-membered cyclic transition state. It is reversible; distilling off acetone shifts it to products. It is chemoselective for C=O (leaves C=C and NO2). Its reverse is the Oppenauer oxidation (excess acetone).

Example 12 Main

Grignard retrosynthesis

Prepare 2-methylbutan-2-ol in three different ways using a Grignard reagent and a carbonyl compound.

Show solution
  1. The target is (CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3: a 3° alcohol with groups CH3, CH3, C2H5 on carbon. Remove each group in turn.
  2. (i) Remove CH3: ketone is CH3COC2H5 (butanone) + CH3MgX. (ii) Remove the other CH3: same pair, so the same route. (iii) Remove C2H5: ketone is propanone + C2H5MgX.
  3. So there are two distinct routes: butanone + CH3MgBr, or propanone + C2H5MgBr. A third route uses an ester: methyl or ethyl propanoate + 2 CH3MgBr (two identical groups come from the Grignard reagent).

Example 13 Adv

Chemoselectivity

Predict the product of NaBH4 on: (a) 4-oxopentanal; (b) CH3COCH2CH2COOH; (c) PhCH=CHCHO.

Show solution
  1. (a) NaBH4 reduces the CHO faster than the ketone, and with excess reagent both: pentane-1,4-diol with excess; with one equivalent at low temperature mostly 5-hydroxypentan-2-one (aldehyde more reactive).
  2. (b) Only the ketone is reduced (acid does not react, apart from forming a carboxylate with the basic reagent): 4-hydroxypentanoic acid, which readily cyclises to γ-valerolactone on acidification.
  3. (c) 1,2-Reduction of C=O with minor 1,4: cinnamyl alcohol PhCH=CHCH2OH is the main product with NaBH4/CeCl3; plain NaBH4 gives some saturated alcohol.
Example 14 Adv

Protecting group

Why must the CHO group of 4-bromobutanal be protected before the Grignard reagent is prepared, and how is it done?

Show solution
  1. A Grignard reagent is a carbanion and the CHO of another molecule (or of the same molecule) is an electrophile. If 4-bromobutanal were treated with Mg, the organomagnesium compound would attack the CHO group of its neighbours and give oligomers.
  2. Protect first: ethane-1,2-diol, H+ (Dean–Stark) gives the cyclic acetal, which is inert to Mg and to RMgX. Then Mg/ether, then the electrophile, then aqueous acid removes the protecting group.
HOCH₂CH₂OH, H⁺, −H₂O⟶ Mg, dry ether⟶
The protected bromide forms a usable Grignard reagent
