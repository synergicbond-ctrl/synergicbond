7. Peroxide formation and safe handling

Ethers that have hydrogen on the carbon next to oxygen react slowly with atmospheric oxygen in a radical chain process (autoxidation) to give hydroperoxides and, on further reaction, polymeric peroxides. The α-C–H bond is weak because the resulting radical is stabilised by the adjacent oxygen lone pair. Light and trace metals accelerate the process.

  1. Initiation: a radical abstracts an α-hydrogen from the ether, giving R2C(•)–OR′.
  2. Propagation 1: this carbon radical adds to O2 giving a peroxyl radical R2C(OO•)–OR′.
  3. Propagation 2: the peroxyl radical abstracts an α-hydrogen from another ether molecule, giving the hydroperoxide R2C(OOH)–OR′ and a new carbon radical.

Reactant:

Reagent: O2 (air), light, long storage

Product:

1-Ethoxyethyl hydroperoxide from diethyl ether.

Peroxides are less volatile than the ether, so they concentrate in the distillation flask and may detonate when the ether is distilled to dryness or when a cap is unscrewed on an old bottle. Ethers that form peroxides most readily include diisopropyl ether (a weak tertiary α-C–H bond), THF, 1,4-dioxane and diethyl ether. Ethers without any α-hydrogen, such as diphenyl ether and di-tert-butyl ether, cannot undergo this reaction.

  • Test: shaking with acidified aqueous KI gives iodine (yellow-brown; blue-black with starch).
  • Removal: shaking with acidified iron(II) sulfate solution (Fe2+ reduces the O–O bond) or passing through activated alumina.
  • Prevention: store in amber bottles, add a radical inhibitor (BHT), date the container and never distil to dryness.
Trap. The peroxide test must be done before every distillation of an old ether sample. The absence of peroxides is not assumed for a bottle of unknown age.
