12. Hofmann elimination of amines

Amines are poor leaving groups (NR2−), but a quaternary ammonium ion carries a good, neutral leaving group (a tertiary amine). The Hofmann elimination takes advantage of this: exhaustive methylation with excess CH3I gives the quaternary ammonium iodide; silver oxide in water exchanges iodide for hydroxide; heating then expels the tertiary amine and forms the alkene.

  1. Step 1: R–NH2 + excess CH3I → R–N+(CH3)3 I− (exhaustive methylation).
  2. Step 2: Ag2O, H2O → R–N+(CH3)3 OH− (precipitation of AgI drives the exchange).
  3. Step 3: heat (100–200 °C): the hydroxide removes a β-hydrogen (E2, anti) and N(CH3)3 leaves.

Reactant:

Reagent: (1) excess CH₃I; (2) Ag₂O, H₂O; (3) heat

Product:

Butan-2-amine gives but-1-ene (Hofmann) as the major alkene.

The orientation is Hofmann: the least substituted alkene is the major product. The leaving group is large, and in the transition state the base takes the most accessible and most acidic hydrogen, normally from a methyl group or from the less substituted β-carbon. Sulfonium salts R–S+(CH3)2 OH− behave similarly.

Ring amines are opened by repeated exhaustive methylation. Piperidine needs two equivalents of CH3I for the first quaternization (N–H becomes N(CH3)2+), the first Hofmann elimination opens the ring to N,N-dimethylpent-4-en-1-amine, and a third CH3I followed by a second Hofmann elimination releases penta-1,4-diene and trimethylamine. The number of carbon atoms in the longest chain and the position of the double bonds show where the nitrogen was attached.

Reactant:

Reagent: (1) CH₃I (excess); (2) Ag₂O, H₂O, heat; repeated twice

Product:

Penta-1,4-diene is formed in two Hofmann steps.

Key idea. Hofmann elimination reports the original position of nitrogen in the alkene product; the rule 'least substituted alkene' applies unless a β-hydrogen is made much more acidic (for example next to a phenyl or carbonyl group), when it goes to the conjugated alkene.
