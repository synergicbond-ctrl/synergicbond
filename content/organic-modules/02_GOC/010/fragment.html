9. Resonance: rules, drawing and stability

9.1 The idea

When a single Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule, we draw two or more contributing (canonical) structures; the real molecule is a resonance hybrid, not an equilibrium between them and not an average in time — a single species whose structure is a weighted mixture. The contributors differ only in the positions of electrons (π-electrons and lone pairs), never in the positions of atoms. The double-headed arrow ↔ joins them; the equilibrium arrow ⇌ is for different molecules (tautomers, conformers).

IMP Resonance energy is the difference in energy between the real hybrid and the most stable contributor. It is large when (i) there are many contributors of similar energy, (ii) the contributors are equivalent (benzene, carboxylate), (iii) the orbitals overlap well (planarity).

9.2 The rules for writing resonance structures

  • Rule 1 — Only electrons move. The skeleton of σ-bonded atoms is fixed. Moving a hydrogen atom changes the compound (a tautomer), not the contributor.
  • Rule 2 — The number of paired and unpaired electrons is constant. All contributors have the same total charge and the same number of electrons.
  • Rule 3 — Only π-electrons and lone pairs move. σ-electrons stay (except in hyperconjugation, which is treated separately).
  • Rule 4 — Every atom stays within the valence limit. Second-row atoms (C, N, O, F) never exceed eight electrons. Counting electrons around each atom after each arrow is the best check.
  • Rule 5 — The contributing atoms must lie in one plane (or nearly so) so that their p-orbitals overlap; a bond that is twisted cannot take part (steric inhibition of resonance, treated in its own section below).

Curved arrows start at an electron source (a lone pair or a π-bond) and end at the place where the electrons will be (a new π-bond or a lone pair on the next atom); the tail never starts at an atom or at a positive charge.

9.4 Which contributor is more important? The ranking rules

When the contributors are not equivalent, the real structure resembles the most stable one. Compare them in this order:

  • 1. Octets: a structure in which all second-row atoms have a complete octet is better than one with a sextet. Example: CH₃–O⁺=CH₂ (all octets) is better than CH₃–O–CH₂⁺ (carbon has a sextet).
  • 2. Number of covalent bonds: more bonds (and more π-bonds) is better.
  • 3. Charge separation: fewer charges, or none, is better. A neutral structure beats a zwitterion; if charges are needed, the shortest separation, and opposite charges close together, is better.
  • 4. Charge on the right atom: a negative charge belongs on the more electronegative atom (O > N > C); a positive charge on the less electronegative atom.
  • 5. Aromatic (and non-quinoid) rings preserved: a contributor that keeps the benzene ring intact is better than one that disrupts it.
  • 6. Equivalent contributors: a set of equivalent contributors gives the greatest stabilisation.
Illustration 9.1 Main

Rank the contributors

Rank the contributors of CH₂=CH–CH=O (acrolein) in order of importance.

  1. I: all octets, no charges, three π/σ bonds as drawn — the major contributor.
  2. II: charge-separated but the negative charge is on O (more electronegative) and the positive on C; all atoms except the terminal carbon have octets (the cation has a sextet) — a significant minor contributor, and it explains why the β-carbon is electrophilic (Michael addition).
  3. III: negative on the less electronegative carbon and positive on oxygen, which would have only a sextet — it does not contribute.
  4. I > II ≫ III.

Which contributor of the enolate is better?

For the enolate of acetone, compare CH₂=C(CH₃)–O⁻ with ⁻CH₂–C(=O)CH₃. Which is the more important contributor and which atom is the nucleophile in alkylation reactions?

  1. In the O⁻ form the negative charge is on oxygen (the more electronegative atom); in the C⁻ form it is on carbon but the structure has a strong C=O bond (more covalent energy).
  2. The two are of similar energy; the O⁻ form is slightly more important for the charge distribution (higher charge density on O), yet the C⁻ form is why the carbon of an enolate acts as the soft nucleophile (alkylation at carbon, aldol).

9.5 Resonance energy and what it can be measured from

The resonance energy of a molecule is estimated from heats of hydrogenation (or of combustion). If benzene contained three isolated C=C bonds, hydrogenating it would release three times the heat released by cyclohexene. The measured values show how much more stable benzene is.

Resonance energies: benzene ≈ 150, naphthalene ≈ 255, anthracene ≈ 350, phenanthrene ≈ 380 kJ mol⁻¹; per ring the resonance energy decreases with fusion (benzene 150 per ring, naphthalene ≈ 128 per ring), so anthracene and phenanthrene undergo addition reactions more readily than benzene.
Illustration 9.3 Main

Resonance energy from heats of hydrogenation

ΔH(hydrogenation) of cyclohexene = −120 kJ mol⁻¹ and of benzene = −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the resonance energy of benzene.

  1. Hypothetical 'cyclohexatriene' with three isolated double bonds: ΔH = 3 × (−120) = −360 kJ mol⁻¹.
  2. Observed ΔH = −208 kJ mol⁻¹.
  3. Resonance energy = 360 − 208 = 152 kJ mol⁻¹ (benzene is 152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than the localised triene).
Illustration 9.4 Adv

Resonance energy of naphthalene

The enthalpy of combustion of naphthalene is −5156 kJ mol⁻¹, whereas the value calculated for a hypothetical localised Kekulé structure (from bond energies, taken as given) is −5411 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the resonance energy and compare it with benzene.

  1. RE = 5411 − 5156 = 255 kJ mol⁻¹.
  2. Naphthalene has two rings, so 255/2 ≈ 128 kJ mol⁻¹ per ring, about 85 % of the value for benzene (152): fused rings share their π-electrons, so each ring is slightly less aromatic. This is why naphthalene is more reactive than benzene in both substitution and addition.

9.7 Resonance, bond order and bond length

The bond order in the hybrid is the average of the bond orders in the contributors: carboxylate 1.5; nitrate N–O 1⅓; carbonate C–O 1⅓; benzene C–C 1.5; allyl C–C 1.5; ozone O–O 1.5; amide C–N between 1 and 2 (about 1.3–1.4). The bond lengths follow the bond orders; the experimental lengths (benzene 139 pm; carbonate 129 pm, between C–O 143 and C=O 122) confirm this.

Illustration 9.7 Adv

Bond order in nitrate

Calculate the N–O bond order in NO₃⁻ and the formal charge on each oxygen in the hybrid.

  1. Each contributor has two N–O single bonds and one N=O. The total number of N–O bond units is 4 spread over three bonds: bond order = 4/3 = 1.33.
  2. Each oxygen carries −1 in two contributors and 0 in one: average = −2/3. The hybrid has a charge of −⅔ on each O and +1 on N (total −1).
