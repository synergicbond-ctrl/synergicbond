25A. Mechanism atlas: step by step, with the reason for every step and an orbital view

Each mechanism below is written one step at a time. After each step you will find why it happens (charge, leaving-group ability, acidity) and, where it helps, the molecular-orbital reason (which filled orbital donates into which empty orbital).

A.1 The orbital picture of the carbonyl group and of nucleophilic attack

  • The C=O π bond is built from a carbon 2p and an oxygen 2p orbital. Oxygen is more electronegative, so the bonding π orbital lies mostly on O (it is the HOMO-side) and the antibonding π* orbital lies mostly on carbon. The π* orbital is the LUMO of every carbonyl compound; its large coefficient on carbon is why every nucleophile attacks carbon.
  • A nucleophile donates its lone pair (its HOMO) into the π* orbital. Overlap is best along a line that makes an angle of about 107° with the C=O bond (the Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory): attack from directly above the carbon (90°) gives good overlap with the π* lobe but ignores the repulsion from the filled π and σ orbitals; attack in the plane (120°) is blocked by the substituents. The compromise is about 107°.
  • As the new bond forms, electrons flow into π*: the C=O bond order falls from 2 to 1, the carbon changes from sp² to sp³ and the oxygen becomes an alkoxide. This is the tetrahedral intermediate.
  • Substituent effect in the MO language. In an acyl derivative R–C(=O)–Y the lone pair on Y (nY) mixes with π* of C=O. This donation raises the energy of the LUMO and puts electron density on carbon, which makes the derivative less electrophilic. Nitrogen has the highest-energy lone pair (it overlaps best with π*), so amides have the highest LUMO and are the least reactive. Chlorine’s 3p lone pair is large and diffuse and overlaps poorly with the carbon 2p, so little donation occurs: the LUMO of an acyl chloride is low and it is the most reactive. Ester and anhydride oxygens are in between (in an anhydride the oxygen lone pair is shared between two carbonyls, so each gets less).
  • The amide bond as a three-orbital system. N lone pair + C=O π and π* give three molecular orbitals, as in an allyl anion: a bonding π₁ (spread over O–C–N), a non-bonding π₂ with a node at carbon (mostly O and N), and an antibonding π₃. Four electrons occupy π₁ and π₂. The occupied π₁ gives the C–N bond partial double-bond character (restricted rotation, planarity). The same picture describes the carboxylate ion O–C–O− (the “4-electron, 3-centre” bond): the two C–O bonds become identical because both oxygens share π₁ and π₂ equally.
  • Why the leaving group matters in step 2. In the tetrahedral intermediate the oxygen lone pair (nO−, now a high-energy alkoxide lone pair) pushes into the σ* orbital of the C–Y bond and expels Y−. The lower the σ*(C–Y) energy (Cl > OCOR > OR > NH2) and the more stable Y−, the easier this step. Only a good “push” from the alkoxide and a good “pull” into σ* together expel Y−.

A.2 Acid chloride formation from RCOOH and SOCl2, step by step

1. O attacks S, Cl⁻ leaves⟶ 2. Cl⁻ adds to C=O⟶ 3. C=O re-forms, SO₂ and Cl⁻ leave⟶
  • Step 1. The carbonyl oxygen or OH oxygen of the acid attacks sulfur of SOCl2 (S is electrophilic: its σ*(S–Cl) orbitals are low-lying), and Cl− leaves. Why: S(IV) bonded to O and two Cl is strongly polarised. Result: the acyl chlorosulfite RCO–O–S(=O)Cl, in which the O–H has been replaced by a much better leaving group (the chlorosulfite group collapses to SO2 + Cl−).
  • Step 2. Chloride, liberated in step 1, adds to the carbonyl carbon (π* acceptor, a better electrophile now because the O substituent is electron-withdrawing). Why: Cl− is a weak nucleophile towards a plain carboxylic acid, but the chlorosulfite makes the carbon strongly electrophilic.
  • Step 3. The alkoxide pushes down, re-forming C=O, and the C–O bond breaks; the leaving group –O–S(=O)Cl falls apart into SO2 and Cl−. Why the reaction is irreversible: SO2 and HCl escape as gases, so there is no reverse reaction.

A.3 Fischer esterification (PADPED), with the reason for each step

1. protonate C=O⟶ 2. EtOH adds⟶ 3. proton transfer⟶ 4. H₂O leaves⟶ 5. deprotonate⟶
  • 1. Protonation of the carbonyl O. Without a catalyst RCOOH is a poor electrophile (π* is raised by donation from OH). Protonating O lowers the energy of π* a lot (it is now like a carbocation) and spreads positive charge onto carbon (resonance: C+–OH). Which O is protonated? The carbonyl O, because the resulting cation is stabilised by both OH groups (a symmetrical, resonance-stabilised cation); protonating the OH oxygen gives no resonance.
  • 2. Addition of the alcohol. The oxygen lone pair of EtOH (HOMO) attacks the carbon (LUMO = π* of the protonated acid). This is the slow, rate-determining step. Why EtOH and not water at this stage: both can add, but the reaction is under equilibrium control and the alcohol is present in excess.
  • 3. Proton transfer. The oxonium O–H+ loses H+ to a neutral O of the intermediate (solvent-mediated shuttle). Why: one OH must become –OH2+, a good leaving group (water), because HO− is a poor leaving group.
  • 4. Loss of water. A lone pair on the remaining O (OH or OEt) pushes in to re-form C=O (n → σ*(C–OH2+)); the neutral H2O leaves. Protonation can occur on either –OH or –OEt, and either can leave; the net outcome is set by the relative stability of ester + water versus acid + alcohol (Kc ≈ 4).
  • 5. Deprotonation. The protonated ester loses H+ to the solvent, regenerating the catalyst. Every step is reversible: Fischer esterification is an equilibrium (Kc ≈ 4), and the reverse is AAC2 hydrolysis. By the principle of microscopic reversibility the reverse follows the same path in the opposite order.

A.4 Saponification of an ester (BAC2), step by step

1. HO⁻ adds to C=O⟶ 2. C=O re-forms, EtO⁻ leaves⟶ 3. EtO⁻ removes H⁺⟶
B_AC2: add, eliminate, then the irreversible proton transfer
  • 1. Hydroxide adds. HO− is a strong nucleophile: its lone pair enters the π* orbital of the ester C=O. The ester carbonyl is less electrophilic than an acid chloride, so this is the slow step (rate = k[ester][OH−]). Electron-withdrawing groups on the acyl part lower the LUMO and speed the reaction (positive Hammett ρ); bulky groups slow it (sp² → sp³ crowding).
  • 2. Elimination of the alkoxide. The tetrahedral O− re-forms the C=O and expels EtO−. Why EtO− and not HO−? Either could leave, and the equilibrium between them explains the 18O exchange; the net reaction goes forward because of step 3.
  • 3. Proton transfer. EtO− (conjugate acid pKa 16) deprotonates the carboxylic acid (pKa 4.8). This step is practically irreversible (ΔpKa ≈ 11, K ≈ 1011) and pulls the whole sequence to completion. The carboxylate is resonance-stabilised and no longer electrophilic, so it cannot be attacked again: this is why base “consumes” a full equivalent and why saponification is irreversible.

A.5 Claisen condensation, step by step

1. EtO⁻ removes α-H⟶ 2. enolate C attacks C=O of 2nd ester⟶ 3. EtO⁻ leaves⟶ 4. EtO⁻ removes the doubly activated H⟶ 5. H₃O⁺⟶
  • 1. Enolate formation. The α C–H σ bond lies parallel to the π* orbital of C=O (that overlap is what makes the H acidic): when the C–H electrons move into the C–C region they are delocalised over C=C–O−. Equilibrium is unfavourable (pKa 25.6 against 15.9) but a tiny amount of enolate is enough.
  • 2. C–C bond formation. The enolate HOMO (largest coefficient on carbon) attacks the π* LUMO of a second molecule of ester. Why carbon and not oxygen? The HOMO of an enolate has its largest coefficient on carbon, and C–C bond formation is thermodynamically more favourable (soft–soft interaction).
  • 3. Loss of ethoxide. The tetrahedral alkoxide re-forms C=O and expels EtO−: a nucleophilic acyl substitution. Product: the β-keto ester, which is more acidic than any species in the mixture (pKa 10.7).
  • 4. The driving deprotonation. EtO− removes the H between the two carbonyls. The anion is delocalised over O–C–C–C–O (a five-atom, six-electron π system). ΔpKa ≈ 5: K ≈ 105. This step makes the whole sequence downhill; it is why one full equivalent of base is needed and why an ester with only one α-H fails.
  • 5. Work-up. Acid protonates the enolate.

A.6 Hofmann rearrangement, step by step

1. NaOBr (Br⁺ transfer to N)⟶ 2. HO⁻ removes N–H⟶ 3. CH₃ shifts to N, Br⁻ leaves (concerted)⟶ 4. HO⁻ adds to C=O⟶ 5. − CO₂, protonate N⟶
  • 1. N-Bromination. Hypobromite supplies Br+ (O–Br bond polarised: Br is the electrophilic end). The amide nitrogen lone pair attacks Br. Result: the N-bromoamide RCONHBr, whose N–H is now more acidic (the Br and the C=O both withdraw).
  • 2. Deprotonation. Hydroxide removes the N–H proton, giving the bromoamide anion, a nitrogen with a negative charge, a leaving group (Br) and an acyl group.
  • 3. 1,2-Shift (the key step). The R–C(O) σ bond is anti-periplanar to the N–Br σ* orbital; as Br− leaves, the σ(C–R) electrons migrate to nitrogen (the bond donating into σ*(N–Br)), and simultaneously the nitrogen lone pair forms the new π bond to carbon: C=N. Concerted migration explains the retention of configuration at R (R is never free). An acyl nitrene is not an intermediate; it would give side-products. Electron-releasing R migrates faster, in line with R acting as the donor.
  • 4. Hydroxide adds to the isocyanate carbon. The isocyanate R–N=C=O has a strongly electrophilic central carbon (low π* with two heteroatoms). Product: the carbamate anion RNH–COO−.
  • 5. Decarboxylation. The carbamate loses CO2 (an acid carbamic acid RNHCOOH is unstable), leaving RNH2. CO2 is trapped as carbonate in the alkaline solution. In alcohol the isocyanate is intercepted by the alcohol (urethane) instead.

A.7 Decarboxylation of a β-keto acid: a pericyclic reaction

six-membered cyclic TS (O–H···O=C)⟶ + tautomerise⟶
  • Step 1 (one concerted step). Three electron pairs move in a ring of six atoms: the carboxylic O–H bond transfers H to the ketone O; the C–C bond to the CO2 breaks; a C=C double bond forms between the two former carbonyl-bearing carbons. This is a pericyclic (retro-ene type) reaction with six electrons in a cyclic array, so it is thermally allowed and proceeds through a chair-like, aromatic-type transition state (Hückel 4n+2). Why this works only for β-keto acids: the ketone C=O is in the right place to accept the proton and keep the six-atom ring; a succinic or glutaric acid would need a larger ring and a place to accept the electrons.
  • Step 2. The enol tautomerises to the ketone by proton transfer (acid or base catalysed).

A.8 Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky reaction

1. PBr₃⟶ 2. enolise (HBr cat.)⟶ 3. Br₂ adds to C=C (enol HOMO → Br–Br σ*)⟶ 4. RCOOH exchange⟶
  • 1. PBr3 (from P + Br2) converts a little acid to the acyl bromide. Why the acid does not brominate directly: RCOOH enolises too little (its enol is not accessible).
  • 2. The acyl bromide enolises (α-H acidic because Br is a stronger –I acceptor than OH and does not donate as well by resonance), giving the enol, which has a high-energy HOMO on the α-carbon.
  • 3. The enol HOMO (a C=C π with large coefficient on the α-carbon) attacks Br2 (donating into the σ* of Br–Br); the cation is the protonated carbonyl; loss of H+ gives the α-bromo acyl bromide. Why only α: the enol exists only toward the α-carbon.
  • 4. The α-bromo acyl bromide exchanges with another molecule of acid (acyl-transfer): product α-bromo acid, and the new acyl bromide continues the cycle.

A.9 Why two carbonyl groups acidify a C–H: orbital view

In a 1,3-dicarbonyl anion the electron pair on carbon overlaps with two π* orbitals (two C=O groups). The system O–C–C–C–O has five atoms and six π electrons: three bonding MOs are filled (π₁, π₂ non-bonding-like, π₃), and the lowest MO (π₁) is delocalised over all five atoms. The more π* acceptors flank the carbon, the lower the energy of the filled orbital, so the pKa falls: acetone 19, ethyl acetoacetate 10.7, acetylacetone 9, 2-nitropropane/Meldrum’s acid lower still.
