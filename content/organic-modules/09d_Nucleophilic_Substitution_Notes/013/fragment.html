14. Competition with elimination, and substitution at carbonyl carbon

Elimination is always the competitor of substitution. As a brief guide: tertiary halides with strong bases give E2; tertiary halides in neutral polar protic solvent give a mixture of SN1 and E1; secondary halides with strong, bulky bases favour E2; primary halides with unhindered nucleophiles favour SN2; high temperature favours elimination (higher entropy change). Weakly basic good nucleophiles (I–, N3–, RS–, CN–) favour substitution even at secondary carbon.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOEt, EtOH, warm (E2 predominates)

Product:

No substitution product is expected at an appreciable level.

Substitution at a carbonyl (acyl) carbon looks similar on paper (Cl is replaced by Nu) but is mechanistically different. The carbonyl carbon is sp2 and planar, so the nucleophile adds to the π* of C=O from above the plane to form a tetrahedral intermediate; the leaving group is expelled in a second step (addition–elimination). There is no backside attack and no SN2-type pentacoordinate transition state; the carbonyl carbon is not a stereocentre, so inversion does not arise.

Reactant:

Reagent: CH3CH2OH (addition–elimination via a tetrahedral intermediate)

Product:

Acyl chlorides react with alcohols much faster than ethyl chloride does, because the carbonyl carbon is more electrophilic and the tetrahedral intermediate is easily formed.

Trap. Do not use SN1 or SN2 vocabulary for acyl substitution; a reaction at sp2 carbonyl carbon through a tetrahedral intermediate is nucleophilic acyl substitution.
