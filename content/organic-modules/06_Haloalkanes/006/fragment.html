5. The SN2 reaction in detail

5.1 Mechanism and transition state

The nucleophile uses its lone pair to attack the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group (backside attack, 180° to the C–X bond), because (i) that is where the large lobe of the σ* orbital of the C–X bond lies, and (ii) electrostatic repulsion between Nu− and Xδ− is minimised. In the transition state the carbon is sp2-hybridised and planar with the three non-reacting substituents in one plane; Nu and X sit above and below on a collinear axis (trigonal-bipyramidal, five-coordinate). The partial charge is shared between Nu and X; there is no discrete intermediate, just one energy maximum.

+ SN2: inversion⟶ +
The three groups on carbon flip like an umbrella in a gale; the configuration is inverted (Walden inversion). Racemic/other mixtures are not formed.
Key idea Inversion of configuration is a spatial statement, not a CIP statement. The R/S letter may or may not change, depending on whether the priorities of the new set of four groups happen to be in the same order. (R)-2-bromobutane + OH− → (S)-butan-2-ol: here the letter changes because the order Br > C2H5 > CH3 > H becomes OH > C2H5 > CH3 > H and the geometry flips. In (S)-2-bromopropanoic acid + OH− the priorities Br > COOH > CH3 > H become OH > COOH > CH3 > H (same order), so the product is (R)-lactic acid: the letter changes because the geometry is inverted.

(R)-2-Bromobutane gives (S)-butan-2-ol with complete inversion.
NaI, acetone⟶
(R)-2-Bromooctane to (S)-2-iodooctane: the classical Hughes demonstration. Iodide then displaces iodide from the product, so racemisation is eventually reached.
Trap Racemisation by repeated SN2. When the nucleophile is also a good leaving group (I− on RI, Br− on RBr), each exchange inverts one molecule, so the optical activity of an enantiopure R–X falls to zero even though every act is a pure inversion. Because only half of the molecules need to be inverted to give a 50:50 mixture, the rate of loss of rotation is exactly twice the rate of halide exchange. That is the signature of SN2; for SN1 (planar cation, both faces) racemisation and exchange would occur at the same rate.

Energy profile. A single maximum; the activation energy Ea depends on the strain in the crowded five-coordinate transition state and on the energy needed to stretch C–X. Bimolecular means the order in the rate law is one in substrate and one in nucleophile: doubling [Nu] doubles the rate, doubling both quadruples it.

Formula rate = k [R–X][Nu−] (units of k: L mol−1 s−1)

  • α-Branching (primary → secondary → tertiary) slows SN2 steeply.
  • β-Branching also slows it: neopentyl halides are 3° in neither sense but block the back side with the three methyl groups of the quaternary carbon, so they are practically inert to SN2 although they are primary. They also cannot do SN1 easily (primary cation), unless a concerted methyl shift occurs.
  • Allyl and benzyl react faster than ethyl: the p-orbitals of the π-system overlap with the p-orbital character of the central sp2 carbon in the transition state and stabilise it.
  • α-Halo carbonyl compounds (X–CH2–C=O) are extremely fast in SN2 for the same reason (π* overlap lowers the TS energy) but very slow in SN1 (adjacent electron-withdrawing C=O destabilises the cation).
  • Bridgehead and cyclopropyl halides: cannot achieve the backside geometry (and the planar TS), and are inert to SN2.
Trap Primary halide does not always mean fast SN2. Neopentyl (CH3)3CCH2X and similar β,β-disubstituted primary halides are as unreactive as tertiary halides in SN2. Conversely, a secondary allylic or benzylic halide may still react by SN2 faster than a simple primary one.

5.3 Effect of the nucleophile

A nucleophile donates an electron pair to carbon (a kinetic property); a base donates it to a proton (a thermodynamic property). The two are correlated but not identical.

  • Same atom, same row: nucleophilicity parallels basicity. RO− > HO− > ArO− > RCOO− > ROH ≈ H2O. Anion > conjugate neutral: HO− ≫ H2O; NH2− ≫ NH3.
  • Across a row (same period): more electropositive atom is more nucleophilic: R3C− > R2N− > RO− > F−; and NH3 > H2O.
  • Down a group (in protic solvents): nucleophilicity increases with size and polarisability although basicity decreases: I− > Br− > Cl− > F−; RS− ≫ RO−; R3P > R3N. This reverses in polar aprotic solvents (F− > Cl− > Br− > I−) because the strong solvation of small anions by H-bonding is removed.
  • Steric hindrance cuts nucleophilicity but not basicity: tert-butoxide is a strong base but a poor nucleophile; ethoxide is a better nucleophile.
  • Good nucleophiles that are weak bases (excellent for SN2 on 2° halides without E2): I−, Br−, N3−, CN−, RS−, PhS−, R3P, NCS−, and (in SN2) RC≡C− (this one is a strong base, so be careful).

Shortcut A quick way to judge nucleophilicity in water/alcohol: the larger and more polarisable the nucleophilic atom, the better; negative beats neutral; and extensive hydrogen-bond solvation (small anions: F−, HO−) cuts nucleophilicity. In DMF/DMSO the order simply follows basicity (naked anions).

5.4 Effect of the solvent

  • Polar protic solvents (H2O, CH3OH, C2H5OH, CH3COOH): have O–H or N–H, solvate anions strongly through hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen-bonded nucleophile has to shed part of its solvation shell before attacking, which raises the activation energy of SN2. They stabilise carbocation and halide ions, so they favour SN1/E1 (ionisation).
  • Polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF, CH3CN, acetone, HMPA, sulfolane): high dielectric constant and strongly solvate cations (via the negative end of the dipole, e.g. S=O, C=O) but expose the anion ("naked anion"). A naked anion is far more reactive; SN2 rate constants rise by factors of 103–106 compared with methanol (e.g. CH3I + N3−).
  • Non-polar solvents (hexane, benzene) dissolve neither salts nor ionic transition states; reactions between ionic Nu and neutral R–X are slow unless a phase-transfer catalyst (R4N+X−, crown ether) carries the anion into the organic phase.
Key idea To speed up an SN2 reaction: (1) use a primary or methyl substrate, (2) a strong, unhindered nucleophile at high concentration, (3) a good leaving group (I, OTs), (4) a polar aprotic solvent, (5) moderate temperature (heat helps elimination more).
Trap Crown ethers (18-crown-6 + KF) and phase-transfer catalysis make a "naked anion" in a non-polar solvent: this accelerates SN2 sharply. A question that says "reaction becomes faster on adding 18-crown-6" is hinting at this.

5.6 Intramolecular S_{N}2: ring closure

When the nucleophile and the leaving group are in the same molecule, the effective concentration of Nu is large and cyclisation competes strongly. Ring-size preference: 3-, 5- and 6-membered rings form fast; 4-membered rings slowly (strain); 7- and larger rings slowly (entropy; the ends rarely meet).

NaOH⟶
2-Chloroethanol with base gives ethylene oxide (3-exo-tet): alkoxide displaces Cl intramolecularly (the nucleophile must attack anti to the leaving group, hence the two groups must be anti).
NaOH⟶
4-Bromobutan-1-ol gives tetrahydrofuran (5-membered ring), faster than the competing intermolecular ether formation.
free amine, dilute⟶
5-Bromopentylamine cyclises to piperidine (six-membered ring, N attacks C–Br intramolecularly).
Key idea Williamson synthesis of an epoxide: halohydrin + base. The alkoxide must attack the carbon bearing X from the back side, so the O− and X must be anti in the reacting conformation. In cyclic systems (e.g. 2-bromocyclohexanol) only the trans-diaxial arrangement works, and the cis isomer fails to give an epoxide (it gives the ketone or other products).
Example 8 Main

Effect of changing conditions on the rate

For the reaction CH3Br + OH− → CH3OH + Br− in a mixed solvent, by what factor does the rate change if (i) [CH3Br] is doubled; (ii) [OH−] is tripled; (iii) both are doubled; (iv) the volume of the solution is halved?

Show solution
  1. The reaction is SN2 with rate = k[CH3Br][OH−] (second order).
  2. (i) ×2 (ii) ×3 (iii) ×4 (iv) halving the volume doubles both concentrations: ×4.
  3. If the same question were asked for tert-butyl bromide (SN1, rate = k[RBr]) the answers would be ×2, ×1, ×2, ×2.
Example 9 Main

Nucleophile ranking

Arrange in increasing order of nucleophilicity towards CH3I in methanol: CH3OH, Cl−, I−, CH3S−.

Show solution
  1. In protic methanol, large polarisable atoms are enhanced and small anions are hydrogen-bonded.
  2. Anionic sulfur ≫ oxygen anion; and neutral methanol is lowest.
  3. Neutral methanol is weakest; Cl− is small and strongly H-bonded; I− is large and polarisable; thiolate (large, anionic, very polarisable S) is best.
  4. CH3OH < Cl− < I− < CH3S−.

Example 11 Adv

Which is faster?

Choose the faster in each pair for SN2 with NaI/acetone: (a) 1-bromobutane or 2-bromobutane (b) 1-bromobutane or 1-bromo-2-methylpropane (c) 1-chlorobutane or 1-bromobutane (d) bromomethylcyclohexane or 1-bromo-2,2-dimethylpropane (e) benzyl chloride or chlorobenzene.

Show solution
  1. (a) 1-Bromobutane: primary beats secondary.
  2. (b) 1-Bromobutane: β-branching in isobutyl bromide slows the backside attack.
  3. (c) 1-Bromobutane: Br− is a better leaving group than Cl−.
  4. (d) Bromomethylcyclohexane: a ring carbon is only a mono-branched β-carbon, while neopentyl has a quaternary β-carbon.
  5. (e) Benzyl chloride: sp3 C–Cl, conjugation stabilises the TS; chlorobenzene does not undergo SN2 at all.
Example 12 Adv

Best Williamson route

Suggest the best route to ethyl tert-butyl ether, (CH3)3C–O–CH2CH3, and explain why the alternative fails.

Show solution
  1. Williamson ether synthesis is SN2 of an alkoxide on an alkyl halide. The halide must be primary or methyl.
  2. Route A: sodium tert-butoxide + ethyl bromide → ether (good: primary halide).
  3. Route B: sodium ethoxide + tert-butyl bromide gives mainly 2-methylpropene by E2 (the tertiary halide cannot undergo SN2 and the alkoxide is a strong base).
  4. Answer: (CH3)3CO−Na+ + CH3CH2Br.

Example 13 Adv

Intramolecular reaction

4-Chlorobutan-1-ol is treated with aqueous NaOH. Predict the major product, and compare with 3-chloropropan-1-ol and 5-chloropentan-1-ol.

Show solution
  1. The base generates the alkoxide, which attacks the carbon bearing Cl intramolecularly.
  2. 4-chlorobutan-1-ol: 5-membered ring, tetrahydrofuran, very fast.
  3. 3-chloropropan-1-ol: oxetane (4-membered; strained and slow), so intermolecular products/diols compete.
  4. 5-chloropentan-1-ol: tetrahydropyran (6-membered); good but a little slower than THF formation.
Example 14 Adv

Mechanism from data

Racemic 2-bromobutane is not formed when optically active (R)-2-bromobutane is treated with 0.1 M NaBr in acetone, but its rotation slowly falls to zero. Rate of loss of rotation = 2 × rate of Br− exchange (measured with 82Br). What does this show?

Show solution
  1. Every exchange event inverts the molecule (R → S). Racemisation needs a 1:1 mixture, i.e. only half of the molecules must be inverted.
  2. Rate of racemisation = 2 × rate of exchange: exactly what pure inversion in an SN2 step predicts. If the mechanism were SN1 (planar cation, both faces), racemisation rate would equal the exchange rate.
  3. Conclusion: SN2 with 100 % inversion at each step.
Example 15 Main

Leaving group exchange

Explain why ethyl iodide reacts with NaOH about 104 times faster than ethyl fluoride, although C–F is the more polar bond.

Show solution
  1. The rate-determining transition state has the C–X bond partially broken. The weak, long, easily polarised C–I bond (≈ 234 kJ mol−1) breaks easily and I− is a very weak base (HI, pKa = −10); the C–F bond (≈ 450 kJ mol−1) is very strong and F− is a stronger base. Polarity helps the attack a little, but it is overwhelmed by bond strength and anion stability.
