2. Nomenclature recap and physical properties

IUPAC naming of an alkane follows three rules: choose the longest continuous chain as the parent; number it from the end that gives the lowest locant set to the substituents at the first point of difference; and list the substituent prefixes alphabetically, using di-, tri- for repeats. The branched alkane below is 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (iso-octane), not 2,4,4-trimethylpentane, because the locant set {2,2,4} is lower than {2,4,4}.

2,2,4-Trimethylpentane, then the three isomers of C5H12: pentane, 2-methylbutane (isopentane) and 2,2-dimethylpropane (neopentane).

Physical state. C1–C4 alkanes are gases, C5–C17 unbranched alkanes are liquids and higher members are waxy solids. Alkanes are insoluble in water (they cannot hydrogen bond) and less dense than water.

Boiling point. Only London dispersion forces act between alkane molecules, and these grow with the contact surface area. Hence the boiling point rises steadily with chain length (CH4 −162 °C, C2H6 −89 °C, C3H8 −42 °C, C4H10 −0.5 °C, C5H12 36 °C). Among isomers, branching makes the molecule more spherical, reduces the surface area and lowers the boiling point: pentane 36 °C, isopentane 28 °C, neopentane 9.5 °C.

Melting point. Melting depends on how well molecules pack in the crystal, not only on surface area. The highly symmetrical neopentane packs very well and melts at −17 °C, far above pentane (−130 °C) and isopentane (−160 °C).

Key idea. Boiling point: n-alkane > branched isomer (more branching, lower b.p.). Melting point: the most symmetrical isomer is highest. A single question can therefore reverse the two orders for the same set of isomers.
