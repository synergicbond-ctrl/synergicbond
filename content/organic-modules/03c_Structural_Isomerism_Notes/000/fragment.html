1. Chain (skeletal) isomerism

Chain isomers have the same molecular formula and the same functional group, but the carbon atoms are connected in different skeletons: a straight chain in one, a branched chain in another, or branches of different size and position. The first example appears at C4: butane and 2-methylpropane (isobutane) are both C4H10 and both alkanes. Pentane has three skeletons, hexane five, heptane nine. Because the functional group is unchanged, chemical behaviour is very similar, while physical properties such as boiling point and melting point differ measurably.

Chain isomers of C4H10: butane (left) and 2-methylpropane (right).

When a functional group is present, chain isomerism is judged with the group held in a comparable position. Butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol both carry the OH group on a terminal CH2 carbon, but the carbon skeleton is straight in the first and branched in the second, so they are chain isomers. The same applies to butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid, or to pentanal and 2-methylbutanal.

Chain isomers: butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol (OH on a terminal CH2 in both).

To see all the skeletons of a given carbon count, list the longest chain first, then shorten it by one carbon and place the removed carbon as a branch in every distinct position, then shorten again and place two branches, and so on. The three C5 skeletons are drawn below.

The three skeletons of C5H12: pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane.

Key idea. Chain isomers share the functional group and differ only in the carbon skeleton. Increased branching lowers the boiling point because the molecule is more compact and has less surface area for London dispersion forces.
Trap. Butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol differ in the skeleton and in the position of OH at once. Such a pair is simply called structural isomers; when a question insists on one type, look for the single difference that is isolated, as in butan-1-ol versus 2-methylpropan-1-ol.
