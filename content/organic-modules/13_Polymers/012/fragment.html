8. Radical addition seen through frontier orbitals

MOT Lens This section uses frontier-orbital (MO) language qualitatively. It explains orientation and relative rates; the localised-bond arrow picture of §5.1 gives the same products. Where the two pictures are used together, they must agree on the product.

The “why” of every step in radical polymerisation can be read from the orbitals of the attacking radical and the alkene.

  • The radical has one electron in a SOMO (singly occupied MO) on the carbon of the chain end. In a stabilised radical such as the benzylic PS• chain end, the SOMO is spread over the ring (lower energy, less reactive, more selective).
  • The alkene offers a filled π (HOMO) and an empty π* (LUMO). A radical can interact with either: SOMO–π* (when the alkene carries an electron-withdrawing group such as –CN or –COOR, whose π* is low in energy) or SOMO–π (when X is an electron donor such as –OR, whose π is high in energy). The stabilisation is largest when the SOMO is close in energy to the alkene orbital it overlaps.
  • Regiochemistry. In CH2=CH–X, the larger coefficient of both π and π* (for X = Ph, vinyl, CN, COOR) is on the terminal CH2 carbon. Bonding to this carbon gives the best overlap, and the SOMO that remains on CH(X) is delocalised into X. Orbital coefficient, spin-density stabilisation and sterics all say the same: attack on CH2, giving the head-to-tail polymer.
  • Why propagation is so fast and exothermic: the C=C π bond (weak, because p–p overlap is sideways) is traded for a C–C σ bond (strong, head-on overlap). The SOMO–π interaction is the start of that exchange; the new σ bond has all its stabilisation in the product.
  • Polar effect. An electron-rich (nucleophilic) radical, e.g. the one from an alkyl or an ether-substituted chain end, adds fast to electron-poor alkenes (acrylonitrile, maleic anhydride), and an electron-poor radical adds fast to an electron-rich alkene (vinyl ether). This is the origin of alternating copolymerisation of electron-rich + electron-poor pairs: styrene (donor) + maleic anhydride (acceptor).
IMP Radical polymerisation is SOMO-controlled. The monomers that polymerise best (styrene, acrylates, acrylonitrile, butadiene, vinyl chloride) have a substituent that lowers the radical’s energy by delocalisation and a low-lying π*/accessible π. Ethene lacks any such group, so it needs 1000–2000 atm to force the slow addition of an unstabilised primary radical.
