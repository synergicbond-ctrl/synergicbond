2. Lewis structures, formal charge and the language of structures

2.1 Formal charge

Formal charge is a bookkeeping device: it assumes that every bond is shared exactly equally.

Formula Formal charge (FC) = V − N − B/2 , where V = valence electrons of the free atom, N = non-bonding (lone-pair) electrons on the atom, B = bonding electrons around the atom.
The sum of all formal charges equals the net charge of the species. The formal charge tells you where the charge is assigned, not how the electron density is really distributed: in a carboxylate ion the formal charge sits on one oxygen in each contributor, but the real charge is shared equally.

Exam Trap The octet rule is a guide to which contributors are important, not a law. Boron and the cations of carbon are stable enough to be intermediates with six electrons, and the elements of period 3 (S, P) in sulfones, sulfonic acids and phosphates can be written with more than eight electrons in JEE-style structures. For second-period elements (C, N, O, F) an octet is never exceeded: a structure with five bonds to carbon or nitrogen is wrong, always.

Illustration 2.1 Main

Formal charges in the nitro group

Assign formal charges to nitrogen and each oxygen in CH₃–NO₂ and explain why the structure has no octet violation although N appears to form five bonds.

  1. Nitrogen: V = 5, N = 0 lone-pair electrons, B = 8 (one single bond to C, one single bond to O⁻, one double bond to O counted as 2 bonds, total four bonds = 8 electrons). FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1.
  2. Double-bonded oxygen: V = 6, N = 4, B = 4. FC = 6 − 4 − 2 = 0. Single-bonded oxygen: V = 6, N = 6, B = 2. FC = 6 − 6 − 1 = −1.
  3. Nitrogen has only four bonds (C–N, N–O, N=O counts as two): 4 bonds = 8 electrons, a full octet. Writing N=O as a double bond to both oxygens (five bonds to N) would put ten electrons on a second-period atom and is not allowed. The charge-separated structure is the correct Lewis picture and the two N–O bonds are equal in the real molecule (resonance).
Illustration 2.2 Main

Formal charges in the azide ion

The azide ion N₃⁻ has 16 valence electrons. Write two valid Lewis structures, assign formal charges, and state which atom is positive in every contributor.

  1. Structure I, symmetric: [:N̤=N=N:] with each terminal nitrogen carrying two lone pairs and a double bond to the central nitrogen. Terminal N: FC = 5 − 4 − 2 = −1. Central N (two double bonds, no lone pair): FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. Sum = −1 + 1 − 1 = −1 ✓.
  2. Structure II: one terminal N is joined to the centre by a triple bond and carries one lone pair, the other terminal N is joined by a single bond and carries three lone pairs. Triple-bonded N: FC = 5 − 2 − 3 = 0. Central N: FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. Single-bonded N: FC = 5 − 6 − 1 = −2. Sum = 0 + 1 − 2 = −1 ✓.
  3. The central nitrogen is +1 in every contributor (it has four bonds and no lone pair). Structure I, with the negative charge spread over two atoms and no −2 charge, is the major contributor, so the two N–N bonds are equal (about 116 pm) and intermediate between N=N and N≡N.

2.2 Writing structures: condensed, bond-line and what hides in them

In a bond-line formula every vertex and every end of a line is a carbon, hydrogens on carbon are not drawn, and every carbon is assumed to have enough hydrogens to make four bonds. Heteroatoms and their hydrogens are always drawn. Practise converting freely between the three forms; the questions of the following sections assume you can count hydrogens and identify α, β positions instantly.

2.3 Degree of unsaturation (DBE) and its uses

Formula DBE = C + 1 + (N − H − X)/2 (O and S do not change the count; N counts +½ each, H and halogens −½ each).
Each ring and each π-bond counts 1, each triple bond counts 2. For benzene (C₆H₆): 6 + 1 − 3 = 4 (one ring + three π bonds). DBE is the quickest check that a formula you have drawn is consistent with its molecular formula, and it limits the number of rings/π-bonds an unknown can contain.
Illustration 2.3 Main

DBE of nicotine and caffeine

Find the degree of unsaturation of nicotine (C₁₀H₁₄N₂) and caffeine (C₈H₁₀N₄O₂).

  1. Nicotine: DBE = 10 + 1 + (2 − 14)/2 = 11 − 6 = 5 (pyridine ring: 1 ring + 3 C=C/C=N = 4; pyrrolidine ring: 1; total 5 ✓).
  2. Caffeine: DBE = 8 + 1 + (4 − 10)/2 = 9 − 3 = 6 (two rings, two C=O, two C=C/C=N in the ring system = 6 ✓).

2.4 Types of carbon and hydrogen

A carbon is primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°) or quaternary (4°) according to the number of other carbons it is bonded to (1, 2, 3, 4). Hydrogens are named for the carbon that carries them. Special names: allylic (on a carbon next to C=C), vinylic (on a C=C carbon), benzylic (on a carbon next to a benzene ring), α-hydrogen (on a carbon next to a carbonyl group or other functional group; in carbocation chemistry, on the carbon next to the cationic centre). Counting types of hydrogen is the first step of every hyperconjugation and radical-stability question.

Illustration 2.4 Main

Counting types of carbon

In 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (isooctane) find the number of primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary carbons and the number of primary hydrogens.

  1. Skeleton: (CH₃)₃C–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₃. Carbons: the three CH₃ on C-2 and the two CH₃ on C-4 are all bonded to one carbon → 5 primary carbons. CH₂ is bonded to two carbons → 1 secondary. The CH is bonded to three carbons → 1 tertiary. C-2 is bonded to four carbons → 1 quaternary.
  2. Primary hydrogens are those on primary carbons: 5 × 3 = 15.
