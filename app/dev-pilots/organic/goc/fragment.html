6. The inductive effect (I effect)

6.1 Definition and origin

Whenever a σ-bond joins two atoms of different electronegativity, the bonding pair is displaced toward the more electronegative atom. This displacement does not stop at that bond: it polarises the next σ-bond, which polarises the next, and so on. This permanent polarisation transmitted through a chain of σ-bonds is the inductive effect.

  • −I (electron-withdrawing) group: pulls σ-electrons toward itself — it is more electronegative than hydrogen (the reference), e.g. –NO₂, –CN, –F, –Cl, –COOH, –OH.
  • +I (electron-releasing) group: pushes σ-electrons away from itself — it is less electronegative than hydrogen (as judged in the conventional series), e.g. –CH₃, –C₂H₅, –C(CH₃)₃, –O⁻, –COO⁻.

The effect is written with arrows on the bonds: C → C → C → Cl (the arrow head toward the electronegative end), or with a small δ⁺ that diminishes along the chain.

IMP Four properties of the I effect. (1) It is permanent — present in the ground state. (2) It operates through σ-bonds only, and each σ-bond passes on roughly half or less of the effect: it is felt strongly at the first carbon, about one half or less at the second, and is negligible beyond the third (or fourth) carbon. (3) It never reverses the sign of the polarity: the atom nearest the group is the most affected. (4) It does not involve displacement of electrons into π-orbitals or lone pairs; that is resonance.
The decay with distance is clearly seen in the pKₐ of chlorobutanoic acids (Cl placed progressively farther from the COOH):

Exam Trap Three common slips. (a) Halogens: the −I order follows electronegativity, F > Cl > Br > I, so fluoroacetic acid is the strongest of the four halogen-substituted acetic acids (pKₐ 2.6 vs 2.9, 2.9 and 3.2). Illustration 6.1 Main

Strength of chloroacetic acids

Arrange in decreasing acid strength: CH₃COOH, ClCH₂COOH, Cl₂CHCOOH, Cl₃CCOOH, CH₃CH₂COOH.

  1. Each −I chlorine stabilises the carboxylate by withdrawing electron density, and the effect adds up: Cl₃C > Cl₂CH > ClCH₂.
  2. The ethyl group is a stronger +I donor than methyl and destabilises the carboxylate more, so propanoic acid is weaker than acetic acid.
  3. Cl₃CCOOH > Cl₂CHCOOH > ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH > CH₃CH₂COOH (pKₐ 0.7, 1.3, 2.9, 4.76, 4.87).
Illustration 6.2 Main

Position of the substituent

Arrange in increasing pKₐ: 2-chlorobutanoic acid, 3-chlorobutanoic acid, 4-chlorobutanoic acid, butanoic acid.

  1. The nearer the −I group is to the COOH, the stronger the acid. Increasing pKₐ (weaker acid): 2-chloro (2.84) < 3-chloro (4.05) < 4-chloro (4.52) < butanoic acid (4.82).
Illustration 6.3 Adv

Acids with different groups on the α-carbon

Arrange in decreasing acidity: NCCH₂COOH, O₂NCH₂COOH, FCH₂COOH, HOCH₂COOH, C₆H₅CH₂COOH, CH₃COOH.

  1. Using the inductive power of the group on the α-carbon: NO₂ (strongest) > CN > F > OH > C₆H₅ > H.

Illustration 6.4 Adv

Why formic acid is stronger than acetic acid

Formic acid pKₐ 3.75, acetic acid 4.76. Explain, and say why hydrogen bonding is not the explanation.

  1. In acetate the +I group CH₃ pushes electrons toward the carboxylate carbon and oxygens, increasing the electron density on the anion and destabilising it relative to formate, in which there is only H (reference). A less stable conjugate base means a weaker acid.
  2. Hydrogen bonding is not responsible: it is a physical (intermolecular) interaction and both acids dimerise in the same way. The difference is electronic (inductive).

6.3 Where the inductive effect matters

  • Acid strength: −I groups stabilise the conjugate base. In carboxylic acids, X–CH₂COOH is stronger than CH₃COOH if X is −I and weaker if X is +I.
  • Base strength: +I groups on N raise the electron density on nitrogen and increase basicity (ammonia < methylamine in the gas phase); −I groups lower it (2,2,2-trifluoroethylamine pKₐ(H) 5.7 against ethylamine 10.7).
  • Carbocations are stabilised by +I groups (tertiary > secondary > primary); carbanions by −I groups.
  • Reactivity of carbonyl compounds toward nucleophiles: the carbon of C=O is more δ⁺ when attached to −I groups: Cl₃CCHO (chloral, forms a stable hydrate) > HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃COCH₃.
  • Acid derivatives: RCOCl > (RCO)₂O > RCOOR′ > RCONH₂ in reactivity toward nucleophiles (the leaving group and the electron demand at C=O).
  • Polarity and dipole direction (propene, toluene, alkyl halides).

Arrange in increasing basicity (aqueous): CF₃CH₂NH₂, CH₃CH₂NH₂, HOCH₂CH₂NH₂, NH₃. Give reasons.

6.4 Field effect: the through-space relative

The field effect is the electrostatic influence of a polar substituent on a reaction centre transmitted through space (or through the solvent) rather than through bonds. It is hard to separate from the inductive effect in flexible molecules, because both fall off with distance and both vary with the polarity of the group. It becomes visible in rigid molecules in which the through-bond path is long but the through-space distance is short.

oxalic malonic succinic glutaric adipic
pKₐ₁ = 1.25, 2.85, 4.21, 4.34, 4.41

The pKₐ₁ of the α,ω-dicarboxylic acids rise toward 4.8 (the value for an alkanoic acid) as the second COOH moves away: 1.25 (oxalic), 2.85 (malonic), 4.21 (succinic), 4.34 (glutaric), 4.41 (adipic). The second group both withdraws electrons through the bonds and exerts a field effect on the departing proton. The pKₐ₂ values fall in the same series for the opposite reason: 4.27, 5.70, 5.64, 5.41, 5.41 — the first-formed carboxylate makes the removal of the second proton harder, and the closer it is, the harder.

Illustration 6.6 Adv

Statement test

State whether true or false: (a) The inductive effect operates through π-bonds. (b) The inductive effect is temporary. (c) +I effect of alkyl groups is greater for –CH₃ than for –C(CH₃)₃. (d) −I effect of –C≡CH is greater than that of –CH=CH₂.

  1. (a) False — it operates through σ-bonds (the π-bond version is the mesomeric or electromeric effect).
  2. (b) False — the inductive effect is permanent; the electromeric effect is temporary.
  3. (c) False — the +I order is t-Bu > i-Pr > Et > Me; the t-butyl group is the stronger donor.
  4. (d) True — the carbon of the ethynyl group is sp (50 % s), vinyl is sp² (33 % s), so ethynyl is more electron-withdrawing.
