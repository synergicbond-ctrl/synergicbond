2.2 Acid–base behaviour of amino acids

The zwitterion

In the solid and in water the amino acid is not H2N–CHR–COOH but the zwitterion (dipolar ion) +H3N–CHR–COO−: the acidic –COOH (pKa ≈ 2) has given its proton to the basic –NH2 (conjugate acid pKa ≈ 9–10). Consequences: high melting points (dec. 200–300 °C); solubility in water and poor solubility in organic solvents; low volatility; large dipole moments; amphoteric behaviour.

− H+⟶ − H+⟶
Glycine: cation (pH < 2) → zwitterion (pH ≈ 6) → anion (pH > 10). pK₁ = 2.34, pK₂ = 9.60
Key idea A group is mostly protonated when pH < pKa and mostly deprotonated when pH > pKa. At pH = pKa the protonated and deprotonated forms are equal (Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log [A−]/[HA]).

The isoelectric point (pI)

The isoelectric point is the pH at which the molecule has zero net charge (the zwitterion is the major species, and the solubility in water is lowest). It is the average of the two pKa values that bracket the neutral species:

Formula Neutral side chain: pI = ½(pK1 + pK2). Acidic side chain (Asp, Glu, Cys, Tyr): pI = ½(pK1 + pKR) or the two lowest pK values. Basic side chain (Lys, Arg, His): pI = ½(pKR + pK2) or the two highest pK values.
Shortcut Algorithm for any amino acid or peptide: (1) write all ionisable groups with their pK values in increasing order; (2) start from the fully protonated form and count its net charge (every NH3+ is +1; every COOH is 0); (3) add the pK values one by one: each removal lowers the charge by 1; (4) the zero-charge species lies between the two pK values where the charge goes from +1 to 0 and from 0 to −1; pI = their mean.
Trap A common error is to average pK1 and pK2 for aspartic acid (giving 5.74). Between pK1 = 1.88 and pK2 (the side chain COOH, 3.65), the net charge changes from +1 to 0, so pI = 2.77. For lysine, the charge changes from +1 to 0 to −1 between the amino groups, so pI = ½(8.95 + 10.53) = 9.74, not ½(2.18 + 8.95).
Titration of glycine hydrochloride with NaOH: two buffer regions (pK ± 1) and a sharp rise at pI (one equivalent added)
Aspartic acid: three pK values; the zero-charge point (pI) lies after only one equivalent of base has been added
Lysine: the pI lies after two equivalents have been added, in the high-pH region

Distribution of species and pH calculations

Formula Fraction of cation, zwitterion and anion for glycine at a given pH: f+ = 1/(1 + 10pH−pK1 + 102pH−pK1−pK2), etc. At the pI the zwitterion fraction is maximal; other species ≈ 10−(pI−pK1) each.
Formula Net charge Z at a given pH: Z = Σ over groups of (charge × fraction in that state). Each basic group of conjugate-acid pKa contributes +1/(1 + 10pH−pK); each acidic group of pK contributes −1/(1 + 10pK−pH).
Example 30 Main

Isoelectric points

Find the pI of (a) glycine (2.34, 9.60), (b) aspartic acid (1.88, 3.65, 9.60), (c) lysine (2.18, 8.95, 10.53), (d) histidine (1.82, 6.00, 9.17), (e) cysteine (1.96, 8.18, 10.28), (f) tyrosine (2.20, 9.11, 10.07).

Show solution
  1. (a) neutral side chain: ½(2.34 + 9.60) = 5.97.
  2. (b) acidic side chain: sorted pK 1.88, 3.65, 9.60; the neutral species is between the first two: 2.76.
  3. (c) basic side chain: sorted 2.18, 8.95, 10.53; the neutral species is between the last two: 9.74.
  4. (d) His: sorted 1.82, 6.00, 9.17; charge +1 up to 6.00 (imidazolium protonated), 0 between 6.00 and 9.17: 7.58.
  5. (e) Cys: the thiol (8.18) ionises before the ammonium (10.28): ½(1.96 + 8.18) = 5.07.
  6. (f) Tyr: ammonium 9.11 ionises before phenol 10.07: ½(2.20 + 9.11) = 5.66.
Example 31 Main

Direction of migration

At pH 6.0 in an electric field, which way do glycine (pI 5.97), aspartic acid (2.76), lysine (9.74) and histidine (7.58) migrate?

Show solution
  1. A species with pH > pI is negatively charged (moves to the anode); pH < pI is positively charged (moves to the cathode).
  2. Glycine: pH 6.0 ≈ pI: essentially stationary (very slightly toward the anode).
  3. Aspartic acid: pH 6 > 2.76 → anion → anode.
  4. Lysine: pH 6 < 9.74 → cation → cathode.
  5. Histidine: pH 6 < 7.58 → cation (weak) → cathode.
Example 32 Adv

Species distribution

For glycine at pH 2.34, calculate the percentage of cation, zwitterion and anion (pK1 = 2.34, pK2 = 9.60).

Show solution
  1. At pH = pK1: [cation]/[zwitterion] = 1. The anion is negligible: [anion]/[zwitterion] = 102.34−9.60 = 5.5 × 10−8.
  2. So ≈ 50 % cation, ≈ 50 % zwitterion, ≈ 0 % anion; net charge ≈ +0.5.
Example 33 Adv

Net charge at pH 7

Give the approximate net charge at pH 7.0 of Asp, Glu, Lys, Arg and His. (pKR: 3.65, 4.25, 10.53, 12.48, 6.00.)

Show solution
  1. Asp: COOH (pK 1.88) −, side chain COO− (3.65), NH3+: net −1. Glu: −1.
  2. Lys: ε-NH3+ (10.53) and α-NH3+ (8.95) with one COO−: +1. Arg: +1 (guanidinium is protonated up to pH 12.5).
  3. His: pH 7 > 6.00, so the imidazole is mostly neutral: charge 0 (with ≈ 10 % protonated, +0.1).
Example 34 Adv

Equivalents of base

Aspartic acid hydrochloride (1 mol; the cation +H3N–CH(CO2H)CH2CO2H with Cl−, net charge +1) is titrated with NaOH. How many moles of NaOH are needed (a) to reach the pI, (b) to reach the fully deprotonated dianion?

Show solution
  1. (a) The hydrochloride is simply the cation: the HCl has protonated the amine. One mole of OH− removes the α-COOH proton (pK 1.88), giving +H3N–CH(CO2−)CH2CO2H, net charge 0: 1 mol.
  2. (b) The side-chain COOH (3.65) takes the second and the ammonium (9.60) the third: 3 mol (the species passes through net charges +1 → 0 → −1 → −2).
Example 35 Adv

pKb format

An amino acid CH3CH(NH2)COOH is described as having pKa = 2.2 (for the –COOH) and pKb = 4.4 (for the –NH2). Find its pI.

Show solution
  1. The pKa of the conjugate acid +NH3– is 14 − pKb = 14 − 4.4 = 9.6.
  2. pI = ½(2.2 + 9.6) = 5.9.
Example 36 Adv

Order of elution

Gly, Asp and Lys are loaded on a cation-exchange column (sulfonated polystyrene, H+ form) at pH 3. Which elutes first when the pH is raised slowly?

Show solution
  1. At pH 3 Lys (+2) binds most strongly, Gly (+1) binds, Asp (≈ 0 to +0.2; pI 2.76) binds weakest.
  2. As pH is raised, each is released as its net charge falls to zero: Asp first, then Gly, then Lys last.
Example 37 Main

Buffer

In which pH range is glycine an effective buffer? What is the buffer capacity maximum?

Show solution
  1. A buffer works within ±1 of a pK. Glycine has two: pH 1.3–3.3 (cation/zwitterion) and pH 8.6–10.6 (zwitterion/anion). Capacity is greatest at pH = pK1 and pK2; near the pI (≈ 6) glycine is a very poor buffer.
