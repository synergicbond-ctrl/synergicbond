13. Acidity: the complete treatment

13.1 Definitions and the pKₐ scale

  • Brønsted–Lowry acid: a proton donor. Base: a proton acceptor. HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺. Every acid has a conjugate base; the stronger the acid, the weaker (and more stable) its conjugate base.
  • Lewis acid: an electron-pair acceptor (BF₃, AlCl₃, H⁺, carbocations, SO₃); Lewis base: an electron-pair donor (NH₃, H₂O, OH⁻, R⁻, alkenes).

For HA + H₂O ⇌ A⁻ + H₃O⁺: K_a = [A⁻][H₃O⁺]/[HA] and pK_a = −log K_a. A lower pKₐ means a stronger acid; a change of one pKₐ unit is a factor of 10 in K_a. The pKₐ is the pH at which the acid is exactly half ionised (Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKₐ + log [A⁻]/[HA]).

Formula Equilibrium constant for HA + B⁻ ⇌ A⁻ + HB : K = K_a(HA)/K_a(HB) = 10^(pKₐ(HB) − pKₐ(HA)). The reaction goes forward (product favoured) when HA is the stronger acid, i.e. when the acid on the left has the lower pKₐ.

Exam Trap Water cannot hold a base stronger than OH⁻ (the levelling effect): NaNH₂, NaH, RLi, RMgX react completely with water to give OH⁻ and so cannot be used in aqueous solution. For the same reason, acids stronger than H₃O⁺ (HCl, HClO₄, HNO₃) all appear equally strong in water; their order is established in non-aqueous solvents or from gas-phase data.

13.2 The four questions that rank any set of acids

Acidity is decided by the stability of the conjugate base A⁻ (and to a lesser extent by the stability of HA). Ask, in this order:

  • A — Atom: On which atom is the negative charge? Compare the atoms bearing the charge: electronegativity (across a period) and size (down a group). *This is decisive when the atoms differ.*
  • R — Resonance: Is the charge delocalised by resonance (carboxylate, phenoxide, enolate, nitronate, aromatic anions)? More equivalent, better-stabilised contributors = more stable A⁻.
  • I — Induction: Do −I groups (halogens, NO₂, CN, C=O, N⁺R₃, sp-carbons) withdraw electrons and stabilise A⁻? Does +I (alkyl) destabilise it? The effect falls off with distance.
  • O — Orbital (hybridisation): In which orbital does the lone pair sit? More s-character = more stable A⁻ (sp > sp² > sp³).

Then check the secondary effects: solvation of the anion (small, localised anions are solvated best in protic solvents), intramolecular H-bonding, steric hindrance to solvation, steric inhibition of resonance, ring strain, aromaticity.

IMP When two factors conflict, resonance beats induction, and a change of atom beats everything. CH₃COOH (pKₐ 4.8; O⁻ with resonance) is far more acidic than CF₃CH₂OH (12.4; O⁻ with three F but no resonance). H₂S (7.0) is more acidic than H₂O (15.7) although O is more electronegative: size dominates down a group. Across a period electronegativity dominates: acidity increases CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O < HF.

13.5 Resonance: why RCOOH ≫ ROH and PhOH ≫ cyclohexanol

ethanol (pKₐ 15.9) acetic acid (4.76) phenol (9.99) cyclohexanol (16–18)
Resonance stabilisation of the conjugate base decides: carboxylate and phenoxide are resonance-stabilised, ethoxide and cyclohexoxide are not

In ethoxide the charge is localised on one oxygen. In acetate it is shared equally by two oxygens (two equivalent contributors). In phenoxide it is shared between oxygen and the ortho and para carbons (five contributors), but the contributors with the charge on carbon are less important, so phenoxide is stabilised less than carboxylate: RCOOH (pKₐ 4–5) > PhOH (10) ≫ ROH (16–18). Phenol is acidic enough to dissolve in aqueous NaOH, not in NaHCO₃; carboxylic acids dissolve in NaHCO₃ (evolving CO₂).

IMP Test for the acid strength of RCOOH, phenol, alcohol and water, used in analysis: the order RCOOH > H₂CO₃ > PhOH > H₂O > ROH > HC≡CH > NH₃ > alkanes. Hence RCOOH reacts with NaHCO₃ (gives CO₂); phenol does not; phenol reacts with NaOH, not with NaHCO₃; ROH reacts with Na, not with NaOH; alkynes react with NaNH₂, not NaOH.

Illustration 13.8 Adv

Maleic and fumaric acids

Explain why maleic acid has a lower pKₐ₁ but a higher pKₐ₂ than fumaric acid.

  1. First ionisation: the maleate monoanion is stabilised by an intramolecular H-bond between the remaining COOH and the COO⁻ (they are held close by the cis double bond). Fumarate cannot form this bond. So H₂Mal → HMal⁻ is easier than H₂Fum → HFum⁻.

13.8 Substituted benzoic acids

The benzoate anion is stabilised by −I and −M substituents and destabilised by +I and +M donors. Exam Trap The substituent effect on benzoic acids is much smaller than on phenols. In the benzoate anion the negative charge sits on the two oxygens, separated from the ring by the carboxyl carbon; it cannot be pushed directly into the ring. In the phenoxide ion the oxygen is attached to the ring and its charge is delocalised into the ring (o, p), so a para −M group such as NO₂ drops the pKₐ of phenol by 2.84 units (9.99 → 7.15) but that of benzoic acid by only 0.76 (4.20 → 3.44). In the language of Hammett: ρ(benzoic acids) = 1.0 by definition, ρ(phenols) ≈ 2.2.

  • In water the order is CH₃OH > primary > secondary > tertiary alcohols (15.5, 15.9, 17, 18) — the alkyl groups are +I (destabilising the alkoxide) and bulky groups hinder the solvation of the alkoxide oxygen by water molecules.
  • In the gas phase the order is reversed: t-BuOH > i-PrOH > EtOH > MeOH > H₂O, because large alkyl groups stabilise an anion by polarisation (charge spread over a larger polarisable volume) when there is no solvent. The 'aqueous' order is therefore a solvation effect.
  • Fluorine and chlorine on the carbon adjacent to the carbinol carbon increase the acidity: CF₃CH₂OH (12.4), (CF₃)₂CHOH (9.3), (CF₃)₃COH (5.4, stronger than carbonic acid).
  • Polyols: ethylene glycol (14.2) and glycerol (14.1) are slightly stronger than ethanol (−I of neighbouring OH).
  • Thiols are stronger acids than alcohols by about five pKₐ units (down the group).
Illustration 13.16 Main

Water or tert-butyl alcohol?

Is tert-butyl alcohol a stronger acid than water in water? In the gas phase? Explain.

  1. In aqueous solution tert-butyl alcohol (pKₐ ≈ 18) is weaker than water (15.7): the three methyl groups push electrons toward O (+I) and the large tert-butoxide ion is poorly solvated by water.
  2. In the gas phase the order is reversed: tert-butyl alcohol is the stronger acid, because the alkyl groups polarise to stabilise the negative charge when no solvent molecules are available, so (CH₃)₃CO⁻ is more stable than HO⁻.

Strength of oxyacids

Arrange in increasing acid strength: HClO, HClO₂, HClO₃, HClO₄. Give two reasons.

  1. HClO < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄.
  2. (i) More oxo oxygens give greater −I, which pulls electron density from O–H, loosening the proton. (ii) The conjugate base (ClO₄⁻ has four equivalent contributors; ClO⁻ only one) is more stabilised by resonance, and the charge is spread over more O atoms.

Cyclopentadiene versus cyclohexa-1,3-diene

Cyclopentadiene has pKₐ 16, whereas the CH₂ hydrogens of 1,3-cyclohexadiene are far less acidic (comparable with an allylic C–H of an alkene, pKₐ well above 30). Explain.

  1. Removing H⁺ from cyclopentadiene gives the planar 6π cyclopentadienyl anion, aromatic.
  2. Removing H⁺ from cyclohexa-1,3-diene gives a pentadienyl anion (6 π-electrons over 5 carbons in an open-chain-like conjugated system) with no aromatic stabilisation, just ordinary allyl-type delocalisation, so it is a much weaker acid.

Acidity of nitroalkanes

Explain why the acidity increases in the order CH₃NO₂ < CH₃CH₂NO₂ < (CH₃)₂CHNO₂ although alkyl groups are +I.

  1. The nitronate ion (R₂C=N⁺(O⁻)O⁻) contains a C=N double bond. Alkyl substituents on the carbon of a C=N bond stabilise it by hyperconjugation (as in alkenes) — the more substituted the double bond, the more stable the ion.
  2. This stabilisation of the delocalised anion outweighs their +I destabilisation of the localised charge. pKₐ: 10.2 > 8.6 > 7.7.
