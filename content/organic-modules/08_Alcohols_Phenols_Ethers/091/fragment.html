24.2 Ring opening: the two regimes

All epoxide ring-opening reactions are nucleophilic substitutions in which one C–O bond is broken and the oxygen remains attached to the other carbon as OH (or O− before work-up). The nucleophile attacks from the back, so the carbon attacked is inverted; the other carbon keeps its configuration. The regiochemistry depends on the conditions.

ConditionNucleophileWhere it attacksMechanism characterStereochemistry
Basic / neutral (RO−, HO−, RS−, CN−, N3−, NH3, RNH2, RMgX, RLi, LiAlH4, acetylides)strong, anionic or neutralthe less substituted (less hindered) carbonpure SN2; steric controlinversion at the carbon attacked; anti product
Acidic (H3O+, HX, ROH/H+, Lewis acids)weak (H2O, ROH, X−)the more substituted carbon (3° or benzylic), as long as it can carry positive chargethe protonated epoxide has a long, weak C–O bond at the more substituted carbon: “loose” SN2 (SN1-like) with inversioninversion at the carbon attacked; anti product

Why? Under basic conditions there is no assistance to the leaving group, the transition state is a normal SN2 and sterics decide. Under acidic conditions the oxygen is protonated first; the positive charge in the transition state is shared by the carbons in proportion to their ability to stabilise it, so the C–O bond at the more substituted carbon is longer and more cationic, and the nucleophile attacks there. When both carbons are primary or secondary, acid-catalysed opening is less selective; for a tertiary or benzylic carbon it is essentially complete at that carbon.

Key idea Remember the one-line rule: base — less hindered carbon (SN2); acid — more substituted carbon (SN1-like); in both cases backside attack, so the product is anti (trans). The nucleophile ends up on one carbon and the OH on the other.
