9. Ambident nucleophiles and the Kornblum idea

An ambident nucleophile has two nucleophilic atoms. Cyanide can react through C or N, nitrite through N or O, and an enolate through C or O. Which atom bonds depends on the character of the reaction. A qualitative rule (Kornblum's rule, taken here at a basic level) states that the more nucleophilic, more polarisable (softer) atom attacks in an SN2-like process, whereas the more electronegative atom, which carries more of the negative charge, attacks when the reaction has more SN1 character (a carbocation-like carbon, or an electrophile activated by Ag+).

Reactant:

Reagent: KCN, ethanol (ionic cyanide, SN2 through carbon)

Product:

A nitrile (R–CN) is formed. With covalent AgCN the lone pair on nitrogen attacks and an isonitrile (R–N+≡C–) is the main product.

Reactant:

Reagent: AgNO2 (covalent salt; nitrogen attacks)

Product:

Nitroethane. Textbooks usually contrast this with ionic nitrite, which is said to give more of the alkyl nitrite R–O–N=O (oxygen attacks); the product ratio depends strongly on solvent and substrate, so only the AgNO2 to nitroalkane result is used in the questions.

Enolates are alkylated mainly at carbon with soft electrophiles such as alkyl iodides and bromides (the C-alkylated ketone or ester retains a strong C=O bond). O-Alkylation, giving enol ethers, increases with hard electrophiles (sulfates, sulfonates, silyl chlorides), polar aprotic solvents and free (unassociated) ions. Phenoxide is alkylated on oxygen by benzyl bromide in DMF, giving an aryl ether (the Williamson ether synthesis).

Trap. Do not memorise ‘N = isonitrile’ without the cause. The attacking atom changes with the metal (covalent or ionic salt), the solvent and the electrophile.
