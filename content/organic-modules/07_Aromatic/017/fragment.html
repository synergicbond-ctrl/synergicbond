15. Heteroaromatic compounds: pyrrole, furan, thiophene, pyridine

15.1 The five-membered rings: pyrrole, furan, thiophene

pyrrole furan thiophene
Each ring has 6 π electrons: 4 from two C=C, 2 from the heteroatom lone pair. The second lone pair of O and S lies in the ring plane.

Aromaticity order: benzene > thiophene > pyrrole > furan. Resonance energy (kJ mol-1, approximate): benzene 150, thiophene 120, pyrrole 90, furan 65. The more electronegative the heteroatom, the more tightly it holds its lone pair and the less readily the lone pair is donated into the ring: O (3.5) > N (3.0) > S (2.5). Sulphur is the least electronegative, but its 3p orbital overlaps poorly with carbon 2p; still, the result puts thiophene closest to benzene. Reactivity toward electrophiles: pyrrole ≫ furan > thiophene ≫ benzene (relative rates in trifluoroacetylation: pyrrole ≈ 5 × 107, furan ≈ 1.4 × 102, thiophene = 1). The heteroatom lone pair is available (the ring is a π-excessive heterocycle: 6 electrons spread over 5 atoms), so these rings are more reactive than benzene, and pyrrole behaves like an enamine / aniline. Furan and thiophene are comparable with phenol/anisole in some reactions.

Why α (C2) substitution?

pyrrole, attack at C2: charge at C3 charge at C5 (allyl) iminium form (N donates)
Attack at C2 (α): three contributors, one of them with the N lone pair stabilising the cation (iminium).
pyrrole, attack at C3: charge at C2 iminium form
Attack at C3 (β): only two contributors.
Key idea For pyrrole, furan and thiophene the electrophile attacks C2 (α) because the arenium ion has three contributors, against two for C3 (β). If both α positions are filled, substitution goes to C3. Pyrrole's N lone pair stabilises the cation best (iminium), so pyrrole is the most reactive.

Trap Never use AlCl3/HNO3–H2SO4/conc. strong acids on pyrrole and furan: they resinify (polymerise or ring-open). The mild reagent list (acetyl nitrate, pyridine·SO3, BF3, Ac2O heated) is the 'fingerprint' of pyrrole and furan chemistry.
Special features
  • Pyrrole: N–H is weakly acidic (pKa ≈ 17.5): forms pyrrolyl potassium with KOH or K metal, pyrrolyl magnesium halide with RMgX; the potassium salt gives N-alkylation, the Mg salt tends to C2-alkylation. As a base, pyrrole is very weak (protonation at C2, pKaH ≈ −3.8): the N lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet. Dipole moment: 1.8 D with the N end positive (pyrrolidine has the opposite polarity). Pine-splint test: a pine splinter moistened with HCl and exposed to pyrrole vapours turns red.
  • Reimer–Tiemann with pyrrole (Ciamician–Dennstedt): dichlorocarbene inserts into the ring to give 3-chloropyridine.
  • Furan has the weakest aromatic character and reacts as a diene in Diels–Alder reactions (with maleic anhydride, giving the oxabicyclic adduct); aqueous acid opens it to 1,4-dicarbonyl compounds (reverse of Paal–Knorr). Furfural (2-furaldehyde) is made from pentosan-rich agricultural waste (corn cobs, oat hulls) by heating with dilute H2SO4; it gives Cannizzaro and benzoin-type reactions (no α-H).
  • Thiophene contaminates coal-tar benzene; it is removed by shaking with conc. H2SO4 (thiophene sulphonates, benzene does not). Indophenin test: thiophene + isatin + conc. H2SO4 → blue. Raney Ni removes sulphur (desulphurisation) giving n-butane from thiophene.

15.2 Syntheses of the five-membered rings

Paal–Knorr synthesis. A 1,4-diketone cyclises to the heterocycle: with NH3 (or RNH2) → pyrrole; with P2O5 (or H+, dehydrating) → furan; with P4S10 or Lawesson's reagent → thiophene. Hexane-2,5-dione gives 2,5-dimethylpyrrole, -furan, -thiophene respectively.

Interconversion (Yurév): furan + NH3 (or H2S) over Al2O3 at ≈ 670 K → pyrrole (or thiophene). Industrially: furan from furfural by Pd-catalysed decarbonylation; thiophene by passing butane/butadiene and S/H2S over a catalyst; pyrrole from coal tar or from furan + NH3.

Example 33 Adv

Compare and rank

Arrange pyrrole, furan, thiophene, benzene in (i) decreasing rate of EAS (ii) decreasing aromatic character (iii) ability to be protonated: what is the best statement?

Show solution
  1. (i) pyrrole > furan > thiophene > benzene (the electron-releasing ability of the heteroatom lone pair: N > O > S, and the resulting stabilisation of the arenium ion).
  2. (ii) benzene > thiophene > pyrrole > furan (resonance energies 150, 120, 90, 65 kJ mol-1; the lower the aromaticity the more diene-like, which is why furan undergoes Diels–Alder).
  3. (iii) All four are extremely weak bases because the heteroatom lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet (benzene has no lone pair at all). Only pyrrole's protonation has been well characterised: on carbon (C2), with pKaH ≈ −3.8, so strong acid destroys the ring by polymerisation rather than simply protonating it. None of these compares with pyridine (pKaH 5.2), whose lone pair is in the plane.
Example 34 Adv

Product prediction

Predict the products: (a) pyrrole + KOH; then CH3I (b) 2-methylfuran + CH3COCl/SnCl4 (c) thiophene + Br2 (excess) (d) pyrrole + CHCl3/KOH, Δ (e) 2,5-dimethylthiophene + HNO3, Ac2O.

Show solution
  1. (a) Pyrrole is deprotonated (pyrrolyl potassium); methylation on N: N-methylpyrrole.
  2. (b) 2-Methylfuran: C5 (the free α-position, activated by the CH3 group at C2) is acylated: 5-methyl-2-acetylfuran (2-acetyl-5-methylfuran).
  3. (c) Br2 excess: both α positions, then β: first 2-bromothiophene, then 2,5-dibromothiophene, then 2,3,5-tribromothiophene and finally tetrabromothiophene as bromination proceeds.
  4. (d) Reimer–Tiemann ring expansion (dichlorocarbene): 3-chloropyridine (Ciamician–Dennstedt).
  5. (e) Both α-positions blocked, so substitution goes to the β-position: 2,5-dimethyl-3-nitrothiophene.

15.3 Pyridine

pyridine pyridinium ion pyridine N-oxide piperidine
Pyridine: sp² N with an in-plane lone pair (basic), a π-deficient ring. Piperidine is the fully saturated, much more basic, analogue.

π-Deficient ring. The electronegative N pulls electron density from the ring carbons (–I and –M effects, like a nitro group). Pyridine is therefore less reactive than benzene toward electrophiles (its deactivation is comparable to that of a nitro group), and more reactive toward nucleophiles. In acid it is protonated (pyridinium, pKaH 5.2), which deactivates it further.

Trap Pyridine is less basic than aliphatic amines (and less than imidazole) but is a better base than aniline. The explanation is the hybridisation: sp2 N holds the lone pair closer to the nucleus than sp3 N. Piperidine (pKaH 11) is 106 times more basic than pyridine.

Electrophilic substitution of pyridine

Substitution occurs at C3 (β) and needs forcing conditions: nitration KNO3, H2SO4, 573 K (yield ≈ 20 %); sulphonation oleum, HgSO4, 513 K → pyridine-3-sulphonic acid; bromination Br2, oleum, 573 K → 3-bromopyridine. Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation fail completely: AlCl3 complexes the N, and the ring is too deactivated.

pyridine, attack at C3: charge at C2 charge at C4 charge at C6
Attack at C3: the positive charge falls on C2, C4, C6: never on N.
pyridine, attack at C2: charge at C3 charge at C5 charge on N (sextet; very unfavourable)
Attack at C2 (or C4): one contributor puts the positive charge on the electronegative N, with only six electrons. This is the worst possible place and rules out C2 and C4 attack.
Key idea For EAS on pyridine: attack at C3 because C2/C4 attack would give a contributor with a positive nitrogen bearing a sextet. The same reasoning for nucleophilic attack is reversed: attack at C2 or C4 puts the negative charge on N (good), attack at C3 does not.
Pyridine N-oxide reverses the deactivation: the O− donates by resonance, so N-oxide is nitrated by HNO3/H2SO4 at C4; PCl3 (or Zn) then removes the oxygen to give 4-nitropyridine. POCl3 converts N-oxide into 2- and 4-chloropyridines.

Nucleophilic substitution of pyridine

Pyridine reacts with strong nucleophiles at C2 (and C4), because the anionic intermediate (Meisenheimer-type) places the negative charge on N.

  • 2- and 4-halopyridines undergo SNAr with RO−, R2NH, RS− readily; 3-halopyridines are as unreactive as chlorobenzene.
  • Side chains: the CH3 of 2- and 4-picoline is acidic (conjugation of the carbanion with N, like o/p-nitrotoluene) and can be condensed with aldehydes (far more acidic than toluene, pKa ≈ 41); 3-picoline lacks this. Oxidation of side chains with KMnO4 leaves the (electron-poor) ring intact: 3-picoline → nicotinic acid (niacin), 4-picoline → isonicotinic acid (precursor of isoniazid).
  • Reduction: H2/Pt or Ni, or Na/EtOH → piperidine (pyridine's ring is hydrogenated, in contrast to the benzene ring in styrene, because the aromatic stabilisation is smaller).
  • Pyridine is an excellent nucleophilic catalyst and acid scavenger for acylation and sulphonylation (via acylpyridinium); N-alkylation with CH3I gives N-methylpyridinium iodide.
Example 35 Adv

Pyridine problems

(a) Why does pyridine nitrate at C3 but pyridine N-oxide at C4? (b) Predict the product of pyridine + NaNH2, 383 K; then H2O. (c) Which is more reactive toward NaOMe: 2-chloropyridine, 3-chloropyridine or 4-chloropyridine?

Show solution
  1. (a) In pyridine, C2/C4 attack puts the positive charge on N+(sextet) so C3 is least bad, and the reaction is slow (also the protonated ring in H2SO4 is deactivated). In the N-oxide, the O− donates through N+=: the contributor with the charge on C4 is stabilised by O (it becomes N+=O-type resonance with all octets), so C4 is the activated position, and N-oxide is attacked 104+ times faster than pyridine.
  2. (b) 2-Aminopyridine (Chichibabin).
  3. (c) 2-Chloro and 4-chloro (comparable, both fast) ≫ 3-chloro: 2- and 4-attack place the negative charge of the Meisenheimer-type intermediate on N; 3-attack cannot.
