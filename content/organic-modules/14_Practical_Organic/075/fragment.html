  • §1 A pure organic solid has a sharp melting point (melts over 0.5 to 1 K); a pure liquid has a constant boiling point at a given pressure. A trace of impurity lowers and broadens the melting range, and raises (non-volatile impurity) or changes the boiling point.
  • §3.2 In a T–x diagram the vapour curve lies above (richer in the more volatile component than) the liquid curve. The staircase between the two curves is the number of theoretical plates needed. For an azeotrope the two curves touch, so beyond that composition the fractionating column gives no further separation.

  • §5.1 On a polar adsorbent (silica gel, alumina) the order of elution with a non-polar solvent is: hydrocarbons < alkyl halides < ethers < esters < ketones, aldehydes < alcohols < amines < acids. The more polar a compound, the stronger it sticks and the lower its Rf.
  • §7.1 Why sodium and not (say) iron? Sodium is a strong reducing agent that cleaves the C–N, C–S, C–X bonds, and it forms ionic salts that dissolve in water. Why not just heat in air? Burning oxidises N to N2 or NOx, S to SO2 and halogen to HX or X2: the information as ions is lost. Why boil with water? To destroy leftover sodium (Na + H2O → NaOH + ½H2) and bring the salts into solution; alcohol is sometimes added first to destroy Na safely. Why is the extract alkaline? NaOH from the excess sodium; it keeps cyanide from being lost as HCN.
  • §7.4 Blood red with FeCl3 but not Prussian blue ⇒ N and S are both present in the compound and the sodium was insufficient to decompose NaSCN. To distinguish, repeat with more sodium: the pattern changes to Prussian blue + violet nitroprusside + black PbS.
  • §8 The ions CN−, S2− and X− interfere with each other: remove CN−/S2− by boiling with HNO3 before the halogen test; do the sulphur test before acidifying with HNO3; excess sodium turns SCN− into CN− + S2−.
  • §9A Why dissolution in an aqueous reagent means a chemical change: NaOH, NaHCO3 and HCl dissolve compounds by turning them into salts (RCOO−Na+, ArO−Na+, RNH3+Cl−), which are ionic and water-soluble; concentrated H2SO4 dissolves compounds that can be protonated on O, N or a π bond (oxonium or carbocation formation) and fails to dissolve alkanes, alkyl halides and arenes.
  • §10.1 Br2/CCl4 detects unsaturation but does not distinguish alkene from alkyne. Ammoniacal silver or copper(I) separates terminal alkynes from alkenes and from internal alkynes (they have no acidic ≡C–H, pKa ≈ 25).
  • §11.1 Which compounds give the iodoform test? Those with CH3–CO–(C or H) or CH3–CH(OH)–(C or H) units: acetaldehyde (yes), ethanol (yes), acetone, butan-2-one, acetophenone, isopropyl alcohol (yes); formaldehyde, methanol, benzophenone, diethyl ketone, acetic acid, ethyl acetate, propan-1-ol (no).
  • §12.1 Hinsberg in words: primary amine → RNHSO2Ph, which still has an N–H and is acidic (pKa ≈ 10-11), dissolves in aqueous NaOH. Secondary amine → R2NSO2Ph, no N–H, insoluble in alkali. Tertiary amine → no sulphonamide; remains as insoluble amine, dissolves in dilute HCl.
  • §15 The order matters: KOH absorbs both CO2 and water, so CaCl2 must come first. Also CaCl2 must not be placed after KOH (water vapour from the KOH solution). If the compound contains nitrogen, oxides of nitrogen form; a spiral of red-hot copper gauze reduces them to N2 so they do not enter the KOH. If it contains halogens, silver gauze retains the halogen; sulphur is retained by lead chromate (PbCrO4) as PbSO4.

  • §18 For a compound with several halogens only the precipitate mass is measured, not the individual halide; if both Cl and Br are present, one needs two equations (total mass of AgX and total Ag). A sulphur-containing compound that also contains halogens: the halogen precipitated as AgX is accompanied by sulphate that remains in solution, so Ag+ is added first and removed before BaCl2.
  • §22.2 Two rules cover most questions: (1) the more electronegative the neighbouring atom/group, the larger δ (CH3–CH3 0.9; CH3–O 3.3; CH3–Cl 3.0); effect falls off over about three bonds; (2) π-electron systems create their own local fields: protons on the periphery of a benzene ring are deshielded (δ 7), aldehyde H (δ 9-10) very strongly deshielded (the C=O acts similarly), alkenyl H δ 5-6. Acid protons (O–H) exchange: add D2O, shake, and the O–H (and N–H) signal disappears, since R–OH + D2O ⇌ R–OD + HDO.
