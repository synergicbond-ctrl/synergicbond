4. Preparation of alkanes

The methods fall into five logical groups: (a) reduction of something with the same carbon skeleton, (b) coupling that joins two carbon fragments, (c) decarboxylation which removes a carbon, (d) hydrolysis of an organometallic, (e) hydrogenation of unsaturated hydrocarbons. Always count carbons: coupling methods increase the chain, decarboxylation shortens it by one, the rest keep the chain.

4.1 Catalytic hydrogenation (Sabatier–Senderens reaction)

Alkene or alkyne + H2 over finely divided Ni (200–300 °C, Sabatier–Senderens), or Pt/Pd (room temperature) gives the alkane with the same carbon skeleton.

H₂, Ni, Δ⟶
Propene → propane

Mechanism (outline): H2 is adsorbed on the metal surface and splits into metal-bound H atoms; the alkene π bond binds to the surface; two H atoms are delivered to the same face of the double bond one after the other (syn addition). The full stereochemical consequences are in section 24.

4.2 Reduction of alkyl halides

  • Zn/H+ or Zn–Cu couple in ethanol: RX + 2[H] → RH + HX. Metal gives electrons: R–X + 2e− → R− + X−, then R− + H+.
  • LiAlH4 (also NaBH4 in polar aprotic solvents for 2°/3°): hydride displaces halide (SN2 at 1°, so 1° and unhindered 2° react best).
  • H2/Pd hydrogenolysis (the C–X bond is cleaved by H2).
  • HI + red P at 150 °C (strong reducing agent) reduces alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and acids all the way to alkanes: ROH + 2HI → RH + H2O + I2; red P regenerates HI from I2.
Zn, H⁺⟶
1-Bromopropane → propane (same skeleton)

4.3 Hydrolysis of Grignard reagents

R–MgX is a source of carbanion character: RMgX + H2O → RH + Mg(OH)X. Any compound with an acidic H (water, alcohol, amine, terminal alkyne, carboxylic acid) destroys a Grignard reagent this way. This is also the reason Grignard reactions must be done in dry ether.

Mg, dry ether⟶ H₂O⟶
2-Bromopropane → isopropylmagnesium bromide → propane
Trap A Grignard reagent reacts with any acidic H (pKa < about 35) faster than with a C=O. A molecule containing both an –OH (or –COOH, ≡C–H) and a C=O cannot be converted to a Grignard reagent without protecting the acidic group.

4.4 Wurtz reaction

2 R–X + 2 Na in dry ether → R–R + 2 NaX. The product has twice the carbon count of R when one halide is used. Mechanism (two accepted descriptions): (i) organosodium route: R–X + 2Na → R–Na + NaX; then R–Na + R–X → R–R + NaX (the carbanion R− attacks the second alkyl halide in an SN2 step); (ii) radical route: single electron transfer gives R• which couple. Both are consistent with the useful rules below.

+ 2 Na, dry ether⟶
Wurtz coupling of bromoethane → n-butane

Trap Wurtz with a mixture of two different halides gives three alkanes (R–R, R′–R′, R–R′), not one. With three different halides there are six coupling combinations, but two combinations can give the same alkane (see the next example), so count distinct alkanes, not combinations.
Example 3 Foundation

Wurtz counting

A mixture of CH3Br, C2H5Br and n-C3H7Br is treated with sodium in dry ether. How many distinct alkanes can form? Name them.

Show solution
  1. Possible couplings: CH3–CH3 (ethane), C2H5–C2H5 (n-butane), n-C3H7–n-C3H7 (n-hexane), CH3–C2H5 (propane), CH3–C3H7 (n-butane), C2H5–C3H7 (n-pentane): 6 combinations.
  2. CH3–n-C3H7 and C2H5–C2H5 are both n-butane, so one alkane is counted twice.
  3. Distinct alkanes: ethane, propane, n-butane, n-pentane, n-hexane = 5.
Example 4 Advanced

Intramolecular Wurtz

Which of the following gives a cyclic hydrocarbon when heated with sodium in dry ether: (a) 1,2-dibromoethane (b) 1,3-dibromopropane (c) 1,4-dibromobutane (d) 1,6-dibromohexane? Give the ring product where it forms and say why (a) differs.

Show solution
  1. (b), (c) and (d) form rings: cyclopropane, cyclobutane, and cyclohexane (ring strain is the only drawback; yields fall for cyclobutane, are good for 5- and 6-rings).
  2. (a): 1,2-dibromoethane + Na (or Zn) gives ethene by dehalogenation (β-elimination of the two bromines): a two-carbon fragment cannot form a ring, so elimination is the only outcome.

4.5 Kolbe electrolysis

Electrolysis of aqueous sodium or potassium salt of a carboxylic acid gives an alkane with twice the carbons of the R group in the acid, along with CO2 (anode) and H2 + NaOH (cathode). 2 RCOO− → R–R + 2 CO2 + 2 e− at the anode. Mechanism: anode: RCOO− → RCOO• + e−; RCOO• → R• + CO2; 2 R• → R–R. Cathode: 2 H2O + 2 e− → H2 + 2 OH−. A radical mechanism, so no rearrangement and coupling of identical radicals.

electrolysis, aq. Na salt⟶
Sodium acetate → ethane (+ CO₂ at anode, H₂ at cathode)
  • Mixed Kolbe (two different salts) gives a mixture of three products, as in Wurtz.
  • Dicarboxylic acid salts (succinate) give an alkene (ethene) with CO2: −OOC–CH2–CH2–COO− → CH2=CH2 + 2CO2.
Shortcut Products of Kolbe: same carbon number as 2 × (R). Acid RCOOH with n carbons → alkane with 2(n−1) carbons. Acetic (2 C) → ethane (2 C). Propionic (3 C) → butane (4 C). Butanoic (4 C) → hexane (6 C).

4.6 Decarboxylation with soda lime

RCOONa + NaOH (as soda lime, NaOH + CaO) heated → R–H + Na2CO3. The carbon chain is shortened by one carbon; the carboxyl carbon leaves as carbonate. The mechanism involves a carbanion-like transition state: hydroxide attacks the carboxylate in an addition step, and R− leaves as it picks up a proton. Works best when R− is stabilised (aryl, vinyl, α to EWG) or simply at high temperature for alkyl.

NaOH, CaO, Δ⟶
Sodium acetate → methane
  • CH3COONa → CH4; C2H5COONa → C2H6; (n carbons) → (n−1) carbons.
  • Dicarboxylate gives the lower alkane after two decarboxylations (sodium succinate + 2 NaOH/CaO → ethane).
  • Compare with Kolbe: Kolbe doubles the chain (R–R), soda lime removes one carbon (R–H).
Example 5 Foundation

Kolbe vs soda lime

Give the product of (a) electrolysis of aqueous sodium propanoate, (b) soda lime decarboxylation of sodium propanoate, (c) electrolysis of potassium succinate.

Show solution
  1. (a) CH3CH2COO− → CH3CH2• → butane (CH3CH2CH2CH3).
  2. (b) CH3CH2COONa + NaOH → ethane + Na2CO3.
  3. (c) Both carboxylates are lost; the two CH2• radicals formed adjacent to each other pair to make a π bond: ethene + 2 CO2.

4.7 Corey–House synthesis (the clean cross-coupling)

R2CuLi (lithium dialkylcuprate, Gilman reagent) + R′–X → R–R′ + RCu + LiX. Prepared as: R–X + 2 Li → R–Li + LiX; 2 R–Li + CuI → R2CuLi + LiI.

  • Gives unsymmetrical alkanes cleanly (no mixture, unlike Wurtz).
  • R′X should be a methyl, 1° (or aryl/vinyl) halide; 2° give lower yields; R in the cuprate can be 1°, 2° or 3°.
  • The carbon count is the sum of the two groups.
1. Li 2. CuI⟶ CH₃CH₂CH₂Br⟶
Isopropyl cuprate + 1-bromopropane → 2-methylpentane
Trap In the Corey–House reaction the alkyl halide R′X is the electrophile and the cuprate is the nucleophile. To make an alkane R–R′ with R tertiary, put the tertiary group on the cuprate and use a 1° halide (not the reverse).
Example 6 Advanced

Make 2,2-dimethylbutane

Plan a synthesis of 2,2-dimethylbutane (CH3CH2C(CH3)3) from alkyl halides using (a) Wurtz and (b) Corey–House. Which is practical?

Show solution
  1. Disconnect: (CH3)3C– + CH2CH3: that is t-butyl and ethyl, or t-butyl + CH3CH2.
  2. (a) Wurtz with t-BuCl + EtBr gives t-BuCl mostly eliminated to isobutene and t-Bu–t-Bu, Et–Et: impractical.
  3. (b) Corey–House: (CH3)3C–Li + CuI → [(CH3)3C]2CuLi, then CH3CH2Br → (CH3)3C–CH2CH3 in good yield (the 1° bromide is the electrophile; the 3° group is carried by the cuprate).

4.8 Reduction of carbonyl compounds to alkanes

  • Clemmensen reduction: C=O → CH2 with Zn–Hg/conc. HCl (acidic medium).
  • Wolff–Kishner (Huang–Minlon): C=O → CH2 with NH2NH2, KOH, ethylene glycol, 200 °C (basic medium).
  • HI/red P and Raney Ni (thioacetals) also work.

Choose the method by the other groups in the molecule: acid-sensitive groups → Wolff–Kishner; base-sensitive → Clemmensen. The mechanisms and further detail are in the carbonyl chapter; for this chapter just record that C=O → CH2 gives the alkane with the same skeleton.

Zn–Hg, conc. HCl⟶
Butanone → butane

4.9 From natural sources

Methane: natural gas (80–95 %) and biogas (anaerobic decay); also from CO + 3H2 → CH4 + H2O (Ni, 250 °C, methanation) and Al4C3 + 12 H2O → 4 Al(OH)3 + 3 CH4 (also Be2C). Calcium carbide → ethyne (alkyne), not alkane; CaC2 + 2 H2O → C2H2 + Ca(OH)2; Mg2C3 + 4 H2O → propyne. These "carbide hydrolysis" facts are standard one-liners: Al4C3 and Be2C → methane (methanides), CaC2 → ethyne (acetylides), Mg2C3 → propyne (allylenides). Petroleum: fractional distillation, cracking, reforming; Fischer–Tropsch: nCO + (2n+1)H2 → CnH2n+2 + nH2O (Co or Fe catalyst).
