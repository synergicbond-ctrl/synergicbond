d-Block Elements

1. Position, definition and classification

Transition elements are those whose atoms have an incomplete d subshell or which can form cations with an incomplete d subshell. The d-block occupies groups 3–12 between the s- and p-blocks. All are metals; their properties change gradually across a series and also show group relationships.

Important distinction: Sc is a transition element (3d1 in the atom), even though Sc3+ is d0. Cu, Ag and Au are transition elements because Cu2+, Ag2+ and Au3+ are d9, d9 and d8 respectively. Zn, Cd and Hg are conventionally excluded as transition elements because their atoms and ordinary cations have d10 configurations. Cn properties are largely predicted.

Configuration exceptions: examination checks

In the first three series, the usual ns² pattern has ten exceptions: Cr, Cu; Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag; Pt, Au. Pd is the only one of these with ns⁰; its 4d shell is full. Ni, Tc and W follow the usual ns² pattern. La and Ac are d¹s² in their neutral ground states; in conventional detached-table teaching they introduce the lanthanoid and actinoid families, not additional exceptions to d¹s². Do not count Gd or Lu as members of the 5d transition row merely because their neutral atoms contain a 5d electron.

3. Physical properties, metallic bonding and atomisation

Most transition metals are hard, lustrous, malleable, ductile and good conductors. Strong cohesion arises from delocalised valence electrons, including d-electrons; exact melting points depend on crystal structure and electronic band energies, not simply the number of unpaired electrons in free atoms.

4. Atomic radii, ionic radii and density

Atomic radii generally decrease from Sc through early transition elements as effective nuclear charge increases; the decrease is less pronounced in the middle and later portions. Increased d-electron shielding and repulsion contribute. A small increase can occur near the end. Exact order depends on the chosen definition of radius.

For comparable ions of equal charge and coordination number, radius generally decreases across a series; for a given element, higher positive charge normally means smaller radius. A high-spin ion can be larger than its low-spin counterpart.

4d and 5d homologues have nearly equal radii because of lanthanoid contraction, e.g. Zr and Hf, Nb and Ta, Mo and W. This makes Zr/Hf separation difficult. Density = mass/volume; the 5d series is much denser than the 4d series because mass increases without a comparable increase in radius. Os and Ir have almost equal very high densities (about 22.6 g cm−3); the exact order depends on values and conditions.

5. Ionisation enthalpies and exchange energy

The first, second and third ionisation enthalpies broadly rise across the first series but are irregular because of changes in orbital occupancy, electron pairing and exchange. Removing an electron from a particularly stable configuration requires extra energy. Exchange pairs for m parallel-spin electrons = m(m−1)/2; this is a counting model, not a quantitative energy formula.

The large first-ionisation-energy increases at Cu → Zn, Ag → Cd and Au → Hg are notable group-end changes, not a universal constant step across each row.

6. Oxidation states and their stability

Both the ns and (n−1)d electrons can participate in bonding. Hence oxidation states often differ by one, unlike the two-unit differences frequently seen for main-group elements. The number of accessible states generally increases toward Mn and then decreases. The highest common oxidation states of the early 3d metals correspond to their group number. Ru and Os also form tetroxides in oxidation state +8.

9. Magnetic properties and crystal field

Diamagnetism: no permanent electronic moment in the simple closed-shell case; weak repulsion by an applied field. Paramagnetism: unpaired electrons produce attraction. Ferromagnetism: cooperative parallel alignment below the Curie temperature (Fe, Co, Ni). Antiferromagnetism: opposing alignment, e.g. MnO below its Néel temperature. Ferrimagnetism: unequal opposing moments, e.g. Fe3O4.

Spin-only magnetic moment μ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons. Use actual ligand field and geometry rather than assuming every d5 ion has five unpaired electrons.

Temperature-dependent and temperature-independent response

For independent localized spins, χM approximately follows Curie’s law C/T. In a magnetically ordered material, χ follows more complex temperature dependence: ferromagnetic ordering disappears above the Curie temperature, antiferromagnetic order above the Néel temperature. Van Vleck paramagnetism arises from field-induced mixing of excited electronic states and may be approximately temperature independent. A d⁰ or d¹⁰ count rules out spin-only paramagnetism, not every form of magnetic response.

Wüstite is better represented as Fe1₋ₓO than FeO. Compositions such as Fe0.94O and Fe0.84O illustrate variable iron deficiency. Fe2+ vacancies are compensated in part by Fe3+. In the simple charge-balance model, Fe2+ per O = 1−3x, Fe3+ per O = 2x, and the vacancy fraction = x.

Dichromate structural equivalence and primary standards

In the idealised dichromate structure, six terminal Cr–O bonds fall in one approximate length class (163 pm); the two bridging Cr–O bonds are longer (179 pm). The Cr–O–Cr angle is about 126°. Chromate is comparable to sulfate (tetrahedral); dichromate to disulfate (corner-sharing tetrahedra). Both CrO₃ and SO₃ are acidic oxides, but solid structures depend on polymorph: do not identify every form of SO₃ with polymeric CrO₃.

Primary standard: a sufficiently pure, stable material of accurately known composition suitable for preparing a standard solution by direct weighing. Examples include K₂Cr₂O₇, oxalic acid dihydrate and suitably pure Mohr’s salt (protected from oxidation). Secondary-standard solutions require standardisation because concentration cannot reliably be established by weighing alone. Deliquescence (absorbing enough moisture to dissolve), hygroscopicity (absorbing moisture), efflorescence (losing water of crystallisation), atmospheric reaction and photodecomposition can compromise standards.

Gd³⁺ (4f⁷) has seven unpaired electrons and is essentially colourless in visible light. This is not diamagnetism: spin selection and excitation energies matter. 21. Lanthanoid contraction, basicity and separation

As Z increases from La to Lu, incoming 4f electrons shield each other poorly. Effective nuclear charge increases and ionic radii decrease. Compare radii at the same coordination number. The contraction is particularly regular for Ln3+ ions; metallic radii show anomalies at Eu and Yb due to divalent metallic bonding character.

Consequences: (1) chemical properties of adjacent Ln3+ ions are very similar, making separation difficult; (2) Zr and Hf have very similar radii; (3) hydroxide basicity generally decreases from La(OH)3 to Lu(OH)3 as charge density increases; (4) hydrolysis and binding to many hard oxygen donors generally become stronger; (5) Y3+ is similar in size to heavier lanthanoids.

Separation methods: fractional crystallisation exploits small solubility differences; ion-exchange exploits small differences in complex formation and resin affinity; solvent extraction uses selective distribution between phases. Selective oxidation of Ce(III) to Ce(IV) or reduction of Eu(III) to Eu(II) can facilitate their separation.

Metallic radius anomalies: Eu and Yb

Unlike the generally smooth Ln³⁺ ionic-radius curve, metallic radii show large upward deviations for Eu and Yb, whose metallic bonding is substantially divalent. The two 6s electrons contribute to bonding without disrupting especially stable 4f⁷ and 4f¹⁴ arrangements. A table omitting or suppressing the Yb anomaly should not be treated as a universal literal ordering. Compare like radius conventions and coordination numbers. Contraction also generally increases ionisation energy and binding to hard donor ligands; individual solubilities and thermal stabilities cannot be predicted from contraction alone.
