Electrical Properties, Semiconductors and Superconductivity

10-part continuous textbook chapter

9.1 From atomic orbitals to energy bands

When a very large number of atoms forms a solid, each atomic orbital splits into an enormous number of closely spaced crystal orbitals. These levels merge into allowed energy bands separated by forbidden energy gaps.

TermMeaning
Valence bandHighest band occupied at absolute zero in an ordinary semiconductor or insulator
Conduction bandHigher band whose mobile carriers can transport charge
Band gap EgE_gEg​Forbidden energy interval between valence- and conduction-band edges
Fermi level EFE_FEF​Chemical potential for electrons; at 0 K it separates occupied and unoccupied states

Electrical conduction requires both mobile charge carriers and nearby unoccupied states into which they can move under an electric field.

9.2 Conductors, semiconductors and insulators

ClassBand pictureTemperature dependence of conductivity
MetalPartially filled band or overlapping bandsUsually decreases as temperature rises because lattice scattering increases
SemiconductorSmall band gap, typically of order electron-volts or lessIncreases strongly with temperature as carriers are generated
InsulatorLarge band gapRemains very low under ordinary conditions
σ=nqμ\sigma=nq\muσ=nqμ

For one carrier type: number density nnn, charge magnitude qqq, mobility μ\muμ.

In a metal, carrier density is already large and temperature mainly changes mobility. In a semiconductor, thermal excitation changes carrier density dramatically, often dominating the mobility decrease.

1
Worked example

Temperature trend

Why does the resistance of a pure semiconductor usually fall on heating while that of a metal rises?

  1. Heating a semiconductor promotes many more electrons across the band gap, increasing carrier density.
  2. Heating a metal mainly increases phonon scattering, reducing mobility.
  3. The dominant effects therefore have opposite signs.
Semiconductor conductivity rises; metallic conductivity usually falls with temperature.

9.3 Intrinsic semiconductors

An intrinsic semiconductor is chemically pure in the ideal model. Thermal excitation creates an electron in the conduction band and a corresponding hole in the valence band.

A hole is not a separate positive particle in vacuum; it is a convenient description of the collective motion of missing valence electrons. Under an electric field, electron and hole currents add to the conventional current.

Fermi level

For a symmetric ideal intrinsic semiconductor, EFE_FEF​ lies near the middle of the gap. Different electron and hole effective masses shift it slightly.

9.4 Extrinsic semiconductors

Controlled substitutional doping introduces shallow energy levels and greatly increases carrier density without destroying the crystal lattice.

TypeDopant in Si/GeMajority carrierMinority carrierFermi-level shift
n-typeGroup 15 donor: P, As, SbElectronsHolesToward conduction band
p-typeGroup 13 acceptor: B, Al, GaHolesElectronsToward valence band

In n-type material, donor atoms contribute electrons but become fixed positively charged donor ions after ionisation. The crystal remains electrically neutral overall. Similarly, ionised acceptors are fixed negative centres in p-type material.

n-type is not negatively charged

The labels n and p identify the majority mobile carrier. They do not mean the bulk solid carries net negative or positive charge.

2
Worked example

Dopant choice

Which dopant converts silicon into p-type material: phosphorus or boron? Explain.

  1. Silicon has four valence electrons.
  2. Boron has three and leaves one unsatisfied bond that acts as an acceptor level.
  3. Thermal excitation fills the acceptor and leaves a mobile hole.
Boron produces p-type silicon.

9.5 The p–n junction

When p-type and n-type regions meet, majority carriers diffuse across the interface and recombine. Fixed ionised donors and acceptors remain, creating a depletion region and an internal electric field that opposes further diffusion.

BiasEffect on barrier/depletion widthCurrent
Forward biasReduces barrier and narrows depletion regionLarge majority-carrier current
Reverse biasRaises effective barrier and widens depletion regionSmall minority-carrier current until breakdown

Rectification follows because the junction conducts much more readily in forward bias than in reverse bias. Light can generate electron–hole pairs, enabling photodiodes and solar cells.

3
Worked example

Forward-bias identification

For a silicon diode, which terminal should be connected to the p side for forward bias?

  1. Forward bias must lower the junction barrier.
  2. Connect the p region to the positive terminal and the n region to the negative terminal.
Positive to p and negative to n gives forward bias.

9.6 Ionic and mixed conduction

In ionic solids, charge can be transported by ions moving through vacancies or interstitial pathways. Ionic conductivity therefore depends strongly on defect concentration, migration energy and temperature.

σion∝ndefectexp⁡(−EmkT)\sigma_{ion}\propto n_{defect}\exp\left(-\frac{E_m}{kT}\right)σion​∝ndefect​exp(−kTEm​​)

Fast-ion conductors such as stabilised zirconia, beta-alumina and some fluorite oxides have connected pathways and abundant mobile defects. Some materials conduct both ions and electrons and are called mixed conductors.

Solid ionic conductors exist

The elementary rule ‘ionic solids do not conduct’ means ordinary defect-poor crystals conduct poorly at room temperature. It is not an absolute statement for all solids and temperatures.

9.7 Superconductivity

A superconductor below its critical temperature exhibits zero dc electrical resistance and expels magnetic flux from its interior, the Meissner effect. Perfect conductivity alone does not define superconductivity; magnetic flux expulsion is essential.

QuantityMeaning
Critical temperature TcT_cTc​Highest temperature for the superconducting state at specified field/current
Critical field HcH_cHc​ or Hc1,Hc2H_{c1},H_{c2}Hc1​,Hc2​Magnetic-field limits of the superconducting state
Critical current density JcJ_cJc​Current-density limit before superconductivity is destroyed
Meissner effectExpulsion of magnetic flux on cooling through TcT_cTc​

Type I superconductors show one critical field and complete flux exclusion below it. Type II superconductors have a mixed vortex state between lower and upper critical fields and are more useful in high-field magnets.

BCS and high-$T_c$ materials

Conventional superconductors are described by Cooper pairing mediated by lattice vibrations. Cuprate high-TcT_cTc​ superconductors are more complex; detailed microscopic theory is outside the JEE syllabus.

9.9 Fermi–Dirac occupancy

The probability that an available one-electron state of energy EEE is occupied at temperature TTT is given by the Fermi–Dirac distribution.

f(E)=11+exp⁡[(E−EF)/(kT)]f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp[(E-E_F)/(kT)]}f(E)=1+exp[(E−EF​)/(kT)]1​
  • At E=EFE=E_FE=EF​, f=1/2f=1/2f=1/2 at every non-zero temperature.
  • At 0 K, states below EFE_FEF​ are occupied and those above are empty.
  • Thermal broadening occurs over an energy scale of order kTkTkT.
Band occupancy is not carrier density by itself

Carrier concentration also depends on the density of available states. The Fermi function supplies the occupation probability.

9.10 Purification and liquid-crystal materials

Semiconductor devices require extremely pure material. In zone refining, a narrow molten zone moves along a solid bar. When an impurity has distribution coefficient k=Csolid/Cliquid<1k=C_{solid}/C_{liquid}<1k=Csolid​/Cliquid​<1, it prefers the melt and is swept toward one end through repeated passes.

Liquid crystals combine fluidity with orientational order. Nematic phases possess average molecular alignment without layered positional order; smectic phases possess layers; cholesteric phases show a helical director. Their optical anisotropy can be controlled by electric fields.

PhaseOrientational orderLayering
NematicYesNo

LCD principle

A twisted-nematic display uses field-controlled molecular orientation to change the polarisation state and transmitted intensity of light between polarisers.
