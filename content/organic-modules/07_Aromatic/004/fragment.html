2. Aromaticity: the criteria

A compound is aromatic if it satisfies all four conditions:

  • 1. Cyclic — a ring of atoms (the cyclic overlap of p orbitals is essential).
  • 2. Planar (or very nearly planar) — so that the p orbitals are parallel and overlap continuously.
  • 3. Fully conjugated — every ring atom has a p orbital (sp2 or sp) that takes part in the π system, with no sp3 atom interrupting the ring.
  • 4. (4n + 2) π electrons in the cyclic system (n = 0, 1, 2, …): Hückel's rule — 2, 6, 10, 14, 18 … electrons.

A cyclic, planar, fully conjugated system with 4n π electrons (4, 8, 12 …) is antiaromatic: it is destabilised relative to an open-chain analogue, and it avoids antiaromaticity if it can by distorting out of plane. A compound failing criteria 1–3 is non-aromatic (neither stabilised nor destabilised).

2.1 Counting π electrons correctly

The most common mistakes in this topic are counting errors. Memorise the rules:

  • A C=C inside the ring contributes 2 electrons.
  • An sp2 carbon with an empty p orbital (carbocation) contributes 0: cyclopropenyl cation 2 π, tropylium 6 π, cyclopentadienyl cation 4 π.
  • An sp2 carbon with a filled p orbital (carbanion lone pair in the p orbital) contributes 2: cyclopentadienyl anion 6 π, cyclopropenyl anion 4 π.
  • A ring heteroatom like the N of pyrrole, the O of furan or the S of thiophene has a lone pair that is in the p orbital (perpendicular to the ring) and contributes 2. A heteroatom like the N of pyridine with its lone pair in the plane (sp2) contributes 1 through its C=N bond: pyridine has 6 π electrons, and the N lone pair is not part of them.
  • A carbonyl C=O in the ring: the carbon contributes 0 (its p orbital is polarised toward O) and the ring is analysed as a cation at C. Tropone and cyclopentadienone: tropone has a 6 π electron aromatic-like tropylium resonance form; cyclopentadienone is antiaromatic and extremely reactive.
  • An exocyclic C=C: count as 0 for the ring carbon in the cationic/anionic resonance form: fulvene is treated using its dipolar form (cyclopentadienyl anion with an exocyclic CH2+).

Aromatic, antiaromatic and non-aromatic cyclic polyenes
Key idea Acid/base and hydride-loss consequences of aromaticity: cyclopentadiene is a remarkably strong carbon acid (pKa ≈ 16) because deprotonation gives the 6π aromatic anion; cycloheptatriene is not acidic (pKa ≈ 36; its anion would have 8π electrons, antiaromatic) but it readily loses hydride to give the aromatic 6π tropylium ion (tropylium bromide is an ionic, water-soluble salt). Cyclopentadiene does not lose hydride: the cation would be antiaromatic (4π).

2.2 Hückel's rule for polycyclic systems

Hückel's (4n + 2) count is exact only for monocyclic systems, but it works for fused benzenoid hydrocarbons if you count the π electrons of the periphery (or equivalently check that every ring can be drawn with Kekulé structures). Naphthalene (10 π), anthracene (14 π) and phenanthrene (14 π) are aromatic. The test for individual rings fails for pyrene (16 π) but its periphery has 14 π electrons: another reminder that this is a rough rule.

2.3 Heterocyclic aromatics

pyrrole furan thiophene pyridine pyrimidine imidazole indole quinoline
Heteroaromatics. Pyrrole, furan, thiophene: the heteroatom lone pair is in the π system (6π in total). Pyridine: the N lone pair is in the plane.
  • Pyrrole: the N lone pair is delocalised into the ring and is therefore unavailable to protons — pyrrole is an extremely weak base (pKaH ≈ −3.8). Its N–H is weakly acidic (pKa ≈ 17.5 in water scale) because the anion (pyrrolide) is aromatic too.
  • Pyridine: the N lone pair is in an sp2 orbital in the plane and available: pyridinium pKa ≈ 5.2.
  • Imidazole: has both kinds of N: the N–H nitrogen (pyrrole-like, lone pair in the π system) and the =N– nitrogen (pyridine-like, in-plane lone pair, the basic site; pKaH ≈ 7).
  • Furan, thiophene: the lone pair of the heteroatom that is in the π system contributes 2 π electrons; the second lone pair of O or S is in the plane and is not part of the π system.
Example 2 Main

Count π electrons

State whether each is aromatic, antiaromatic or non-aromatic: (a) cyclopentadienyl cation (b) cyclooctatetraene dianion (c) pyridine N-oxide ring (d) cyclopropenyl anion (e) 1,3-cycloheptadiene (f) pyrene periphery.

Show solution
  1. (a) 4 π electrons, planar, cyclic, conjugated → antiaromatic.
  2. (b) C8H82−: 10 π electrons, planar once it takes two extra electrons → aromatic (4n + 2, n = 2).
  3. (c) The ring is pyridine with N+–O−; ring π count is still 6 → aromatic.
  4. (d) 4 π electrons (2 in C=C, 2 in carbanion) → antiaromatic.
  5. (e) Two sp3 carbons interrupt conjugation → non-aromatic.
  6. (f) Pyrene has 16 π electrons in total; the perimeter has 14 (4n + 2 with n = 3) → treated as aromatic.
Example 3 Adv

Heteroatom contributions

How many π electrons does each ring contribute in (i) pyrrole (ii) pyridine (iii) furan (iv) imidazole (v) tropone (ring only)?

Show solution
  1. (i) 4 (two C=C) + 2 (N lone pair) = 6.
  2. (ii) 6: three C=C/C=N double bonds, N lone pair in plane not counted.
  3. (iii) 4 + 2 (one O lone pair) = 6; the other O lone pair is in the plane.
  4. (iv) 6: 4 from C=C and C=N, 2 from the pyrrole-like NH; the pyridine-like N lone pair is in the plane.
  5. (v) Tropone (cycloheptatrienone): the ring has 3 C=C = 6 π electrons and the exocyclic C=O is polarised C+–O−, giving a tropylium-like 6π ring; tropone is stabilised, strongly polar and basic at O.
