3. Classifying reactions

A reaction can be classified in three independent ways, and a complete description gives all three.

3.1 By the overall change in structure

  • Substitution: one atom or group replaces another (R–X + Nu− → R–Nu + X−).
  • Addition: two groups add across a multiple bond and a π bond is lost (C=C + HBr → H–C–C–Br).
  • Elimination: two groups are removed from adjacent atoms and a π bond forms (the reverse of addition).
  • Rearrangement: a group migrates and the skeleton or the functional group changes with no net gain or loss of atoms (pinacol, Beckmann, Claisen).
  • Oxidation and reduction: the oxidation state of carbon changes; gain of C–O, C–N or C–halogen bonds, or loss of C–H bonds, is oxidation.
  • Condensation: two molecules join with loss of a small molecule such as water (aldol condensation, esterification).
  • Pericyclic reactions: a concerted cyclic reorganisation of electrons (Diels–Alder, electrocyclisation, sigmatropic shifts).

3.2 By the attacking reagent

  • Nucleophilic: the key step is attack by a nucleophile (SN1, SN2, SNAr, addition to C=O or C≡N, conjugate addition, acyl substitution).
  • Electrophilic: the key step is attack by an electrophile (addition to alkenes and alkynes, electrophilic aromatic substitution, reaction of an enol or enolate with an electrophile).
  • Radical: the key step is attack by a radical (halogenation of alkanes, polymerisation, addition of HBr in the presence of peroxides, autoxidation).
Why It Happens The rate law reflects the species present in the rate-determining transition state, not the number of steps. Hence SN1 is first order in substrate and zero order in nucleophile, whereas SN2 is first order in each.

Four questions identify an unfamiliar reaction. (1) Is a π bond lost or gained (addition or elimination)? (2) If a σ bond is exchanged, is the substrate a carbonyl compound (acyl substitution or addition), an sp3 C–X compound (SN1, SN2, E1, E2) or an arene (substitution)? (3) What are the conditions: strong base, acid, light or peroxide, heat, Lewis acid? (4) Is a carbon–carbon bond formed (enolate, Grignard reagent, Friedel–Crafts, Diels–Alder)? The answers fix the intermediate.
