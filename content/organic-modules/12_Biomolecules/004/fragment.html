1.2 Fischer projections: rules you can use without losing marks

A Fischer projection is a flat drawing of a chain in its eclipsed conformation, with the vertical bonds pointing away from the viewer and the horizontal bonds pointing toward the viewer. For sugars the carbonyl carbon is at the top (C-1 for an aldose; the CH2OH carbon is C-1 and the C=O is C-2 for a 2-ketose).

  • Allowed: rotation by 180° in the plane of the paper (any multiple of 180°); an even number of swaps of substituents on one stereocentre.
  • Not allowed: rotation by 90° (gives the enantiomer-looking mistake), lifting the projection out of the plane and flipping it over, or a single swap of two groups on one centre (inverts it).
  • Odd and even swaps: one swap = inversion of that centre; two swaps = same configuration. Three cyclic permutations of the three non-vertical-chain groups keep the configuration.
  • Assigning R/S from a Fischer diagram: write the priorities; if the lowest priority group is on a horizontal bond (pointing toward you), the apparent direction 1→2→3 is reversed. If the lowest priority is on a vertical bond, read the direction as drawn.
Key idea D and L. Look at the stereocentre farthest from the carbonyl (C-5 in a hexose, C-4 in a pentose). If its –OH is on the right the sugar is D; on the left, L. D/L is a relative (configurational) label tied to D-(+)-glyceraldehyde; it has nothing to do with the sign of rotation (d/l or +/−) or with R/S.

How many stereoisomers?

For an open-chain monosaccharide with n stereocentres (no symmetry): 2n. An aldose with N carbons has N − 2 stereocentres; a 2-ketose has N − 3.

The D-aldose family

Each D-aldose of n carbons gives two D-aldoses of n + 1 carbons when a CHOH is inserted below C-1 (the new C-2 is either right or left). That is exactly what Kiliani–Fischer synthesis does (1.8). Learn the eight D-aldohexoses with the mnemonic "All altruists gladly make gum in gallon tanks": allose, altrose, glucose, mannose, gulose, idose, galactose, talose.

<h4>Epimers, anomers, enantiomers, diastereomers</h4>
  • Enantiomers: mirror images; D-glucose and L-glucose (every centre inverted).
  • Epimers: diastereomeric sugars that differ in configuration at exactly one stereocentre. D-Glucose/D-mannose are C-2 epimers; D-glucose/D-galactose are C-4 epimers; D-glucose/D-allose are C-3 epimers. (Glucose/gulose differ at C-3 and C-5 and are not epimers.)
  • Anomers: cyclic forms that differ only at the new stereocentre created on ring closure (the anomeric carbon: C-1 of an aldose, C-2 of a 2-ketose): α- and β-D-glucopyranose.
D-glucoseD-mannose
C-2 epimers: they give the same osazone and interconvert in dilute alkali

Trap The word epimer applies to compounds with several stereocentres differing at one. A compound pair that differ at the only stereocentre are enantiomers, not epimers; two anomers are epimers too (differing at C-1 only) but are never called "C-1 epimers" in exam answers unless the question uses that word.
Example 1 Main

Counting D-sugars

(a) How many aldoheptoses are possible? (b) How many are D-sugars? (c) How many stereoisomers of a 2-ketoheptose exist?

Show solution
  1. (a) An aldoheptose has 7 carbons and 7 − 2 = 5 stereocentres: 25 = 32.
  2. (b) Half are D: 16.
  3. (c) A 2-ketoheptose has 7 − 3 = 4 stereocentres: 24 = 16 (8 D + 8 L).
Example 2 Main

D/L from R/S

D-Glyceraldehyde is (R). Without drawing, decide whether L-erythrose is (2S,3S) or (2R,3S).

Show solution
  1. D-Erythrose has both OH groups on the right: (2R,3R). Mirror image inverts both centres.
  2. L-Erythrose is therefore (2S,3S). (2R,3S) would be a meso-type diastereomer, which tetroses do not have in this open form: erythrose with unlike labels would be threose.
  3. Check by the threose pair: D-threose (2S,3R), L-threose (2R,3S).
Example 3 Adv

Same sugar, different drawing

A Fischer projection is drawn with CH2OH on top and CHO at the bottom. Counting from the CHO end (C-1), the –OH groups are on the left at C-2, right at C-3, left at C-4 and left at C-5. Identify the sugar.

Show solution
  1. A rotation by 180° in the plane is allowed. It puts CHO on top and moves every –OH to the opposite side.
  2. C-2 L→R, C-3 R→L, C-4 L→R, C-5 L→R, so the new code (C-2 to C-5, top to bottom) is RLRR.
  3. RLRR with C-5 on the right is D-glucose. (Without rotating, the C-5 –OH on the left might suggest an L-sugar: that is the trap in this question.)
Example 4 Adv

Aldaric acids: meso or not?

D-Glucose and D-galactose are separately oxidised with hot HNO3. Which product is optically inactive? Use Fischer codes.

Show solution
  1. HNO3 oxidises C-1 (CHO) and C-6 (CH2OH) to COOH and leaves C-2 to C-5 untouched, so the aldaric acid has the same code as the sugar.
  2. A chain HOOC–[code]–COOH is meso if it has an internal horizontal mirror plane. A horizontal mirror plane maps the top centre onto the bottom centre without changing left/right, so the code must read the same from both ends: a palindrome.
  3. D-Galactose is RLLR: a palindrome, so galactaric (mucic) acid is meso and optically inactive.
  4. D-Glucose is RLRR: not a palindrome (its reverse RRLR is a different compound), so D-glucaric acid is optically active.
Trap The test for a meso aldaric acid in a Fischer projection: the code must be a palindrome. RLLR (galactose) is one, RLRR (glucose) is not. Only D-allose (RRRR) and D-galactose (RLLR) among the eight D-aldohexoses give meso aldaric acids; for the pentoses, D-ribose (RRR) and D-xylose (RLR) do.
