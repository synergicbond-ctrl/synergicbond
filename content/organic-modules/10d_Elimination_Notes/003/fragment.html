4. E1cB: acidic hydrogen and a poor leaving group

In the E1cB (elimination, unimolecular, conjugate base) mechanism the order of events is reversed. The base first removes a hydrogen to give a carbanion (the conjugate base of the substrate), and only then does the leaving group depart. This is observed when the β-hydrogen is unusually acidic (for example α to a carbonyl, nitro or sulfonyl group, so the carbanion is stabilised by resonance) and the leaving group is poor (HO−, RO−, F−).

  1. Step 1: B− removes the acidic proton to give the stabilised enolate (carbanion).
  2. Step 2 (usually rate limiting): the enolate expels the leaving group from the neighbouring carbon, forming the C=C.
  3. Net result: the double bond ends up conjugated with the electron-withdrawing group.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOH, warm

Product:

Dehydration of the aldol (3-hydroxybutanal) to (E)-crotonaldehyde. The hydroxide is a poor leaving group, but the enolate pushes it out.

Reactant:

Reagent: Dilute acid or base, heat

Product:

Dehydration of diacetone alcohol gives mesityl oxide, a conjugated enone.

Characteristic features: (i) the reaction occurs under mild conditions despite the poor leaving group; (ii) the product is conjugated with the carbonyl group, so the more stable conjugated alkene forms even when it is not the Zaitsev alkene predicted from substitution alone; (iii) hydrogen-deuterium exchange at the acidic position is faster than elimination in a reversible first step; (iv) the anti-periplanar requirement is relaxed, because the carbanion has time to rotate. This is why aldol condensations eliminate water so readily.

Key idea. E1cB is the mechanism to think of whenever a β-hydroxy or β-halo carbonyl compound loses HX or H2O, and in E1cB-like eliminations where the β-hydrogen is made acidic by a neighbouring aryl or carbonyl group.
