1. What reduction means in organic chemistry

Reduction is the gain of electrons by a species. In organic molecules the electron count is followed through bonds: a carbon atom is reduced when it gains C–H bonds or loses bonds to more electronegative atoms (O, N, halogen). Addition of H2 to C=C, conversion of C=O into CH–OH, and replacement of C=O by CH2 are all reductions; removal of H2 or addition of O is oxidation.

Oxidation numbers of the functional carbon give an exact ladder. A primary carbon is −3 in R–CH3, −1 in R–CH2OH and R–CH2NH2, +1 in R–CHO and +3 in R–COOH, R–COOR′, R–CONH2, R–COCl and R–C≡N. Moving down the ladder by two units needs two electrons and two protons, written 2[H]. A ketone carbon (+2) becomes a secondary alcohol carbon (0) and then a CH2 carbon (−2).

Reducing agents belong to four families:

  • Hydride donors – NaBH4, LiAlH4, DIBAL-H, NaBH3CN: nucleophilic H− adds to polarised multiple bonds.
  • Catalytic hydrogenation – H2 with Pd, Pt, Ni or Rh: two H atoms are delivered from a metal surface.
  • Dissolving metals – Na/NH3(l), Zn, Sn, Fe: single-electron donors followed by protonation.
  • Transfer reductants – Al(OiPr)3/iPrOH, diimide, hydrazine: hydrogen is moved from a donor molecule to the substrate.

Trap. Not every change of functional group is a redox change. Hydration of ethene to ethanol, or formation of an ester from an acid and an alcohol, leaves the oxidation numbers of carbon unchanged. Always compare oxidation numbers before calling a step a reduction.
