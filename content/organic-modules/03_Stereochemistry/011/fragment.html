8. Chirality and symmetry

8.1 Definition and the symmetry test

An object is chiral if it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image (hands, screws, a spiral staircase). A molecule is chiral if and only if it has no improper axis of rotation Sn. Because the common improper axes are the mirror plane (σ = S1) and the centre of inversion (i = S2), the practical test is:

  • Look for a plane of symmetry (σ). If one exists: achiral.
  • Look for a centre of symmetry (i). If one exists: achiral.
  • Look for a higher improper axis (S4, S6...): rare, tested only with special molecules (e.g. certain spiranes and tetra-substituted adamantane-like or 1,3,5,7-tetra-substituted cyclooctatetraene). If one exists: achiral.
  • If none of these is present: the molecule is chiral.

Rotational axes (Cn) do not decide chirality: a molecule with only a C2 axis (such as (R,R)-tartaric acid) is chiral.

Key idea Chirality test = absence of σ, i and Sn. A single stereocentre always means chiral. Two or more stereocentres can be chiral or achiral (meso). A molecule with no stereocentre can still be chiral (allenes, biaryls, spiranes, helicenes, trans-cyclooctene).
Trap A conformer can look chiral while the molecule is achiral. We always judge the molecule in its most symmetrical accessible conformation (equilibrating conformers average out). That is why gauche-butane (chiral conformer) as a whole is achiral, and why meso-tartaric acid, which has a centre of symmetry in the anti conformation and a mirror plane in the eclipsed conformation, is achiral.

The ring example deserves a careful reading. In methylcyclohexane the ring carbon C-1 bears H, CH3 and two ring branches that are identical (going left or right gives the same sequence), so it is not a stereocentre. In 3-methylcyclohexanol the branches from C-1 (carrying OH) differ (one passes C-2 then C-3 methylated, the other passes C-6, C-5, C-4...): C-1 and C-3 are both stereocentres, giving 22 = 4 stereoisomers (cis pair and trans pair). Compare 4-methylcyclohexanol: C-1 and C-4 are not stereocentres (the two ring paths are identical), but cis/trans isomers exist.

Example 15 Main

Count stereocentres

Mark the stereocentres in (a) 2-chlorobutane, (b) 2,3-dichlorobutane, (c) 3-methylhexane, (d) 2-methylbutane-1-ol, (e) glucose (open chain), (f) cholesterol (no need to count exactly, but how many does it have?).

Show solution
  1. (a) C-2: one. (b) C-2 and C-3: two (equivalent halves, so a meso form exists). (c) C-3 has H, CH3, C2H5, C3H7: one. (d) CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2OH: C-2 has H, CH3, C2H5, CH2OH: one. (e) Open-chain glucose: C-2, 3, 4, 5: four. (f) Cholesterol: eight stereocentres (C-3, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14, 17, 20), giving 28 = 256 stereoisomers in principle.

8.4 Pseudoasymmetric centres and "r/s"

A stereocentre bearing four different groups, two of which are enantiomorphic (same constitution, opposite chirality sense: R and S versions), is pseudoasymmetric. It is labelled in lower-case, r or s. The standard example: C-3 of 2,3,4-trihydroxypentanedioic acid (trihydroxyglutaric acid), or C-3 of 2,3,4-trichloropentane. The molecule is achiral when the two enantiomorphic arms are (R) and (S) (meso, with r or s at the middle), and chiral when both arms are the same.

meso form A (R,s,S) meso form B (R,r,S) chiral (S,S)

Example 16 Adv

How many stereoisomers of 2,3,4-trichloropentane?

Show that there are four.

Show solution
  1. C-2 and C-4 are real stereocentres, each with H, Cl, CH3 and a CH(Cl)... arm. C-3 has H, Cl and two arms which differ only if C-2 and C-4 have opposite configurations: then C-3 is pseudoasymmetric.
  2. (2R,4R) and (2S,4S): the two arms are the same, C-3 is not a stereocentre; the molecule is chiral (enantiomeric pair): 2 stereoisomers.
  3. (2R,4S): the arms are enantiomorphic; C-3 may take Cl on one side or the other: two achiral (meso) diastereomers (3r and 3s).
  4. Total = 2 + 2 = 4. (Computer count: 4 total, 1 enantiomeric pair, 2 achiral.)
