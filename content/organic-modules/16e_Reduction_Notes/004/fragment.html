5. Heats of hydrogenation and alkene stability

The enthalpy released on hydrogenation is a direct measure of how much energy the alkene holds above the alkane. Since different alkenes often give the same alkane, a smaller (less exothermic) heat of hydrogenation means a more stable alkene. Approximate values are in kJ mol−1:

  • 1-Butene 127; cis-2-butene 120; trans-2-butene 116 – all give butane.
  • Cyclohexene 120; 1,3-cyclohexadiene 232; benzene 208 (all give the cyclohexane skeleton).

1-butene, cis-2-butene, trans-2-butene (least stable to most stable).

Stability increases with the number of alkyl groups on the double bond (hyperconjugation and stronger sp2–sp3 bonds) and is higher for trans than cis because the cis isomer suffers steric repulsion between its substituents. Conjugation also lowers the heat: two isolated cyclohexene units would release 2 × 120 = 240, but 1,3-cyclohexadiene releases 232, so conjugation stabilises it by about 8 kJ mol−1.

Benzene is the extreme case. Three isolated C=C bonds would release 3 × 120 = 360 kJ mol−1, yet the observed value is 208. The difference, about 152 kJ mol−1, is the resonance (aromatic stabilisation) energy, and explains why the benzene ring resists hydrogenation under conditions that reduce every ordinary C=C bond.

Key idea. Compare heats of hydrogenation only when the hydrogenation products are identical. Smaller value = more stable reactant. The cost of disturbing the aromatic sextet is why benzene needs forcing conditions.
