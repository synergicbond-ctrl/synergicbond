53. Mechanism: LiAlH4 reduction of an amide to an amine (why C=O becomes CH2)

  • Step 1 – hydride addition. AlH4− delivers H− to the carbonyl carbon (σ Al–H → π*C=O). The amide carbonyl is a poor electrophile (N lone pair donates into π*), so a very strong hydride donor is required; the oxygen becomes an alkoxide bound to Al.
  • Step 2 – the oxygen leaves, not the nitrogen. The O–Al(H)3 group is an excellent leaving group (a Lewis-acid-activated oxide), whereas an amide anion (R2N−) would be a very poor one. The N lone pair expels O–Al and forms the iminium ion R'–CH=N+R2.
  • Step 3 – second hydride. The iminium ion is a far better electrophile than the amide (its π* is low and carries a positive charge): a second H− gives the amine R'CH2NR2.
1. H⁻ adds to C=O⟶ 2. O–Al leaves (N lone pair pushes)⟶ 3. H⁻⟶
N-Methylacetamide → N-ethylmethylamine via an iminium ion

This explains two facts: (i) C=O → CH2 (the oxygen is lost as aluminate, nitrogen stays); (ii) the contrast with esters, which give alcohols: after the first hydride the ester's tetrahedral intermediate expels the alkoxy group (OR), giving an aldehyde that is reduced again to RCH2OH. In amides nitrogen is the better π-donor and O–Al the better leaving group, so the iminium path wins.
