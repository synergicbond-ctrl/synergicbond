12. Chemical tests for phenols

A set of simple tests distinguishes phenols from alcohols, carboxylic acids and ethers. Each rests on a specific feature of the phenol structure.

  • Neutral FeCl3: phenol, cresols, catechol, salicylic acid and salicylaldehyde give a violet, blue or green colour from the iron(III)-phenoxide complex. Alcohols, ethers and benzoic acid do not give the violet colour.
  • Bromine water: a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol forms with decolourisation of the bromine. The test works because the OH group strongly activates o/p positions; anisole also decolourises bromine water but gives a different product.
  • Liebermann nitroso test: phenol is warmed with NaNO2 and conc. H2SO4; a deep green or blue colour appears, turns red on dilution with water and returns blue or green with alkali. The p-nitrosophenol first formed condenses with excess phenol to form an indophenol dye.
  • Solubility in aqueous alkali: phenol dissolves in aqueous NaOH because it forms sodium phenoxide, but not in aqueous NaHCO3. A carboxylic acid dissolves in both, with CO2 effervescence in NaHCO3.
  • Litmus: phenol is so weakly acidic that it turns blue litmus only faintly red or not at all, because it is far weaker than a carboxylic acid; litmus is not a reliable test for phenols.
  • Sodium metal: evolves H2 but does not distinguish phenols from alcohols.
Trap. Enols (for example ethyl acetoacetate) also give colours with FeCl3, so no single test is conclusive; the combination of solubility in NaOH, no CO2 with NaHCO3, a violet colour with FeCl3 and a bromine-water precipitate is what confirms a phenol.

Consider an unknown C7H8O that dissolves in aqueous NaOH, gives violet colour with neutral FeCl3 and forms a tribromo derivative with excess bromine water. Only the meta isomer has all three o/p positions (2, 4, 6) free, so it is m-cresol; o-cresol and p-cresol would give dibromo products.
