37. Oxidation of aldehydes: Tollens', Fehling's, Benedict's

Aldehydes are oxidised by even mild oxidants because the carbonyl carbon carries a hydrogen that can be removed (via the hydrate). Ketones have no such H and are inert to these. Tollens' reagent: ammoniacal silver nitrate, [Ag(NH3)2]+OH−, freshly prepared. Aldehyde → carboxylate; Ag+ → Ag (a silver mirror on clean glass, black ppt on dirty glass).

Formula RCHO + 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 3OH− → RCOO− + 2Ag↓ + 4NH3 + 2H2O
Fehling's solution: Fehling A (aqueous CuSO4) + Fehling B (alkaline sodium potassium tartrate, Rochelle salt), mixed just before use; the tartrate keeps Cu2+ in solution as a deep-blue complex in alkali. Aliphatic aldehydes reduce it on warming to red-brown cuprous oxide. Benedict's solution is the citrate/Na2CO3 variant, milder.
Formula RCHO + 2Cu2+ + 5OH− → RCOO− + Cu2O↓ + 3H2O

Trap Why do ketones such as fructose give Tollens' test? Fructose is an α-hydroxy ketone; in alkaline medium it isomerises through an enediol to glucose and mannose (aldoses), which are oxidised. Similarly benzoin is oxidised at the CH(OH)–CO unit. Simple ketones, and acetone in particular, give no silver mirror.
Other mild oxidants: Schiff's reagent (see tests), Br2/H2O (aldehydes → acid; useful for aldoses), KMnO4, K2Cr2O7/H+, HNO3, H2O2, and even air: benzaldehyde slowly autoxidises to benzoic acid (via perbenzoic acid, a radical chain; benzaldehyde stored in air gets crystals of benzoic acid; commercial samples contain a stabiliser). Ketones are not oxidised by mild reagents; vigorous oxidation breaks C–C bonds (Popoff's rule).
