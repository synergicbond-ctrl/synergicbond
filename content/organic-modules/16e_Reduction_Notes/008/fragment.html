9. Reduction of carboxylic acid derivatives

Acid derivatives sit at the +3 level and can be reduced to aldehydes (+1) or to alcohols/amines (−1). The aldehyde is more reactive than the starting derivative, so stopping at that stage needs a bulky or electrophilic reagent at low temperature.

  • Acyl chloride → aldehyde: LiAlH(OtBu)3, −78 °C; or H2, Pd/BaSO4, quinoline (Rosenmund). → primary alcohol: NaBH4 or LiAlH4.
  • Ester → aldehyde: DIBAL-H (1 equiv), −78 °C. → two alcohols (RCH2OH + R′OH): LiAlH4.
  • Carboxylic acid → primary alcohol: LiAlH4 (first an acid–base step releases H2); NaBH4 fails.
  • Amide → amine (RCH2NR′2): LiAlH4. The C=O oxygen is removed, not the nitrogen.
  • Nitrile → primary amine: LiAlH4 or H2/Ni; → aldehyde: DIBAL-H then H3O+, or SnCl2/HCl then H2O (Stephen).
  • Lactone → diol (LiAlH4) or lactol (DIBAL-H, −78 °C).

Reactant:

Reagent: LiAlH(OtBu)3, THF, −78 °C

Reactant:

Reagent: LiAlH4, ether; then H2O

Product:

  1. Hydride adds to the amide carbonyl to give a tetrahedral intermediate containing O–Al.
  2. The nitrogen lone pair expels the aluminium oxide group, forming an iminium ion; an ester intermediate would instead expel alkoxide to give an aldehyde.
  3. A second hydride adds to the iminium ion and gives the amine.
Trap. Weinreb amides RCON(OMe)Me are the exception: hydride adds to give a stable chelated intermediate that does not collapse until work-up, so LiAlH4 or DIBAL-H delivers the aldehyde, not the amine.
