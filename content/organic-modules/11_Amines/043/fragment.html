43. Mechanism: azo coupling, step by step, with the orbital argument

Coupling is an electrophilic aromatic substitution in which the electrophile is the terminal nitrogen of Ar–N≡N+.

  • Step 1 – the electrophile. In ArN2+ the LUMO is π*(N≡N), mixed with the ring; its largest coefficient is on the terminal nitrogen (the one remote from the ring). So the nucleophile bonds to this N. Why it is a weak electrophile: the positive charge is delocalised into the aromatic ring (resonance), so only highly electron-rich partners react.
  • Step 2 – attack by the nucleophile. The HOMO of phenoxide (or of an aromatic amine) has its largest coefficient at the para carbon (also ortho). That carbon bonds to terminal N: a σ-complex (arenium ion) in which the O−/N lone pair stabilises the charge (quinoid resonance form). Why phenoxide, not phenol: the O− lone pair is much higher in energy and a far better donor; hence pH 9–10.
  • Step 3 – loss of H+. The σ-complex loses H+ from the sp3 carbon to restore aromaticity: the azo compound. Why para: the para carbon has the largest HOMO coefficient and attack there is less hindered; ortho is used when para is blocked.
+ 1–2. HOMO(phenoxide, para C) → LUMO(terminal N)⟶ 3. −H⁺, aromatise⟶
Azo coupling: attack through the para carbon gives a cyclohexadienone-type σ-complex; loss of H⁺ restores the aromatic ring

The pH window. Below pH ≈ 3 for amines (or below ≈ 8 for phenols) the nucleophile is largely un-ionised/protonated: too few donors. Above pH ≈ 10–11 the diazonium ion is attacked by OH− at the terminal N giving Ar–N=N–OH → Ar–N=N–O− (diazotate): no electrophile. Hence the compromise windows pH 9–10 (phenols) and 4–5 (amines). N-coupling: a 1° or 2° amine can attack through N (giving a triazene Ar–N=N–NR–Ar') – reversible; in acid the triazene returns to diazonium + amine and eventually the thermodynamically more stable C-coupled product (p-aminoazo compound) forms.
