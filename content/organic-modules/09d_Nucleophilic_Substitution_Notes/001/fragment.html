2. Structure and SN2 reactivity: steric effects

The transition state is crowded: five groups surround the carbon. Every alkyl group on the carbon (or on the adjacent carbon) obstructs the approach of the nucleophile and raises the energy of the transition state, so reactivity falls in the order methyl &gt; primary &gt; secondary &gt;&gt; tertiary. Tertiary halides essentially do not react by SN2; with strongly basic nucleophiles they undergo elimination instead.

Branching at the β-carbon also hinders the backside approach even though the reacting carbon is primary. Among primary bromides the SN2 rate falls along ethyl &gt; propyl &gt; isobutyl &gt;&gt; neopentyl (2,2-dimethylpropyl). In neopentyl bromide the three methyl groups of the t-butyl group sit directly in the path of the attacking nucleophile, so SN2 is extremely slow, several orders of magnitude slower than for ethyl bromide.

Methyl, ethyl, isopropyl, tert-butyl and neopentyl bromide: SN2 reactivity falls steeply along this series.

  • Methyl halides and primary halides: SN2 is the usual pathway with good nucleophiles.
  • Secondary halides: SN2 and SN1 compete; solvent and nucleophile decide (strong nucleophile in aprotic solvent favours SN2).
  • Tertiary halides: SN1 (and E1/E2); no SN2.
  • β-Branched primary halides (isobutyl, neopentyl): SN2 slowed by steric hindrance.
Trap. Primary does not automatically mean fast. Neopentyl halides are primary but practically inert to SN2, and they cannot ionise easily either (a primary cation would be needed) unless a concerted methyl shift occurs.
