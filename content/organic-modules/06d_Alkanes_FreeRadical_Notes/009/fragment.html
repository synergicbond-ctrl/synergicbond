10. Combustion, pyrolysis, isomerisation, aromatisation and other reactions

Combustion. Complete combustion is highly exothermic: CnH2n+2 + (3n+1)/2 O2 → n CO2 + (n+1) H2O. One mole of propane needs 5 mol O2, butane needs 6.5 mol. In limited oxygen carbon monoxide and soot (carbon) also form.

Pyrolysis (cracking). Heating alkanes at about 700–1000 K in the absence of air breaks C–C and C–H bonds homolytically, giving smaller alkanes, alkenes and hydrogen; for example C12H26 → C6H14 + C6H12. The chain mechanism involves alkyl radicals that undergo β-scission.

Isomerisation. Anhydrous AlCl3 with HCl at about 300 K converts a straight-chain alkane into its branched isomers via carbocations.

Reagent: anhyd. AlCl3/HCl

Aromatisation (reforming). Alkanes with six or more carbons in a chain are dehydrogenated and cyclised at about 770 K and 10–20 atm over Cr2O3/Al2O3 (or Pt/Al2O3). Four molecules of H2 are lost per molecule of benzene or toluene formed.

Reactant:

Reagent: Cr2O3/Al2O3, ~770 K

Product:

n-Hexane → benzene + 4 H2.

Vapour-phase nitration (HNO3, 670–720 K) is a free-radical process giving a mixture of nitroalkanes, with C–C cleavage; propane gives CH3NO2, C2H5NO2, 1-nitropropane and 2-nitropropane. Sulfochlorination (Reed reaction) uses SO2 + Cl2/hν to give alkanesulfonyl chlorides, R–SO2Cl. Oxidation with KMnO4 converts a tertiary C–H into C–OH.

Reactant:

Reagent: KMnO4 (dilute, neutral)

Product:

Only the tertiary hydrogen is oxidised easily.

Key idea. Many industrial alkane reactions share one theme: the C–H or C–C bond is broken homolytically at high temperature or on a metal surface, giving radicals; this is why the products are mixtures.
