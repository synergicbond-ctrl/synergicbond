18. Hydrogen bonding and the physical properties of organic compounds

18.1 The hydrogen bond

A hydrogen bond is an attractive interaction X–H···Y between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a strongly electronegative atom X (F, O, N) and a lone pair on another electronegative atom Y (F, O, N; weaker with Cl, S, and π-systems). It is mostly electrostatic: the small, nearly bare proton is attracted to the lone pair.

  • Strength: 10–40 kJ mol⁻¹, about one-tenth of a covalent bond but ten times larger than van der Waals attractions. Order of strength: F–H···F (≈ 29) > O–H···O (≈ 21) > N–H···N (≈ 13) in typical values; the bond is strongest when X–H···Y is linear.
  • Intermolecular hydrogen bonds link separate molecules (association): raises boiling point, melting point, viscosity, solubility in water. Intramolecular hydrogen bonds close a ring inside one molecule (chelation): lowers boiling point, raises volatility, lowers solubility in water.

18.2 Boiling points of organic compounds

Rules, in order of importance for similar molar masses: (1) hydrogen bonding, (2) dipole–dipole interaction, (3) molecular size (London dispersion forces increase with surface area and number of electrons), (4) branching (compact molecules have lower b.p.).

Illustration 18.3 Adv

Why is the boiling point of an alkane lower with branching?

Explain the b.p. order n-pentane (36 °C) > isopentane (28 °C) > neopentane (9.5 °C).

  1. The three molecules have the same molar mass and the same type of interaction (London dispersion). London forces depend on the contact surface between molecules: a long chain has a large surface; a spherical molecule has the smallest area of contact.
  2. Branching reduces the surface area, so the intermolecular attraction falls and the boiling point decreases: linear > monobranched > dibranched (spherical).

Illustration 18.4 Adv

Why does a primary alcohol boil higher than a tertiary isomer?

Explain the b.p. order butan-1-ol (118 °C) > butan-2-ol (99.5 °C) > 2-methylpropan-2-ol (82 °C).

  1. The same O–H···O hydrogen bonding is present in each, so the difference arises from molecular shape. The primary alcohol is an extended chain with a large surface area for dispersion forces; the tertiary alcohol is nearly spherical (smaller surface) and its OH group is shielded by three methyl groups, which hinders hydrogen bonding.

18.3 Melting points and crystal packing

The melting point depends on the strength of the intermolecular forces and on how well the molecules pack in the crystal lattice. Symmetric molecules pack well and therefore melt at a higher temperature than less symmetrical isomers of similar boiling point.

IMP Rules. (1) para isomers of disubstituted benzenes usually have the highest m.p. (the most symmetrical, best packed); meta is often the lowest. (2) trans isomers have higher m.p. than cis (more symmetrical, pack better) but lower dipole moment and usually lower b.p. than the cis isomer (cis-2-butene 3.7 °C vs trans 0.9 °C; cis-1,2-dichloroethene 60 °C vs trans 48 °C). (3) An intramolecular H-bond (ortho-nitrophenol, salicylaldehyde) gives a lower m.p. than the para isomer, whose H-bonds link the molecules. (4) The m.p. of even-carbon members of a homologous series is above the trend line (alternation): n-alkanes and dicarboxylic acids (oxalic 189, malonic 135, succinic 188, glutaric 98, adipic 153 °C): the even-carbon molecules pack better because their terminal groups lie on opposite sides.
Illustration 18.5 Main

Melting points of the isomers of dichlorobenzene

The m.p. of o-, m-, p-dichlorobenzene are −17, −25 and 53 °C. Explain the high m.p. of the para isomer even though it has the lowest dipole moment.

  1. The melting point depends on the crystal packing as well as on the intermolecular forces. The para isomer is the most symmetrical (a centre of symmetry): it packs into the crystal lattice most efficiently, so more energy is needed to break the lattice.
  2. The ortho and meta isomers are less symmetrical and pack poorly: lower m.p. (the dipole moment affects the boiling point more than the melting point).
Illustration 18.6 Adv

Maleic acid and fumaric acid

Maleic acid melts at 135 °C and fumaric acid at about 287 °C (sealed tube); maleic acid is far more soluble in water. Explain both facts.

  1. Fumaric acid (trans) is symmetrical (centrosymmetric) and its COOH groups are far apart, so each molecule forms intermolecular hydrogen bonds with neighbours in a tight, well-packed lattice: high m.p., low solubility.
  2. In maleic acid (cis) the two COOH groups are close and form an intramolecular hydrogen bond (in the monoanion; partly in the diacid), so it forms fewer intermolecular H-bonds. It is also more polar (dipole moment). It is therefore lower melting and more soluble in water.

18.4 Solubility

Like dissolves like. Polar and hydrogen-bonding compounds dissolve in polar solvents (water); non-polar compounds dissolve in non-polar solvents (hexane, benzene).

  • Alcohols: methanol, ethanol and propanols are miscible with water; butanol 7.7 g in 100 mL; pentanol about 2.2 g in 100 mL. As the non-polar alkyl chain grows, solubility drops. Branching raises solubility (t-butyl alcohol is miscible; n-butanol is not).
  • Carboxylic acids up to C₄ are miscible with water; from C₅ upward the solubility drops; benzoic acid is slightly soluble in cold water. Amines up to C₅ are soluble (H-bonds with water); aniline is slightly soluble (3.6 g in 100 mL).
  • Ethers are slightly soluble: diethyl ether about 7 g in 100 mL (the oxygen accepts H-bonds from water but cannot donate); THF and dioxane are miscible.
  • Ionic compounds and salts of organic acids/bases dissolve in water: RCOONa, RNH₃⁺Cl⁻ (the basis of extraction of acids with NaOH or NaHCO₃ and of amines with HCl).
  • Intramolecular H-bonding lowers solubility in water (o-nitrophenol is sparingly soluble, p-nitrophenol more soluble); intermolecular H-bonding with water raises it.

18.5 Carboxylic acid dimers and colligative properties

In the vapour phase and in non-polar solvents (benzene, CCl₄) carboxylic acids exist mainly as cyclic dimers held by two O–H···O hydrogen bonds. This explains the abnormally high boiling points (acetic acid 118 °C, b.p. > ethanol 78 °C), and the molar mass measured by freezing-point depression in benzene being twice the formula mass (for benzoic acid ≈ 244 g mol⁻¹ instead of 122; van't Hoff factor i = 0.5 for complete dimerisation). In water the acids hydrogen-bond to the solvent and are monomeric (or ionised).

Illustration 18.7 Adv

Molar mass of benzoic acid in benzene

A solution of 1.22 g of benzoic acid (formula mass 122) in 100 g of benzene lowers the freezing point by 0.28 K (K_f of benzene = 5.12 K kg mol⁻¹). Calculate the apparent molar mass and the degree of association to dimers.

  1. M(apparent) = K_f × w × 1000 / (ΔT_f × W) = 5.12 × 1.22 × 1000 / (0.28 × 100) = 223 g mol⁻¹ (about twice 122: the acid is dimerised).
  2. For 2A ⇌ A₂ with degree of association α, the number of particles per mole of acid is 1 − α/2, so i = 122/223 = 0.547.
  3. α = 2(1 − 0.547) = 0.91: about 91 % of the benzoic acid is present as dimers.

18.6 Water, ice and other consequences

  • Ice is less dense than water (0.917 vs 1.000 g cm⁻³ at 0 °C): in ice each oxygen is bonded to four H atoms (two covalent, two H-bonds) in an open tetrahedral cage; on melting some H-bonds break and the molecules fall into the holes. Water is densest at 4 °C.
  • H-bonding in biomolecules: the α-helix and β-sheet of proteins (C=O···H–N); the DNA double helix (A–T with two, G–C with three hydrogen bonds); cellulose.
  • High viscosity of glycerol, glycols, sulfuric acid.
  • Anomalously high heats of vaporisation of water, ammonia, HF.

Illustration 18.9 Adv

Explain the ordering of melting points and boiling points of nitrophenols

o-Nitrophenol m.p. 45 °C, m-nitrophenol 97 °C, p-nitrophenol 114 °C. Which is steam volatile?

  1. In the ortho isomer the OH and NO₂ are close, so one intramolecular hydrogen bond forms and no intermolecular association occurs; the molecules are held only by weaker forces: low m.p. and volatile in steam.
  2. In the meta and para isomers the OH can only bond to a different molecule; the para isomer is the more symmetrical and packs best (highest m.p.).
  3. o-Nitrophenol only is steam volatile; this is the basis of its separation from the para isomer in nitration of phenol.
Illustration 18.10 Main

Boiling point order for hydrogen halides

Why is HF the only hydrogen halide with an abnormally high boiling point?

  1. Fluorine is the most electronegative element and F is small, so the H–F bond is highly polar and the strong F–H···F hydrogen bonds link HF molecules into zigzag chains (and cyclic hexamers in the vapour). HCl, HBr and HI hardly form H-bonds; their b.p. rises with molar mass: HCl −85, HBr −67, HI −35 °C. HF boils at +20 °C.

Summary sheet: physical properties
