3. E1: mechanism, Zaitsev orientation, rearrangement, competition with SN1

The unimolecular elimination (E1) shares its first step with SN1: ionization of the substrate to a carbocation. In a second, fast step a weak base (often the solvent) removes a β-hydrogen. Because ionization is rate determining, the rate law is rate = k[R–X], independent of the base.

  1. Step 1 (slow): R–X → R+ + X−, assisted by a polar protic solvent.
  2. Step 2 (fast): the solvent or a weak base removes a β-proton from the carbocation, forming the alkene.
  3. Competing step 2′: the nucleophilic solvent attacks the carbocation, giving the SN1 substitution product.

Because the carbocation is planar, there is no anti-periplanar requirement. The more stable alkene (Zaitsev product, with more substituted C=C) predominates, and trans (E) isomers are favoured over cis (Z) for steric reasons. Carbocations can rearrange by 1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shifts toward a more stable cation, so E1 products often have a different carbon skeleton or double-bond position than the simple substrate would suggest.

Reactant:

Reagent: EtOH, heat (no strong base)

Product:

Ionization gives the secondary cation, a 1,2-hydride shift gives the tertiary cation, and loss of a proton gives 2-methylbut-2-ene as the major elimination product (ethyl ether from SN1 also forms).

Reactant:

Reagent: EtOH, heat

Product:

One substrate, three products: two alkenes (E1) and one ether (SN1).

Key idea. Elimination is favoured over substitution in E1/SN1 competition by higher temperature, because elimination has a higher entropy gain (more molecules formed). Strong base destroys the E1 pathway by switching the mechanism to E2.
Trap. A secondary substrate with a strong base goes E2 (no rearrangement). The same substrate in boiling ethanol with no added base may go E1/SN1 and can rearrange.
