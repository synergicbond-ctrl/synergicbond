5. Acidity of carboxylic acids: the full treatment

5.1 Definitions and numbers

For RCOOH + H2O ⇌ RCOO− + H3O+, Ka = [RCOO−][H3O+]/[RCOOH], pKa = −log Ka. Smaller pKa means stronger acid. Each unit of pKa is a factor of ten in Ka.

5.2 Why carboxylic acids are acidic: carboxylate versus alkoxide

Both ethanol and acetic acid lose H+ from an O–H bond. The difference of about 11 pKa units (16 against 4.76) has two causes:

  • Resonance in CH3COO−: the negative charge is spread over two oxygens. In CH3CH2O− it is localised on one oxygen and is further destabilised by the electron-releasing alkyl group.
  • Inductive effect of the C=O: the carbonyl carbon is electron-deficient and pulls electron density from the O–H bond, again stabilising the anion.

Phenol (pKa 10) lies in between because the phenoxide charge is delocalised into the ring, but onto carbon atoms (less electronegative than oxygen) and with loss of aromatic stabilisation in the canonical structures. This explains why carboxylic acids dissolve in NaHCO3 with effervescence but ordinary phenols do not.

5.3 Substituent effects on aliphatic acids

Electron-withdrawing groups (–I) pull electron density through the σ framework, spread the negative charge of the carboxylate and increase acidity; electron-releasing groups (+I) do the opposite.

Key idea Attenuation: α-chloro (2.86) ≫ β-chloro (4.08) > γ-chloro (4.52) > acetic (4.76). Additivity: each extra Cl on the same carbon adds roughly 1.4–1.5 pKa units of strength (2.86 → 1.29 → 0.65). Because the effect passes through bonds, it applies to every acid; it is the logic behind ordering any list of substituted acids.
Example 3 Main

Ordering

Arrange in order of increasing acid strength: CH3CH2CH2COOH, CH3CHClCH2COOH, ClCH2CH2CH2COOH, CH3CH2CHClCOOH.

Show solution
  1. All have the same carbon count. Strength depends on distance of Cl from COOH: α is strongest, β weaker, γ weaker still, none weakest.
  2. CH3CH2CH2COOH (no Cl) < ClCH2CH2CH2COOH (γ, pKa ≈ 4.5) < CH3CHClCH2COOH (β, ≈ 4.0) < CH3CH2CHClCOOH (α, ≈ 2.84).
  3. Answer: butanoic < 4-chlorobutanoic < 3-chlorobutanoic < 2-chlorobutanoic.

5.4 Alkyl groups: formic > acetic > propanoic ...

+I alkyl groups destabilise the carboxylate by pushing electrons to an already negative centre, so acidity falls from formic (3.75) to acetic (4.76) to propanoic (4.87), butanoic (4.82), pivalic (5.03). Beyond ethyl the change is small and not strictly monotonic in water because solvation of the anion also matters (larger alkyl groups hinder solvation of the carboxylate). In the gas phase the order reverses, since larger alkyl groups stabilise the anion by polarisability.

Trap JEE usually treats the order as HCOOH > CH3COOH > CH3CH2COOH > (CH3)2CHCOOH > (CH3)3CCOOH in water. Do not worry about the butanoic anomaly (4.82); it is not asked. Also remember formic acid is stronger than acetic acid but weaker than chloroacetic acid and much weaker than trichloroacetic acid.
<h4>5.5 Hybridisation of the attached carbon</h4>

The greater the s-character of the carbon bonded to COOH, the more electronegative that carbon, and the stronger the acid:

sp > sp2 > sp3 in electron-attracting ability. Propiolic acid is about as strong as a moderately strong organic acid (cf. chloroacetic 2.86).

5.6 Acidity of substituted benzoic acids

Benzoic acid (4.20) is stronger than acetic acid because the sp2 ring carbon is more electron-attracting than CH3, even though the phenyl ring is also a resonance donor in some contexts. The effect of a ring substituent depends on its position, combining –I/+I and –M/+M (mesomeric) effects:

Approximate pKa values at 25 °C in water.
  • para: –I weakest (distance), –M/+M acts fully. A strong +M (OCH3, OH, NH2) pushes electrons into the ring and onto the carboxylate side, weakening the acid (p-OCH3 4.47 > benzoic 4.20 in pKa). A strong –M (NO2) withdraws electrons and strengthens the acid. For halogens, –I and +M compete and the net effect at para is mildly acid-strengthening (3.98).
  • meta: the mesomeric effect cannot be transmitted to the carbon bearing COOH (it places charge on ortho/para positions only), so only the inductive effect operates. So m-OCH3 (4.09) and m-OH (4.08) are stronger acids than benzoic acid, and m-NO2 (3.45) is much stronger.
  • ortho: almost every ortho substituent, whatever its electronic nature, makes the acid stronger than benzoic acid (the ortho effect): the substituent twists the COOH out of the plane of the ring (steric inhibition of resonance), which removes conjugation of COOH with the ring. The ring no longer donates to the carbonyl, which makes the carboxylic acid more positive and the carboxylate better stabilised relative to the acid. Further, in some cases (o-OH, o-NO2, o-halogen) the proximity of the group adds H-bonding or field effects.
Key idea Ortho effect: o-CH3 benzoic acid (3.91) is stronger than benzoic (4.20), whereas p-CH3 (4.36) is weaker. Therefore: ortho-substituted benzoic acids are almost always stronger than benzoic acid, regardless of the group, and also stronger than the meta and para isomers.
Trap The classic ordering for nitrobenzoic acids is ortho (2.17) > meta (3.45) ≈ para (3.44) > benzoic (4.20). Do not claim that para is stronger than meta because “–M operates”: the para and meta values are nearly equal because the para –M effect is offset by a smaller –I at the greater distance. Questions usually give a clean answer such as ortho > para > meta only when the data are given.
Example 4 Main/Adv

Hammett-type ordering

Arrange in increasing order of acidic strength: benzoic acid (A), p-nitrobenzoic acid (B), p-methoxybenzoic acid (C), m-nitrobenzoic acid (D), o-nitrobenzoic acid (E).

Show solution
  1. o-NO2: ortho effect + –I: pKa 2.17, strongest.
  2. m-NO2 (3.45) and p-NO2 (3.44): both strongly –I/–M; nearly equal; both stronger than benzoic.
  3. Benzoic (4.20) is intermediate; p-OCH3 (4.47) is weakest because of +M.
  4. Increasing acid strength: C < A < D ≈ B < E, i.e. p-methoxy < benzoic < m-nitro ≤ p-nitro < o-nitro. If the question forces a choice between B and D, the para isomer is usually quoted as marginally stronger (pKa 3.44 vs 3.45).

5.7 Salicylic acid: an acid made strong by H-bonding

o-Hydroxybenzoic (salicylic) acid has pKa 2.97, much stronger than benzoic acid (4.20), p-hydroxybenzoic (4.58) and m-hydroxybenzoic (4.08). In the salicylate ion the carboxylate oxygen is held by an intramolecular hydrogen bond from the neighbouring OH, which strongly stabilises the anion; at the same time, there is no such stabilisation of the neutral acid. The same H-bond makes the second ionisation (phenolic OH) very hard, pKa2 ≈ 13.6.

Trap Intramolecular H-bonding raises the boiling point less than intermolecular bonding (salicylic acid is volatile with steam, p-hydroxybenzoic acid is not), but it strengthens the acidity of o-hydroxybenzoic acid by stabilising the conjugate base. Do not mix up these two statements.

<h4>5.9 pH and pK<sub>a</sub> calculations</h4>

For a weak monobasic acid of concentration c with degree of dissociation α: Ka = cα²/(1 − α) ≈ cα² when α ≪ 1, so [H+] = √(Kac) and pH = ½(pKa − log c).

Example 5 Main

Acid strength from pH

A 0.1 M solution of an organic acid has pH 2.88. Find Ka, pKa and α, and identify the acid.

Show solution
  1. [H+] = 10−2.88 = 1.32 × 10−3 M; α = 1.32 × 10−3/0.1 = 1.32 %.
  2. Ka = [H+]²/(c − [H+]) = (1.32 × 10−3)²/(0.0987) = 1.77 × 10−5; pKa = 4.75.
  3. Matches acetic acid (pKa 4.76): Ka = 1.8 × 10−5.
Example 6 Main/Adv

Which is stronger?

For 0.01 M solutions, compare α for chloroacetic acid (Ka = 1.4 × 10−3) and formic acid (Ka = 1.8 × 10−4).

Show solution
  1. Formic: solve x²/(0.01 − x) = 1.8 × 10−4: x² + 1.8 × 10−4x − 1.8 × 10−6 = 0, x = 1.25 × 10−3, α = 12.5 %.
  2. Chloroacetic: x² + 1.4 × 10−3x − 1.4 × 10−5 = 0, x = 3.1 × 10−3, α = 31 %.
  3. Note that √(Kac) would overestimate α for chloroacetic acid (3.7 × 10−3 → 37 %); the approximation α ≪ 1 fails above about 5 %, so use the quadratic formula.
