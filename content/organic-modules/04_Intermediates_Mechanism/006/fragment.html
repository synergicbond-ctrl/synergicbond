5. Carbocations

5.1 Structure and bonding

A carbocation is a species in which a carbon atom carries a positive charge and has only three bonds and six valence electrons. The carbon is sp2 hybridised and trigonal planar, with the three σ bonds at 120° and an empty p orbital perpendicular to the plane. That empty orbital is the LUMO. It explains why carbocations are strong electrophiles and why a planar cation can be attacked from either face.

Methyl cation: trigonal planar, sp², empty p orbital (the LUMO)

Carbocations were characterised directly by dissolving alcohols and alkyl halides in very strongly acidic, non-nucleophilic media ("superacids" such as FSO3H–SbF5–SO2, "magic acid") and recording NMR spectra. Tertiary alcohols ionise to the cation under these conditions; primary and secondary alcohols are first protonated and ionise only at higher temperature, and then with rearrangement. Because the medium has almost no nucleophile, the cations live long enough to undergo repeated rearrangement and accumulate as the most stable isomer that the carbon skeleton allows (for example, the tert-butyl cation from butan-2-ol, isobutanol and butan-1-ol). Cations with strong stabilisation can be handled as salts. The triarylmethyl cations are stable enough in acid for their equilibrium with the corresponding carbinol to be measured (section 5.4), and the tropylium ion forms salts that can be isolated and stored for months.

MOT Lens The carbocation carbon uses three sp2 hybrids for the σ framework and leaves one p orbital empty. That empty p orbital is the LUMO of the cation and its energy decides reactivity: anything that mixes filled orbitals into it (hyperconjugation, π conjugation, lone pairs) lowers the energy of the filled combination and raises the energy of the LUMO, making the cation less electrophilic and more stable.

5.4 Stability of carbocations

A carbocation is electron deficient, so substituents that donate electron density into the empty p orbital stabilise it and substituents that withdraw electron density destabilise it. Two kinds of measurement quantify the effect, and both are used below.

  • Gas-phase hydride affinity is the energy released in R+ + H− → R–H. It measures the intrinsic stability of the cation without solvent. The smaller the hydride affinity, the more stable the cation.
  • pKR+ applies to cations stable enough to be in equilibrium with the carbinol in acid: R+ + H2O ⇌ ROH + H+. It is the pH at which cation and carbinol are present in equal concentrations. The larger the pKR+, the more stable the cation.

These data lead to the following ordering rules, each stated with the evidence that supports it.

(a) Alkyl substitution: hyperconjugation and polarisability

The hydride affinities fall steadily from methyl (314) through ethyl (274) and isopropyl (247) to tert-butyl (230). The same order is seen in solution by solvolysis rates and by the enthalpy of ionisation of chlorides in a non-nucleophilic solvent. The gap between tertiary and secondary cations is about 17 kcal mol−1 in the gas phase and about 9.5 kcal mol−1 in solution, because solvent partly levels the differences. Larger ions are also more stable than smaller ones of the same class (tert-pentyl is more stable than tert-butyl in the gas phase) because the charge is spread over more atoms. Two effects operate. Alkyl groups are weakly electron-releasing towards an sp2 carbon (inductive effect), and they are polarisable. Most important is hyperconjugation, the interaction of a filled σ(C–H) or σ(C–C) orbital on an adjacent carbon with the empty p orbital.

Hyperconjugation: the filled C–H σ orbital donates into the empty p orbital (no-bond resonance)

In MO terms the filled σ orbital and the empty p orbital mix to give a lower-energy bonding combination holding two electrons and a higher-energy empty combination. The two electrons are therefore stabilised, and some electron density is moved onto the cationic carbon. The interaction is strongest when the C–H bond lies parallel to the p orbital, so it is a conformation-dependent effect. The more α-substituents carrying such bonds, the greater the stabilisation. The number of C–H bonds on α-carbons is a convenient measure: CH3+ 0, CH3CH2+ 3, (CH3)2CH+ 6, (CH3)3C+ 9. C–C bonds are also effective donors, and calculations on the 2-methyl-2-butyl cation find little energy difference between conformations in which a C–H or a C–CH3 bond is aligned with the empty orbital.

(b) Conjugation with a π system

An adjacent C=C or aromatic ring shares the positive charge through overlap of the empty p orbital with the π orbitals, as the Hückel treatment in section 4.6 shows. In the allyl cation the charge resides on the two terminal carbons, and rotation about the C–C bonds is hindered because the delocalisation requires planarity (calculated: the perpendicular allyl cation is about 38 kcal mol−1 less stable than the planar one). In the benzyl cation the positive charge is shared by the benzylic carbon and the ortho and para ring carbons.

Benzyl cation: positive charge at the benzylic carbon and at the two ortho positions and the para position; the meta positions carry none

Conjugation helps a great deal, but the stabilisation depends on how much the unconjugated reference is already stabilised. The gas-phase data show that the unsubstituted primary allyl cation (256) is *less* stable than isopropyl (247), whereas benzyl (233) lies between isopropyl and tert-butyl (230). Substitution of the allyl terminus by methyl groups lowers the hydride affinity to 237 (one methyl) and 225 (two methyls); a secondary or tertiary allylic cation is therefore more stable than the corresponding saturated cation. Each additional phenyl group stabilises further: in solution the pKR+ increases from benzyl (−20) to diphenylmethyl (−13.3) to triphenylmethyl (−6.63), and a methyl group on the benzylic carbon raises it by about 8 units (cumyl, −12.3). Substituents on the ring act through resonance and induction. Electron-donating groups in the ortho or para position stabilise the cation (4-methoxy raises the pKR+ of triphenylmethyl from −6.63 to −3.40, and three methoxy groups to +0.82; three dimethylamino groups raise it to +9.36), and electron-withdrawing groups destabilise it (three nitro groups lower it to −16.27).

(c) Lone pairs on the carbon attached to the cationic carbon

Oxygen, nitrogen and sulfur lone pairs donate into the empty p orbital and give a contributor in which every atom has an octet (an oxocarbenium or iminium ion).

↔⟶
Methoxymethyl cation: all atoms have an octet in the right-hand contributor

The C–O bond in such ions has double-bond character: restricted rotation about it has been measured (a free-energy barrier of about 14 kcal mol−1 in one case). Even though oxygen is electronegative, donation outweighs the inductive withdrawal. Fluorine and chlorine on the cationic carbon also stabilise by lone-pair donation; in the gas phase they are about as effective as a methyl group. Acetals and glycosides hydrolyse readily in acid because oxocarbenium ions are easy to form.

(d) Cyclopropyl groups

A cyclopropane ring attached to the cationic carbon stabilises the cation strongly, because its bent C–C bonds are higher in energy than ordinary σ bonds and overlap well with the empty p orbital, but only in the bisected conformation, in which the plane of the ring bisects the plane of the cationic carbon. The tricyclopropylmethyl cation (pKR+ −2.3) is *more* stable than the triphenylmethyl cation (−6.63).

(e) Aromatic and antiaromatic cations

Cyclic, planar, fully conjugated cations obey Hückel's rule. The tropylium ion (6 π electrons) has the lowest hydride affinity in the table (200) and pKR+ +4.7, so it is stable in water at pH below about 5; the cyclopropenyl cation (2 π electrons) is also aromatic, and substituted cyclopropenyl cations are very stable (trimethylcyclopropenyl +7.8, tricyclopropylcyclopropenyl +9.7). The cyclopentadienyl cation (4 π electrons) is antiaromatic: its hydride affinity (258) is far above that of tropylium or cyclopropenyl and similar to that of the primary allyl cation. The Hückel comparison of ring and open chain in section 4.6 gives the same classification (ring more stable than chain for 2 and 6 π electrons, less stable for 4). Aromaticity means that the ring cation is more stable than the open-chain cation, not that it is as stable as benzene.

(f) Hybridisation of the cationic carbon

An empty orbital with high s character is held close to the nucleus, which is unfavourable. The data show vinyl (287) and phenyl (298) cations lying between ethyl (274) and methyl (314) in stability, so both are less stable than a primary alkyl cation but more stable than the methyl cation in the gas phase. In the vinyl cation the empty orbital is a p orbital perpendicular to the π bond; in the phenyl cation it is an sp2 orbital in the ring plane, with no overlap with the π system. In solution aryl and vinyl halides do not ionise by SN1 under ordinary conditions; vinyl cations form only from very good leaving groups (triflates) and when substituents help, and aryl cations from diazonium ions.

(g) Geometry: bridgehead cations

A carbocation wants to be planar, and a bridgehead carbon in a small bicyclic skeleton cannot flatten. Relative rates of solvolysis of bridgehead bromides in 80 % ethanol at 25 °C are 1-bromoadamantane 1, 1-bromobicyclo[2.2.2]octane 10−3 and 1-bromobicyclo[2.2.1]heptane 10−10, and the rates correlate with the strain introduced on forming the cation. 1-Chloroapocamphane, a very rigid bridgehead chloride, was recovered unchanged after 48 h in refluxing ethanolic silver nitrate, and SN2 is impossible at a bridgehead because backside attack is blocked.

1-norbornyl cation (bridgehead) 1-adamantyl cation
Bridgehead cations cannot become planar; solvolysis rates fall as the strain on ionisation rises
(h) Electron-withdrawing groups

Groups attached directly to the cationic carbon destabilise it: in the solvolysis of 2-substituted-2-propyl derivatives CF3 slows ionisation by a large factor, and CN and CHO by less than their inductive effects alone would predict because they can donate through their π systems. An α-carbonyl or α-cyano cation is therefore a poor intermediate, and SN1 at the α-carbon of ketones, esters and nitriles is very slow.

Compare & Distinguish Gas-phase hydride affinity measures the intrinsic stability of the cation; it is not affected by solvent; smaller means more stable. pKR+ measures stability in solution relative to the carbinol and applies only to cations stable enough to be observed in equilibrium with it; larger means more stable. Do not mix the two scales, and do not rank a cation measured in one scale against one measured in the other.
Exception & Its Reason Conjugation does not always beat alkyl substitution: the primary allyl cation (hydride affinity 256) is less stable than isopropyl (247), and primary benzyl (233) is slightly less stable than tert-butyl (230). Reason: conjugation delocalises the charge, but the saturated tertiary and secondary cations also have large hyperconjugative and polarisability stabilisation; the allyl cation has only two terminal carbons sharing the charge and no alkyl donors.
Exception & Its Reason The vinyl (287) and phenyl (298) cations are more stable than the methyl cation (314) although they are poor cations in solution. Reason: the methyl cation has no carbon substituent to donate electron density, whereas the vinyl and phenyl cations carry a substituent carbon (the C=C or ring σ framework) that donates by polarisation; the empty orbital is poorly matched with the π system, but even this weak donation outweighs the complete absence of donors in CH3+.
Counterexample "A more substituted cation is always more stable." The 1-norbornyl cation is tertiary but cannot become planar; 1-bromobicyclo[2.2.1]heptane solvolyses about 1010 times more slowly than 1-bromoadamantane. Substitution helps only when the cation can adopt the planar sp2 geometry.
MOT Lens Aromatic cations. Cyclic delocalisation lowers the π energy when the electron count is 4n + 2: for the three-membered ring (2 electrons) the lowest orbital α + 2β holds both electrons, and the remaining orbitals are empty. 5.5 Reactions of carbocations: the main fates

Once formed, a carbocation has few options, and the conditions decide which one is taken.

  • 1. Capture by a nucleophile (water, alcohol, halide ion, carboxylate): SN1 solvolysis, acid-catalysed hydration, hydrohalogenation, halohydrin formation.
  • 2. Loss of a proton from an adjacent carbon to give an alkene: E1 and alcohol dehydration, which compete with SN1 and are favoured by heat and by a weakly nucleophilic conjugate base.
  • 3. Rearrangement to a more stable cation by a 1,2-shift of hydride, alkyl or aryl, including ring expansion and contraction.
  • 4. Addition to a π system: to an alkene (cationic polymerisation) or an arene (Friedel–Crafts alkylation).
  • 5. Hydride abstraction from another molecule.
  • 6. Fragmentation, mainly in the gas phase and in superacid.
The six fates of a carbocation
Experimental Connection Superacid NMR. Dissolving alcohols or halides in FSO3H–SbF5 at low temperature gives long-lived cations whose NMR spectra show only the most stable isomer (tert-butyl from all four butyl alcohols; tert-pentyl from the C5 alcohols including neopentyl alcohol). This shows that rearrangement is thermodynamically driven and is limited in ordinary solvents only by the fast capture of the cation by the solvent.

5.6 Carbocation rearrangements

5.6.1 Principles

In a 1,2-shift a hydrogen atom, alkyl group or aryl group moves with its bonding electron pair from a carbon to the adjacent cationic carbon; the carbon it leaves becomes the new cation. The driving force is a thermodynamic one: the product cation is more stable. The barriers for shifts that lead to a more stable ion are low. For the 2-butyl cation, hydride exchange between C-2 and C-3 has a barrier of at most about 2.5 kcal mol−1, and scrambling of the methyl and ethyl carbons occurs with an activation energy of about 7 to 8 kcal mol−1. By contrast the rearrangement of 2-butyl to tert-butyl is much slower (about 18 kcal mol−1) because it must pass through a primary cation; calculations place primary cations about 20 and 35 kcal mol−1 above secondary and tertiary cations respectively. A tertiary-to-secondary shift costs about 10 kcal mol−1 and can still occur if it leads on to a more stable ion.

Capture by a nucleophile also has a very low barrier, so extensive rearrangement is seen only when the cation lives long enough: in superacid, in solvents of low nucleophilicity (trifluoroacetic acid) or with very fast shifts. Solvolysis of 2-butyl tosylate in acetic acid shows only about 9 % hydride shift, but in trifluoroacetic acid the shift approaches the statistical 50 %. Solvolysis of 3-methyl-2-butyl tosylate in trifluoroacetic acid gives 98.5 % of products from the rearranged 2-methyl-2-butyl cation. Calculations indicate that many shifts proceed through bridged structures in which the migrating atom or group is partly bonded to both carbons; for hydride shifts the bridged ion is at or near the transition state and, in some cases, a shallow intermediate.

Exam Trap A rearrangement needs a carbocation. A hydride or alkyl shift does not occur in SN2 reactions, in E2 eliminations, in hydroboration, in oxymercuration (the mercurinium ion does not rearrange), in radical reactions, or in the reactions of carbanions. If the mechanism involves no cation, write the product with the unrearranged skeleton.

Benzene is treated with 1-chloropropane and AlCl3. Which product predominates, and which sequence gives n-propylbenzene instead?

Why It Happens Shifts are fast because they pass through a bridged, three-centre two-electron transition state of low energy; hydride exchange in the 2-butyl cation has a barrier of at most 2.5 kcal mol−1. A shift that requires a primary cation is slow (2-butyl to tert-butyl, about 18 kcal mol−1) because the primary cation lies about 20 kcal mol−1 above the secondary cation.
Exception & Its Reason Tertiary to secondary shifts do occur (about 10 kcal mol−1 uphill) when they lead on to a more stable ion, as in the interconversion of the five methyl groups of the 2,3,3-trimethylbutyl cation (barrier below 5 kcal mol−1); the thermodynamic rule applies to the end point, not to each step.

5.6.2 The pinacol rearrangement

A vicinal diol rearranges in acid to a ketone with loss of water. The conversion of pinacol into pinacolone is the standard example.

Pinacol rearrangement: pinacol → tertiary cation → 1,2-methyl shift → protonated ketone → pinacolone
  • The oxygen of one hydroxyl group is protonated; loss of water gives a tertiary cation next to the C–OH carbon.
  • A methyl group migrates from the carbinol carbon to the cationic carbon (a 1,2-shift) with its bonding pair of electrons.
  • The cation now on the former carbinol carbon is an O-protonated ketone (an oxocarbenium ion), in which every atom has an octet. This is the thermodynamic driving force.
  • Loss of H+ from oxygen gives the ketone pinacolone, 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-one.

With an unsymmetrical diol the hydroxyl group that leaves is the one that gives the more stable cation (tertiary or benzylic in preference to secondary), and the migration that follows is then decided by the groups available. The reaction is also the basis of several ring-expansion methods: cyclic diols and 2-amino-alcohols, the latter with nitrous acid (the Tiffeneau–Demjanov reaction, which converts 1-(aminomethyl)cyclohexanol into cycloheptanone).

5.6.3 Other rearrangements that involve cations
  • Dienone–phenol rearrangement. 4,4-Dimethylcyclohexa-2,5-dienone in acid gives 3,4-dimethylphenol: the protonated dienone is an oxocarbenium ion with charge at C-3 and C-5, a methyl group moves from C-4 to C-3, and loss of H+ aromatises the ring.
  • Aliphatic diazotisation. A primary aliphatic amine with nitrous acid forms an unstable diazonium ion that loses N2 to give a cation. A mixture of alcohols (including rearranged ones), alkenes and halides results.
  • Beckmann rearrangement. The group anti to the leaving group on nitrogen migrates to the electron-poor nitrogen as water leaves, giving a nitrilium ion that is hydrolysed to an amide.

5.7 Non-classical carbocations

Some cations are better described with a three-centre two-electron bond than with a single positively charged carbon. The 2-norbornyl cation is the classic case. Evidence for σ-bridging includes the following. The acetolysis of exo-2-norbornyl brosylate is faster than that of the endo isomer by a factor of 350, and both give exclusively exo-acetate. Optically active exo-brosylate gives completely racemic exo-acetate. A symmetrical (achiral) bridged intermediate accounts for this, with participation of the C1–C6 bonding electrons possible only in the exo isomer where the C1–C6 bond is anti to the leaving group. In solution under stable-ion conditions the 13C NMR signals of the cation are at high field, not near the position expected for a classical secondary cation carbon, which excludes a rapidly equilibrating pair of classical ions, and the stabilisation provided by σ-bridging has been estimated at about 6 to 11 kcal mol−1, with high-level calculations favouring the bridged structure by a larger amount. In the crystal structures of two substituted norbornyl cations the C1–C6 bond is lengthened to 1.74 Å and the C2–C6 distance is shortened from 2.5 Å to 2.09 Å, which corresponds to a partly bridged structure.

bicyclo[2.2.1]heptane (norbornane) classical 2-norbornyl cation
In the bridged ion the C1–C6 σ bond donates into the empty orbital at C2

Two related manifolds should be recognised: the interconverting cyclopropylcarbinyl, cyclobutyl and homoallyl cations, from which solvolysis gives mixtures of the three alcohols, and the phenonium ion, a bridged three-membered ring involving the ipso carbon of a β-aryl group, whose formation leads to retention of configuration and explains aryl participation in solvolysis (the proportion of aryl-assisted reaction rises with electron-donating substituents on the ring and with decreasing nucleophilicity of the solvent).

MOT Lens σ-participation. In the exo-2-norbornyl system the C1–C6 σ bond lies anti to the leaving group. Its filled σ orbital overlaps with the back lobe of the C2–X σ* orbital (the same stereoelectronic requirement as in SN2 and E2), so electrons flow from the σ bond into the developing empty orbital at C2 as X leaves. The result is a three-centre two-electron bond, a σ-delocalised (non-classical) cation. The endo isomer has no anti σ bond and ionises without this help, which gives the exo : endo rate ratio of 350.

5.8 Stereochemistry

  • A free planar cation is attacked equally from both faces, so complete ionisation of a chiral substrate gives racemic products.
  • In practice the departing group, as part of an ion pair, partly shields one face for a short time. Many SN1 reactions therefore give some net inversion (more inversion than retention) rather than a perfectly racemic mixture.
  • A bridging neighbouring group (halonium, phenonium, acyloxonium) gives retention through two successive inversions.
  • The carbon that becomes cationic loses its stereochemical information, whereas the migrating group moves with retention of its own configuration.
