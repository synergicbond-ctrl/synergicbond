Chapter 1: Fundamentals, Homopolymers & Copolymers

  • Polymer (Greek: poly = many, mer = unit/part): High molecular mass macromolecule (103 – 107 u) built by covalent linking of a large number of simple, reactive repeating structural units.
  • Monomer: Simple reactive molecule that combines repeatedly to form a polymer. Polymerisation is the chemical process joining monomers into a polymer chain.
  • Terminal End-Groups: Starting from n molecules of monomer M, linear linking forms x–M–(M)n−2–M–y; the terminal bonds M–x and M–y depend on the initiation and termination mode.
  • Functionality of a Monomer: Number of bonding sites (reactive functional groups or multiple bonds) available per monomer molecule. Bifunctional monomers form linear chains; trifunctional / polyfunctional monomers generate branched or 3D cross-linked networks.
  • Four Backbone Industries: Plastics, Elastomers, Fibres, and Paints & Varnishes.

1.1 Homopolymers vs. Copolymers

  • Homopolymer: Formed from only one type of monomeric species (e.g., Polythene, PVC, Teflon, Nylon-6, Polystyrene, Orlon, PMMA, PVA, Starch, Cellulose, Glycogen, Dextrin, Inulin, Natural rubber, Neoprene).
  • Copolymer: Formed by polymerisation of two or more different monomeric species (via either chain-growth or step-growth). Examples: Buna-S, Buna-N, Nylon-6,6, Terylene, Bakelite, Glyptal, Saran, SAN, ABS, Butyl rubber, PHBV, Nylon-2–Nylon-6.

Chapter 2: Five-Way Classification of Polymers

2.1 Based on Origin

  • 1. Natural Polymers: Obtained from plants and animals — Polysaccharides (starch, cellulose), Proteins (α-L-amino acids), Nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), Silk, Natural rubber (cis-polyisoprene), Gutta-percha (trans-polyisoprene). Natural polymers participating in metabolic processes are called Biopolymers.
  • 2. Semi-Synthetic Polymers: Chemically modified natural polymers (mostly from cellulose) — Cellulose acetate (Rayon), Cellulose nitrate (gun cotton), Cellulose xanthate, Vulcanised rubber.
  • 3. Synthetic Polymers: Man-made polymers — Polythene, PVC, Polystyrene, Teflon, Nylon-6, Nylon-6,6, Nylon-6,10, Terylene, Bakelite, Buna-S, Buna-N, Neoprene.

2.2 Based on Structure (Shape)

  • Linear: Close-packed, high density, high m.p., high tensile strength; all fibres are linear (e.g., HDPE, PVC, Nylon, Terylene, Cellulose, Silk).
  • Branched: Irregular packing → lower density, lower m.p., lower tensile strength (e.g., LDPE, Amylopectin, Glycogen).
  • Cross-Linked (3D Network): Formed from bi- and tri-functional monomers via strong covalent cross-links → hard, rigid, brittle, infusible; may originate from either condensation or addition polymer chemistry (e.g., Bakelite, Melamine, Urea–formaldehyde resin).

2.3 Based on Mode of Polymerisation & Growth Mechanism

FeatureAddition (Chain-Growth) PolymersCondensation (Step-Growth) Polymers
Monomer RequirementUnsaturated molecules (double / triple bonds).At least two functional groups (bi- or tri-functional).
Atom RetentionRetain all atoms of the monomer (no by-product).Elimination of small molecules (H2O, ROH, HCl, NH3).

2.4 Based on Intermolecular (Secondary) Forces

  • 1. Elastomers: Weakest van der Waals forces; randomly coiled amorphous chains with a few cross-links so chains retract after stretching up to 10× length. Rule of thumb: addition polymers of butadiene & derivatives (Natural/Vulcanised rubber, Buna-S, Buna-N, Neoprene).
  • 2. Fibres: Strongest intermolecular forces (H-bonding in Nylons or dipole–dipole in Terylene); close-packed crystalline nature, high tensile strength, high modulus, least elasticity (Nylon-6,6, Nylon-6, Terylene, Cellulose, Silk, Wool, Orlon).
  • 3. Thermoplastic Polymers: Forces intermediate between elastomers and fibres in this textbook classification; usually linear or branched and without permanent network cross-links. Soften on heating, harden on cooling reversibly; can be remoulded repeatedly (Polythene, Polypropene, Polystyrene, PVC, Teflon, PMMA).
  • 4. Thermosetting Polymers: Heavily branched / extensively cross-linked 3D network; harden irreversibly on heating into an infusible solid; cannot be remelted or reused (Bakelite, Urea–formaldehyde resin, Melamine–formaldehyde resin).
Increasing Order of Intermolecular Forces
Elastomers (weakest: Buna-S, Neoprene, Natural rubber) < Thermoplastics (Polythene, PVC, Polystyrene) < Fibres (strong secondary attractions: Nylon-6,6, Terylene). Thermosets are separately distinguished by permanent covalent network links, not simply ranked as having stronger intermolecular forces.

Chapter 3: Addition (Chain-Growth) Mechanisms & Tacticity

3.1 Free-Radical Polymerisation

  • Initiators: Benzoyl peroxide (C6H5COO)2, Acetyl peroxide, tert-Butyl peroxide, Potassium persulphate (K2S2O8), AIBN (azobisisobutyronitrile).
  • Intermediate: Free radical; favoured by monomers forming resonance-stabilised radicals (Styrene, Vinyl chloride, Vinyl acetate, Acrylonitrile, Methyl methacrylate, 1,3-Dienes).
  • Chain Transfer Agents (CCl4, CBr4): React with the growing polymer radical to cap the chain and start a new radical (•CCl3), lowering average molecular mass.
  • Inhibitors (Amines, Phenols, Quinones): Form highly unreactive radicals that arrest/stop polymerisation — hence purest monomer must be used.

Styrene is the classic monomer that undergoes Free-Radical, Cationic, and Anionic polymerisation with almost equal ease because its phenyl group can stabilise benzylic radical, cationic, and anionic chain ends; this does not imply equal reaction rates!

3.3 Stereoregularity (Tacticity) in Vinyl Polymers

  • Isotactic: All pendant groups (–R) lie on the same side of the zig-zag carbon backbone → crystalline, high m.p., tough (formed by Ziegler–Natta).
  • Syndiotactic: Pendant groups alternate regularly on opposite sides of the backbone → crystalline and strong.
  • Atactic: Pendant groups arranged randomly → amorphous, soft, rubbery.
  • No Tacticity Possible: Polymers with two identical substituents on every backbone carbon — Polythene (–CH2–CH2–), Teflon (–CF2–CF2–), and Polyvinylidene chloride (–CH2–CCl2–) — cannot exist as isotactic/syndiotactic stereoisomers.

4.1 Plasticisers

  • Definition: High-boiling organic compounds dissolved into stiff plastics (like PVC) that slip between polymer chains, weaken intermolecular attractions, and allow chains to slide past one another → makes plastic soft and workable.

Chapter 5: Diene Polymerisation, Natural Rubber, Vulcanisation & Synthetic Rubbers

5.1 1,4- vs. 1,2-Polymerisation of Conjugated Dienes

  • 1,4-Polymerisation (at C1 and C4 of 1,3-butadiene): Yields unbranched chain with an internal C2=C3 double bond per unit → exists as cis-1,4 or trans-1,4 geometric isomers.
  • 1,2-Polymerisation (at C1 and C2): Yields branched polymer (polyvinyl polyethene) with pendant vinyl (–CH=CH2) side groups.

5.2 Natural Rubber vs. Gutta-Percha & Vulcanisation

  • Natural Rubber: Obtained as milky colloidal latex from bark of Hevea brasiliensis; coagulated with acetic/formic acid. Average chain has ~5000 isoprene units (2-methyl-1,3-butadiene) linked head-to-tail in an all-cis-1,4-polyisoprene coiled spring structure.
  • Limitations of Raw Rubber: Soft & sticky at high temp (> 335 K), brittle at low temp (< 283 K), high water absorption, soluble in non-polar solvents, attacked by oxidising agents.
  • Gutta-Percha: Naturally occurring all-trans-1,4-polyisoprene isomer — harder, more brittle, non-elastic; used by dentists for root canal fillings.
  • Vulcanisation (Charles Goodyear, 1839): Heating raw rubber with sulphur + additives at 373 K – 415 K (~150°C) introduces sulphur cross-links (–S–, –S–S–) at reactive double-bond & allylic sites → stiffens rubber and locks elastic recovery.
  • Sulphur Percentage Scale: 5% S → Automobile tyres; 20–25% S → Ebonite (hard rubber); ~30% S → Battery cases.
  • Accelerators & Fillers: Accelerators (Diphenyl guanidine, Tetramethylthiuram disulphide) speed vulcanisation; Fillers (Carbon black, ZnO) improve wear resistance.

5.3 Synthetic Rubbers (Homopolymers & Copolymers)

  • Neoprene: Superior resistance to vegetable/mineral oils, petrol, sunlight, ozone & non-inflammable → conveyor belts, gaskets, hoses, wire insulation.
  • Buna-S (Bu = Butadiene, na = Sodium, S = Styrene): Compounded with carbon black & vulcanised → autotyres, floor tiles, footwear components, cable insulation.
  • Buna-N: Resistant to petrol, lubricating oil & organic solvents → oil seals, fuel tank linings, hoses.
  • Cold Rubber: Butadiene + styrene polymerised at −18°C to 5°C with a redox system → higher tensile strength & abrasion resistance than standard SBR.

Chapter 6: Polyamides (Nylons & High-Strength Aramids)

  • Naming Rule: Suffix numbers indicate the number of carbon atoms in each monomer unit (e.g., Nylon-6,6 has 6C diamine + 6C diacid; Nylon-6 has one 6C monomer).
  • Nylon-6,6: Hexamethylenediamine + Adipic acid at 553 K, high pressure (−H2O). Strong inter-chain H-bonding → sheets, bristles for brushes, textiles.
  • Nylon-6,10: Hexamethylenediamine (C6) + Sebacic acid (C10) → cords, ropes, carpets, garments.
  • Nylon-6 (Perlon L): Heating Caprolactam with H2O at 533–543 K (hydrolysis opens lactam ring to ε-aminocaproic acid, which self-condenses). Caprolactam is made via Beckmann rearrangement of cyclohexanone oxime → tyre cords, fabrics, ropes.
  • Kevlar (Aramid): Terephthalic acid + p-phenylenediamine; tensile strength greater than steel → bulletproof vests, army helmets.
  • Nomex (Aramid): Isophthalic acid + m-phenylenediamine → protective clothing for fire-fighters.

Chapter 7: Polyesters & Polycarbonates

  • Terylene / Dacron / Mylar (PET): Ethylene glycol + Terephthalic acid (benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid) at 420–460 K with zinc acetate + Sb2O3 catalyst. Polar ester groups create strong dipole–dipole forces → crease-resistant, low moisture absorption; used in wash-and-wear garments, blending with cotton/wool, glass-reinforcing safety helmets.
  • Glyptal (Alkyd Resin): Ethylene glycol + Phthalic acid (benzene-1,2-dicarboxylic acid) → paints and lacquers.
  • Kodel Polyester: Terephthalic acid + 1,4-cyclohexanediol.
  • Lexan (Polycarbonate): Phosgene (COCl2) + Bisphenol A in pyridine (−HCl); carbonyl linked to two aryloxy groups. Strong & transparent → bulletproof windows, traffic-light lenses, compact discs, two-wheeler safety helmets.

Chapter 8: Formaldehyde Resins (Thermosetting 3D Networks)

  • Phenol–Formaldehyde (Oldest Synthetic Polymer): Initial step is electrophilic aromatic substitution of phenol with HCHO (H+ or OH−) forming o- and p-hydroxymethylphenols.
  • Novolac: Linear polymer formed by condensation of o-hydroxymethylphenol (methylene –CH2– bridges) → used in paints.
  • Bakelite: Heating Novolac with excess HCHO introduces extensive 3D cross-linking → infusible thermoset used in combs, phonograph records, electrical switches, utensil handles, computer discs.
  • Melamine–Formaldehyde Resin (Melmac): Condensation of Melamine (2,4,6-triamino-1,3,5-triazine: 6 N atoms, 6 lone pairs — 3 localised on ring sp2 N + 3 delocalised on –NH2) with HCHO → unbreakable crockery.
  • Urea–Formaldehyde Resin: Condensation of Urea + HCHO, repeat unit [–NH–CO–NH–CH2–]n → unbreakable cups, laminated sheets, wood laminates.

Chapter 9: Biodegradable Polymers

  • Why Addition Plastics Pollute: Carbon–carbon (C–C) single bonds in addition polymers are inert to microbial enzyme-catalysed hydrolysis.
  • Design Strategy: Insert hydrolysable functional groups (ester or amide linkages resembling biopolymers) into the backbone so soil bacteria/enzymes degrade them cleanly.
  • 1. PHBV (Poly β-hydroxybutyrate-co-β-hydroxyvalerate): Aliphatic polyester copolymer of 3-hydroxybutanoic acid (imparts stiffness; 1 chiral C) + 3-hydroxypentanoic acid (imparts flexibility; its monomer also contains one stereogenic carbon). Uses: speciality packaging, orthopaedic devices, controlled drug release capsules.
  • 2. Nylon-2–Nylon-6: Alternating biodegradable polyamide copolymer of Glycine (H2N–CH2–COOH) and ε-Aminocaproic acid (H2N–(CH2)5–COOH).
  • 3. PGA & PLA (Dextron): Poly(glycolic acid) and Poly(lactic acid) — first commercially successful bioabsorbable surgical sutures for post-operative stitches.

Chapter 10: Molecular Mass of Polymers, Degree of Polymerisation & PDI

  • Why Average Molecular Mass? Synthetic polymer samples contain chains of varying lengths (polydisperse), whereas natural proteins have identical chain lengths (monodisperse, definite molecular mass).
  • Number-Average Molecular Mass (M̄n): M̄n = (Σ NiMi) / (Σ Ni) — determined by colligative properties (e.g., osmotic pressure) which depend on the number of molecules.
  • Weight-Average Molecular Mass (M̄w): M̄w = (Σ NiMi2) / (Σ NiMi) — determined by light scattering and ultracentrifugation (depends on mass of individual molecules).
  • Polydispersity Index (PDI): PDI = M̄w / M̄n. For an ideal monodisperse sample, PDI = 1 (M̄w = M̄n). For polydisperse samples, PDI > 1 (M̄w > M̄n).
  • Degree of Polymerisation (DP): Number of monomer units in a chain; for addition polymers DP = Mpolymer / Mmonomer. For condensation segments with end-group H2O retained (e.g., Nylon-6 segment of 809 u): 113x + 18 = 809 ⇒ x = 7.

10.1 Worked Examples: Degree of Polymerisation and Dispersity

Example 1 — polyethylene: If a chain has molar mass 28,000 g mol−1 and the ethene repeat unit has molar mass approximately 28 g mol−1, then DP ≈ 28,000/28 = 1000 (ignoring end groups).
Example 2 — two chain populations: 50 chains of mass 100 kDa and 50 chains of mass 200 kDa → M̄n = 150 kDa; M̄w = (50×1002 + 50×2002)/(50×100 + 50×200) = 166.67 kDa; Đ = 1.111.
For condensation polymers, the repeat-unit mass excludes the small molecules lost in the repeating condensation step; end groups require separate bookkeeping.
