17. Preparation of alkenes

Two logical classes: β-elimination (E1, E2, Ei: remove H and a leaving group from adjacent carbons) and reduction/coupling (alkyne reduction, Wittig, Kolbe...). Most JEE questions test: which β-H is lost (regiochemistry), whether the alkene is E or Z (stereochemistry) and whether the carbon skeleton rearranges.

17.1 Dehydration of alcohols (acid-catalysed E1)

R–CH2–CH2–OH → R–CH=CH2 + H2O, catalysts: conc. H2SO4, conc. H3PO4, Al2O3 (350 °C), P2O5. Ease: 3° > 2° > 1° (carbocation stability). Typical conditions: 3° alcohol 20–80 °C (e.g., 20 % H2SO4); 2° alcohol 100–140 °C (e.g., 75 % H2SO4); 1° alcohol 170–180 °C (conc. H2SO4). Mechanism (E1):

  • Step 1: protonation of –OH to –OH2+ (fast, reversible).
  • Step 2: loss of water gives the carbocation (slow, rate-determining).
  • Step 3: a base (HSO4− or H2O) removes a β-H, the electrons form the π bond.
H₂SO₄, Δ⟶
tert-Butyl alcohol → 2-methylpropene

Regiochemistry follows Saytzeff (Zaitsev): the more substituted (more stable) alkene predominates, and the E isomer predominates over Z (thermodynamic control, since the carbocation can lose a proton from either side and the products equilibrate under acid).

H₂SO₄, 100 °C⟶
Butan-2-ol → but-2-ene (major, mostly E, ~80 %) + but-1-ene (minor)
Shortcut Competing reaction at lower temperature: ethers. At 140 °C and excess alcohol, ethanol + conc. H2SO4 gives diethyl ether (SN2 by ROH on ROH2+); at 170 °C and excess acid gives ethene.

17.2 Carbocation rearrangements during dehydration

The carbocation of E1 may undergo a 1,2-hydride shift or 1,2-methyl (alkyl) shift to give a more stable cation before losing the proton. A secondary or primary cation next to a quaternary or tertiary C–H centre will rearrange.

H₂SO₄, Δ⟶
3,3-Dimethylbutan-2-ol: 2° cation → CH₃ shift → 3° cation → 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene (major) + 2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene
  • Protonate and lose water: (CH3)3C–CH+–CH3 (secondary).
  • 1,2-Methyl shift: one CH3 of the tert-butyl group migrates with its bonding pair to the adjacent cationic carbon: (CH3)2C+–CH(CH3)2 (tertiary, more stable).
  • Loss of H+: from the CH gives (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2 (tetrasubstituted, major); from a CH3 gives CH2=C(CH3)–CH(CH3)2 (minor).
Trap Dehydration of a 1° alcohol can proceed without a free 1° cation (concerted H shift on departure of water). 2-Methylpropan-1-ol (isobutyl alcohol) gives 2-methylpropene because the hydride shift happens with ionisation: (CH3)2CH–CH2OH2+ → (CH3)3C+ → alkene. Do not write 1-propene-type products.
Example 23 Advanced

Rearrangement in dehydration

Predict the major alkene from dehydration of (a) 3-methylbutan-2-ol, (b) 2,2-dimethylcyclohexanol, (c) neopentyl alcohol (2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol).

Show solution
  1. (a) (CH3)2CH–CH(OH)–CH3: 2° cation (CH3)2CH–CH+–CH3 → 1,2-hydride shift → (CH3)2C+–CH2CH3 (3°) → 2-methylbut-2-ene (major, trisubstituted).
  2. (b) 2,2-Dimethylcyclohexanol → 2° cation, adjacent quaternary C: methyl shift → 3° cation (1,2-dimethylcyclohexyl cation) → 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene (tetrasubstituted, major).
  3. (c) Neopentyl: (CH3)3C–CH2OH has no β-H (the β carbon is quaternary). Ionisation is accompanied by a methyl shift to give (CH3)2C+–CH2CH3, whose elimination gives 2-methylbut-2-ene (major) and 2-methylbut-1-ene.

17.3 Dehydrohalogenation (E2): alcoholic KOH

R–CH2–CH(X)–R′ + KOH (alc.) → alkene + KX + H2O. Alcoholic KOH (ethoxide formed in situ) is the standard base. Aqueous KOH gives substitution (alcohol); alcoholic KOH gives elimination. E2 mechanism: one step, bimolecular, rate = k[RX][base]. The base removes the β-H at the same moment the leaving group leaves and the π bond forms. The H and the leaving group must be anti-periplanar (180° dihedral) so that the orbitals overlap in the transition state (the 120° "syn-clinal" arrangements do not satisfy it). Evidence: stereospecificity.

17.4 Saytzeff vs Hofmann

Saytzeff (Zaitsev) rule: elimination gives the more substituted (thermodynamically more stable) alkene. Applies when the base is small and the leaving group is not unusual (Cl, Br, I, OTs; in E1). Hofmann rule: elimination gives the less substituted alkene when (i) the base is bulky (t-BuOK, LDA...), (ii) the leaving group is bulky or poor (–NR3+, –SR2+, F), or (iii) the substrate is hindered (steric crowding, 3° and bulky groups) so the base reaches the more accessible H, i.e. at the less substituted β carbon; also when the TS has carbanion character.

Small base: Saytzeff, 2-methylbut-2-ene (~70 %)
t-BuOK, t-BuOH⟶

Trap Fluoride as a leaving group: R–F eliminates by an E1cB-like TS because C–F is strong and F− is a poor leaving group; the base removes the most acidic (least hindered, most accessible) H: Hofmann product. 2-Fluorobutane/alkoxide gives mostly but-1-ene.
Example 24 Advanced

Saytzeff or Hofmann?

Predict the major alkene: (a) 2-bromo-3-methylbutane + NaOEt/EtOH (b) 2-bromopentane + t-BuOK/t-BuOH (c) 2-chloro-2,4,4-trimethylpentane + NaOEt (d) (CH3)3C–CH2–CH(CH3)–Br + KOH(alc).

Show solution
  1. (a) β-H's: C-3 H (tertiary) and CH3 H's. Small base → Saytzeff: 2-methylbut-2-ene (CH3)2C=CHCH3.
  2. (b) Bulky base → Hofmann: pent-1-ene major (some pent-2-ene).
  3. (c) (CH3)3C–CH2–C(CH3)2Cl: β-H's are on the CH3 groups and on CH2. Saytzeff would give (CH3)3C–CH=C(CH3)2 (trisubstituted) but its t-Bu and cis-CH3 groups clash badly; the base prefers the accessible CH3 hydrogens, so 2,4,4-trimethylpent-1-ene (Hofmann-type, 1,1-disubstituted) predominates.
  4. (d) 2-Bromo-4,4-dimethylpentane: β-H on C-1 (CH3) and C-3 (CH2); Saytzeff: 4,4-dimethylpent-2-ene (E major) vs 4,4-dimethylpent-1-ene (minor).

17.4a Anti-periplanar requirement in rings (stereospecific E2 in cyclohexanes)

In a cyclohexane chair, the leaving group and the β-H are anti-periplanar only when both are axial. If the only axial β-H is on one side, only that alkene forms regardless of Saytzeff.

⇌
trans-diaxial arrangement needed for E2: Cl axial on C-1 and axial H on C-2 (down) and C-6 (down)
Example 25 Advanced

Menthyl chloride vs neomenthyl chloride

Menthyl chloride (2-isopropyl-5-methylcyclohexyl chloride with Cl, i-Pr and CH3 all equatorial) and its diastereomer neomenthyl chloride (Cl axial when i-Pr and CH3 are equatorial) react with NaOEt/EtOH. Which gives the more substituted alkene and which reacts faster?

Show solution
  1. Neomenthyl chloride: in its stable chair, Cl is axial and the β-H's on C-2 and C-6 are both axial (anti to Cl). The C-2 H is on the carbon carrying i-Pr, so Saytzeff elimination to the trisubstituted alkene (1-isopropyl-4-methylcyclohexene) is allowed; it forms fast and in high yield.
  2. Menthyl chloride: the stable chair has Cl equatorial (no anti H). The ring must flip to a diaxial conformer (i-Pr, CH3 and Cl all axial, a high-energy form). In that chair the C-2 H is equatorial, so only the C-6 axial H is anti to Cl: the only alkene formed is the less substituted, 3-isopropyl-6-methylcyclohexene (Hofmann-type) and slowly.
  3. Lesson: in rings, the anti-periplanar rule overrides Saytzeff and reaction rate depends on the population of the diaxial conformer.
Example 26 Advanced

E2 stereospecificity in acyclic systems

meso-1,2-Dibromo-1,2-diphenylethane and the (±)-diastereomer each lose HBr with a base. Determine the geometry of the 1-bromo-1,2-diphenylethene formed in each case (assume anti elimination).

Show solution
  1. Anti elimination: place the leaving Br and the departing H anti-periplanar. After the rotation required, the remaining substituents that lie gauche (60° apart) end up on the same side of the new double bond; those anti (180°) end up on opposite sides.
  2. Meso (1R,2S): the two phenyl groups are gauche to each other in the reacting conformer, so they end up cis; Br and the phenyl on the other carbon are trans: the product is (E)-1-bromo-1,2-diphenylethene.
  3. (±) (1R,2R)/(1S,2S): the phenyls become trans; Br and the other Ph cis: (Z)-1-bromo-1,2-diphenylethene.
  4. (Geometry derived from 3-D models and checked with RDKit by constraining H–C–C–Br = 180°.)

17.5 Dehydrohalogenation of dihalides to alkenes and alkynes

  • Vicinal or geminal dihalide + 1 equiv. alc. KOH → vinyl halide (alkenyl halide); with a second equivalent of a very strong base (NaNH2) → alkyne (section 31).
  • 1,2-Dihalide + Zn dust (or NaI/acetone): dehalogenation → alkene, anti elimination (stereospecific). meso-2,3-dibromobutane + NaI → trans-but-2-ene; (±)-2,3-dibromobutane → cis-but-2-ene.
Zn or NaI, acetone⟶
meso-2,3-Dibromobutane → trans-but-2-ene (anti elimination of Br, Br)

Dehalogenation stereochemistry

Predict the alkene from (2R,3R)-2,3-dibromobutane with NaI in acetone, and from the meso dibromide.

Show solution

Example 28 Advanced

Hofmann elimination count

N,N-Dimethyl-2-pentylamine is exhaustively methylated with CH3I/excess, treated with Ag2O/H2O and heated. Give the alkene.

Show solution
  1. Amine: CH3CH2CH2–CH(CH3)–N(CH3)2. Exhaustive methylation gives the quaternary ammonium iodide; Ag2O exchanges I− for OH−.
  2. Heating: β-H's are on CH3 (C-1, three H) and CH2 (C-3, two H). Hofmann rule: remove H from the less substituted β carbon, the CH3.
  3. Major product: pent-1-ene; minor: pent-2-ene.

17.7 Wittig reaction (olefination of carbonyls)

Aldehyde or ketone + phosphorus ylide (Ph3P=CHR) → alkene + Ph3P=O. Ylide made by Ph3P + RCH2X → phosphonium salt, then BuLi (or NaH, NaOEt for stabilised ylides). The C=C forms exactly where the C=O was (no mixtures of positional isomers), a major advantage over elimination routes. Non-stabilised ylides (R = alkyl) with aldehydes → mainly Z alkenes; stabilised ylides (R = COOEt, COR) → mainly E.

+ Wittig⟶
Cyclohexanone + methylenetriphenylphosphorane → methylenecyclohexane + Ph₃P=O
+ THF⟶
Ethanal + ethylidenetriphenylphosphorane (non-stabilised) → mainly cis-but-2-ene

17.8 Other routes to alkenes

  • Partial hydrogenation of alkynes: H2/Lindlar → cis (Z); Na/liq. NH3 → trans (E).
  • Kolbe electrolysis of succinate gives ethene (section 4.5).
  • Cracking of petroleum (industrial source of ethene, propene).
  • Dehydration of diols: 1,4-butanediol → THF (ether), butane-2,3-diol: pinacol-type rearrangement. 1,2-diols + P/I2 or Zn: alkenes.
  • Ring opening of cyclopropanes does not give alkenes under simple conditions.
  • Alkyne isomerisation and retro-Diels–Alder (cyclohexene → butadiene + ethene at 800 K).
  • Metathesis (Grubbs): 2 RCH=CH2 → RCH=CHR + CH2=CH2; ring-closing metathesis makes cycloalkenes.
