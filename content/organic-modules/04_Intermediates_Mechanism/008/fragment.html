7. Free radicals

7.1 Structure, persistence and detection

A free radical has an atom with an unpaired electron. A carbon radical R3C• has three bonds and one electron in an orbital on carbon, so it has seven valence electrons: one more than a carbocation and one fewer than a carbanion. The geometry depends on the substituents. The methyl radical is planar or at most a very shallow pyramid (infrared and EPR studies limit the deviation from planarity to about 5°). Simple alkyl radicals such as tert-butyl are best described as shallow pyramids that invert extremely fast (barrier at most 1 to 2 kcal mol−1), so for chemical purposes they behave as planar species. Two effects favour the shallow pyramid: staggering of the substituents, and hyperconjugation between the half-filled orbital and the C–H bonds aligned with it, which also weakens those β C–H bonds. Fluorine and oxygen substituents favour a pyramidal geometry (the trifluoromethyl radical is clearly pyramidal) because the singly occupied orbital is repelled by the lone pairs and is stabilised by interaction with the σ* orbital of the C–F or C–O bond. The allyl radical is planar, which maximises π delocalisation. The vinyl radical is bent (C–C–H angle about 137°) and the E and Z forms interconvert with a very small barrier, so reactions through vinyl radicals give the same mixture from either isomer. In the aryl radical the electron is in an sp2 orbital in the ring plane and is not conjugated with the π system.

Radical geometry: the unpaired electron sits in a p orbital (alkyl) or in a σ orbital (aryl, vinyl)

Radicals are detected by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, ESR) spectroscopy, which sees only unpaired electrons; hyperfine splitting by nearby nuclei (H, 13C, 14N, 19F, 31P) gives the spin density at each atom. The number of lines from n equivalent protons is n + 1. Most radicals have fleeting lives because they dimerise, disproportionate, abstract hydrogen from the solvent, or react with oxygen (itself a diradical). A few radicals are persistent: very delocalised or hindered ones such as triphenylmethyl, the nitroxides (the unpaired electron is shared by N and O, N–O bond order 1.5), galvinoxyl, and, because of steric protection, the tris(tert-butyl)methyl radical (half-life about 20 minutes at 25 °C). The dimer of the triphenylmethyl radical is not hexaphenylethane but a cyclohexadiene derivative formed by bonding of the central carbon of one radical to a para ring carbon of another.

IMP Radical: seven electrons on carbon, neutral, nearly planar and inverting rapidly, so substitution at a stereogenic radical carbon gives racemic product. 1,2-Shifts of hydrogen and saturated alkyl groups are very rare (section 7.10); aryl, vinyl and acyl groups can migrate.
MOT Lens A radical has one electron in an orbital (the SOMO). It can interact with a filled orbital (a three-electron interaction: two electrons stabilised, one destabilised, a small net gain) and with an empty orbital (a one-electron interaction, also stabilising). This is why a radical is stabilised by both donor and acceptor substituents, much less strongly than a cation or an anion is stabilised by a donor or an acceptor respectively.
Concept check. Why is a methyl radical (or a tert-butyl radical) racemising at a stereogenic centre?
Show answer
It is planar or a shallow pyramid inverting with a barrier of about 1 to 2 kcal mol−1; the next bond forms to either face.

7.2 How radicals are formed

  • Thermolysis of weak bonds: peroxides (O–O), azo compounds (AIBN loses N2), N–halogen bonds.
  • Photolysis: X2 → 2X• with ultraviolet or visible light; ketones, alkyl iodides and many organometallic compounds.
  • Electron transfer: Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + HO− + HO•; Cu+ with a diazonium ion (Sandmeyer); alkali metals in ammonia (radical anions); anodic oxidation of carboxylates.
  • Atom abstraction: X• + R–H → HX + R•, the propagation step of chain reactions.

7.3 Stability of radicals

A radical has a half-filled orbital (the SOMO), and it is stabilised by interactions with filled and empty orbitals in the same way as a carbocation, though less strongly.

IMP From C–H bond dissociation enthalpies (kcal mol−1): allyl (89) ≈ benzyl (90) > tertiary (96) > secondary (99) > primary (101) > methyl (105) > vinyl (111) > aryl (113). Resonance gives the largest stabilisation (the diphenylmethyl and triphenylmethyl radicals are more stable still), hyperconjugation gives a smaller increase from primary to tertiary, and a vinyl or aryl radical with the electron in an sp2 orbital is least stable.
  • Delocalisation: in the allyl radical the Hückel nonbonding orbital holds the single electron, with density on the two terminal carbons (0.5 each). In the benzyl radical the squared coefficients of the singly occupied orbital are 4/7 (0.571) at the benzylic carbon and 1/7 (0.143) at each ortho and para carbon, so the spin density is shared among them; EPR hyperfine couplings measure it.
  • Hyperconjugation: the interaction of the half-filled orbital with an adjacent C–H or C–C σ bond (a three-electron, two-orbital interaction, which is stabilising but less so than in the cation).
  • Lone pairs on the adjacent atom: an α-alkoxy or α-amino radical is stabilised by overlap of the lone pair with the half-filled orbital. This is why ethers are oxidised by air at the α-position.
  • Polar effects can override stability. Chlorine atoms are electrophilic, so radical chlorination avoids carbon atoms that carry electron-withdrawing groups even when the radical formed there would be stabilised (see section 7.5).

Concept check. What is the proof that a reaction proceeds by a radical chain and not by a single step?
Show answer
A quantum yield far greater than one, an induction period with inhibitors, and the square-root dependence of rate on light intensity (when termination is bimolecular).

7.4 The radical chain: initiation, propagation, termination

Radical chain mechanism (R–H + Cl₂, light)
  • Initiation: a few radicals are produced from a non-radical molecule (Cl2 + hν → 2Cl•). This step is slow and rare.
  • Propagation: a radical reacts with a molecule to give a product and another radical. Each such step consumes one radical and creates one, so the chain can repeat thousands of times. For methane: Cl• + CH4 → HCl + •CH3; •CH3 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•.
  • Termination: two radicals combine (2Cl• → Cl2; •CH3 + •CH3 → C2H6) or disproportionate (a hydrogen β to one radical centre is transferred to the other: two ethyl radicals give ethane and ethene). Radical concentrations are so low that termination is rare compared with propagation, so chains are long.
  • Quantum yield Φ is the number of product molecules per photon absorbed. Φ ≫ 1 shows a chain mechanism.
  • Inhibitors (oxygen, quinones, phenols, iodine) trap chain-carrying radicals and give an induction period.
  • If termination is bimolecular, the rate of a chain reaction is proportional to the square root of the rate of initiation, so it is proportional to the square root of the light intensity.

In the chlorination of methane the reaction (i) occurs in sunlight but not in the dark; (ii) is delayed by traces of oxygen; (iii) gives thousands of CH3Cl molecules per photon. What do these observations show? Identify the initiation, propagation and termination steps.

Show solution

Why It Happens By the Hammond postulate the transition state of the endothermic bromination step is late and resembles the radical, so the difference in radical stability controls the rate; for the exothermic chlorination step the transition state is early and resembles the reactants, so the effect is small. Bromination is therefore selective and chlorination is not.
Exception & Its Reason Chlorination avoids positions next to electron-withdrawing groups (CN, CO2R) although a radical there would be stabilised, because the electrophilic chlorine atom is deterred by electron-poor carbon in an early transition state.

7.5.2 Selectivity

In the chlorination of propane with light at 25 °C the products are 1-chloropropane (40 %) and 2-chloropropane (60 %). Dividing by the number of hydrogens (6 primary, 2 secondary) gives 6.7 % per primary H and 30 % per secondary H, so each secondary hydrogen is 4.5 times as reactive as a primary one. For bromination of propane at 125 °C the products are 3 % 1-bromopropane and 97 % 2-bromopropane, which gives a secondary hydrogen about 97 times as reactive as a primary one.

Radical halogenation of propane: chlorine is unselective, bromine highly selective

The reason is the Hammond postulate (section 4.3). Abstraction by Br• is endothermic: its transition state comes late and resembles the radical product, so most of the 2 kcal mol−1 difference in the stability of the primary and secondary radicals (and, as the table above shows, of their formation enthalpies) is felt in the activation energies. Abstraction by Cl• is exothermic: the transition state is early and resembles the reactants, so the activation energies differ only slightly. The tert-butoxy radical (from tert-butyl hypochlorite) has an intermediate selectivity (tertiary : secondary : primary about 60 : 10 : 1 in chlorobenzene). Chlorination also shows a polar effect. Because the chlorine atom is an electrophilic radical, it avoids positions next to electron-withdrawing groups such as CN or CO2R, even when the radical at that position would be stabilised by the group; it attacks the β and γ positions instead. In the chlorination of ethylbenzene the stability of the benzylic radical has a limited influence on the product distribution.

Example 25 Adv

Why bromination is selective

Explain why bromination is far more selective than chlorination in the radical halogenation of propane, and what that implies for practical use.

Show solution
  1. H abstraction by Br• from propane is endothermic (the C–H bond is 99 kcal mol−1, H–Br is 88): the transition state is late and carries much of the character of the product radical, so the difference between the secondary and primary radical stabilities is almost fully expressed in the barriers.

7.6 Allylic and benzylic substitution

Allylic and benzylic C–H bonds are weak (89 and 90 kcal mol−1), so bromine atoms abstract them selectively. The usual reagent is N-bromosuccinimide (NBS). NBS does not itself abstract hydrogen: it keeps the concentration of molecular bromine low by reacting with the HBr formed. This matters because the addition of Br2 to the double bond (through a bromonium ion) is second order in Br2, so a very low bromine concentration favours substitution over addition.

NBS, CCl₄, peroxide or hν⟶
Allylic bromination of cyclohexene gives 3-bromocyclohexene

  • Fast: HBr + NBS → Br2 + succinimide, which regenerates a low concentration of Br2.

7.7 Addition of HBr to alkenes in the presence of peroxides

In the presence of peroxides or light, hydrogen bromide adds to an unsymmetrical alkene with the opposite regiochemistry to the ionic addition (Markovnikov's rule): bromine ends up on the less substituted carbon.

HBr, ROOR⟶
1-Bromopropane: the radical chain places Br on the less substituted carbon
  • Initiation: RO–OR → 2RO•; RO• + HBr → ROH + Br•.
  • Propagation 1: Br• adds to the less substituted carbon of the alkene, which gives the more stable (secondary) radical CH3–CH•–CH2Br.
  • Propagation 2: the radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from HBr: CH3CH•CH2Br + HBr → CH3CH2CH2Br + Br•.
  • Termination: combination or disproportionation of radicals.

Why only HBr? Each propagation step must be exothermic or nearly so.

Addition of HBr to alkenes under radical conditions is stereoselective: anti addition is favoured with cyclic and acyclic alkenes. This implies that the intermediate radical is not a freely rotating or inverting carbon radical but a bridged bromine radical in which bromine is bonded to both carbons, which is opened by hydrogen transfer from the face opposite the bromine. In the radical bromination of optically active 2-bromobutane to give 2,3-dibromobutane, most of the product is racemic, a result also explained by the bridged radical.

Exam Trap The peroxide effect operates for HBr only and requires an unsymmetrical alkene to give a different product. It is not a feature of hydroboration–oxidation, which gives anti-Markovnikov alcohols by a concerted polar mechanism; the peroxide in that reaction is only the oxidant in the second step.

The same kind of chain adds BrCCl3, CBr4, CCl4 and thiols to alkenes: the CCl3• radical adds to the less substituted carbon and the new radical abstracts bromine from BrCCl3; thiols add with anti-Markovnikov orientation, with the same stereochemical preference for anti addition to cyclic alkenes as in HBr addition, but with lower stereoselectivity.

7.8 Radical polymerisation

Chain-growth polymers from alkenes CH2=CHX can form by a radical chain. Initiation: I• + CH2=CHX → I–CH2–CH•X. Propagation: the radical adds to the CH2 end of a new monomer, always giving the more stable radical (head-to-tail). Termination: combination or disproportionation of two growing chains. Inhibitors such as hydroquinone are added to monomers for storage; the polymers formed this way include polyethylene (high pressure, with oxygen or peroxide), polystyrene, poly(vinyl chloride), polyacrylonitrile and poly(methyl methacrylate). The chapter on polymers gives the details.

7.10 Rearrangement, fragmentation and cyclisation of radicals

  • Rearrangements are rare. Compared with carbocation rearrangements, radical rearrangements are quite rare. Migration of aryl, vinyl, acyl and other unsaturated groups occurs by addition to the radical centre to give a bridged intermediate; migration of hydrogen and saturated alkyl groups through a three-centre bridged transition state is unusual. The reason is an orbital one: a carbocation shift uses a three-centre, two-electron bond; in a radical there is a third electron, which cannot be accommodated in the bonding orbital and must occupy an antibonding level. The transition state for the migration is therefore much higher in energy than for the cation.
  • Aryl migration (neophyl rearrangement): the neophyl radical PhC(CH3)2CH2• rearranges to the tertiary radical (CH3)2C•–CH2Ph through a bridged spiro intermediate.
  • Intramolecular hydrogen abstraction through a six-membered cyclic transition state allows functionalisation of remote carbon atoms (for example the Hofmann–Löffler reaction of N-haloamines in acid, which gives pyrrolidines).
  • β-Scission: a radical cleaves the bond β to the radical centre to give an alkene and a new radical (the tert-butoxy radical gives acetone and a methyl radical). The thermal cracking of alkanes is a radical chain of hydrogen abstraction and β-scission.
  • Cyclisation: a 5-hexenyl radical closes rapidly to the cyclopentylmethyl radical (five-membered ring, 5-exo), which is the basis of many radical cyclisations that use tributyltin hydride or iodine atom transfer. The cyclopropylmethyl radical opens rapidly to the but-3-enyl radical, and the ring opening is used as a timing probe for the lifetime of a radical intermediate.

Radical or cation?

Neopentyl chloride, (CH3)3CCH2Cl, is treated (i) with Cl2 and light and (ii) with silver nitrate in aqueous ethanol. Predict the carbon skeleton of the products.

Show solution
  1. (i) Radical chlorination: radicals do not undergo 1,2-methyl shifts, so the neopentyl skeleton is kept, for example ClCH2–C(CH3)2–CH2Cl.
  2. (ii) Silver ion removes chloride with concerted methyl migration to give the tert-pentyl cation (CH3)2C+–CH2CH3. The products are 2-methylbutan-2-ol, 2-ethoxy-2-methylbutane and 2-methylbut-2-ene: the skeleton is changed.
  3. Retention of the skeleton points to a radical intermediate; rearrangement of the skeleton points to a cation.
MOT Lens Why aryl and vinyl groups migrate in radicals but alkyl and hydrogen do not. The migration of H or alkyl would pass through a three-centre transition state holding three electrons, of which the third must occupy an antibonding orbital. Aryl and vinyl groups can instead add to the radical centre through their π system, forming a bridged intermediate (a cyclopropane-like radical) without that penalty.

7.11 Radicals with a charge: radical anions and radical cations

One-electron reduction of an aromatic hydrocarbon by an alkali metal in an ether gives a radical anion (EPR shows the spin distributed over the ring; the HOMO–LUMO gap decreases with ring size, so polycyclic hydrocarbons are both easier to reduce and easier to oxidise than benzene). In the presence of a proton source the radical anion is protonated and further reduced (Birch reduction). Cyclooctatetraene accepts two electrons to give the aromatic dianion (10 π electrons) very readily. Ketyls are the radical anions of carbonyl compounds; the benzophenone ketyl is deep blue, and ketyls couple (reversibly) to dianions, which is the basis of the reductive dimerisation of ketones to pinacols. One-electron reduction of 1,2-diketones gives semidiones, and of quinones semiquinones. Radical cations form by one-electron oxidation, and their ease of formation correlates with the HOMO energy.

Think Further Predict the percentage of 2-bromobutane in the radical bromination of butane at 125 °C if the secondary : primary reactivity is the 97 : 1 measured for propane. Comment on the assumption.
Show solution
Butane has 6 primary and 4 secondary hydrogens: 4 × 97 = 388 against 6, so 2-bromobutane is 388/394 = 98.5 %. The assumption is that the reactivity of a secondary C–H in butane equals that in propane; this holds approximately because the bond strengths and the radicals formed are similar, but the value is an estimate.
