3. Hybridisation

3.1 The idea and the three hybrid sets of carbon

A free carbon atom has the configuration 1s²2s²2p². To make four equivalent bonds the 2s orbital is mixed with the 2p orbitals: the number of hybrid orbitals produced equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed. The three types of hybridisation of carbon are summarised below.

IMP Counting rule for carbon. Count the number of σ-bonded neighbours plus lone pairs (the *steric number*): 4 → sp³, 3 → sp², 2 → sp. For carbon in stable molecules this is the same as: only single bonds → sp³; one double bond → sp²; one triple bond or two double bonds → sp.
For any atom: steric number = (number of σ-bonds) + (number of lone pairs); 4 → sp³, 3 → sp², 2 → sp, and the 'extra' bonds beyond the first to the same neighbour are π-bonds made from unhybridised p orbitals. A π-bond is never counted in the steric number.

3.2 Hybridisation of nitrogen, oxygen and the heteroatoms

Lone pairs occupy hybrid orbitals, so they count in the steric number. The one complication is conjugation: a lone pair that is delocalised into an adjacent π-system must sit in a p orbital, and an atom whose lone pair is in a p orbital is classified sp² (it becomes planar). This is why the nitrogen of an amide is sp² but the nitrogen of an amine is sp³.

Exam Trap Pyrrole and pyridine both contain an sp² nitrogen but behave completely differently: in pyridine the lone pair is in the plane, orthogonal to the π cloud and free to take a proton (basic); in pyrrole the lone pair is part of the six-electron π cloud and cannot be protonated without destroying aromaticity (non-basic). This is the single most tested nitrogen distinction.

Hybridisation in a drug molecule

In paracetamol (4-hydroxyacetanilide) state the hybridisation of every non-hydrogen atom and the numbers of σ and π bonds.

  1. Methyl C: sp³. Carbonyl C and carbonyl O: sp². Amide N: sp² (its lone pair is delocalised into C=O). The six ring carbons: sp². Phenolic O: its lone pair overlaps with the ring π system, so it is sp² in the resonance sense (a question that asks for 'steric-number hybridisation' would call it sp³; read the wording).
  2. σ-bonds: every bond contributes one σ. With hydrogens counted explicitly the molecule has 20 bonds when each double bond is counted once: 6 ring C–C, 5 chain bonds (CH₃–C, C–O, C–N, N–C(ring), C(ring)–O), 9 X–H bonds (3 C–H of CH₃, 4 aromatic C–H, 1 N–H, 1 O–H). Total σ = 6 + 5 + 9 = 20.

3.3 s-character: the single most useful number

The 2s orbital is lower in energy and held closer to the nucleus than the 2p orbitals. Therefore the greater the s-character of a hybrid orbital, the more tightly its electrons are held, the shorter and stronger the bond it forms, and the more electronegative that carbon appears. All four properties of Table 3.3 follow from this one statement.

Illustration 3.3 Main

Rank by C–H bond length

Arrange in decreasing C–H bond length: ethane, ethene, ethyne, benzene.

  1. s-character: ethane sp³ (25 %), ethene and benzene sp² (33 %), ethyne sp (50 %). A greater s-character shortens the bond.
  2. Ethane (109 pm) > ethene (108.5 pm) ≈ benzene (108 pm) > ethyne (106 pm).
  3. Ethane > ethene ≈ benzene > ethyne. In JEE style the two sp² compounds are treated as equal.
Illustration 3.4 Adv

Dipole moment from hybridisation

Propane is almost non-polar (0.08 D) whereas propene has μ = 0.36 D and propyne μ = 0.78 D. Give the direction of the dipole in propene and propyne and explain why propyne is more polar.

  1. The CH₃ group is attached to a carbon that is sp² (33 % s) in propene and sp (50 % s) in propyne. A carbon with more s-character holds electrons more tightly, so it behaves as the more electronegative end of the C–C bond. The dipole therefore points from CH₃ (δ+) toward the unsaturated carbon (δ−).
  2. The difference in s-character between the two carbons of the σ-bond is larger in propyne (50 % vs 25 %) than in propene (33 % vs 25 %), so μ(propyne) > μ(propene). Hyperconjugation (σ C–H → π*) pushes density the same way and adds to the effect.

Illustration 3.5 Adv

Allene geometry and chirality

Explain why the two CH₂ planes of allene lie at 90° and why penta-2,3-diene CH₃CH=C=CHCH₃ can exist as a pair of enantiomers.

  1. The central carbon is sp-hybridised. Its two unhybridised p orbitals are perpendicular to each other; one overlaps with the p orbital on the left carbon and the other with the p orbital on the right carbon. The two π bonds are therefore in perpendicular planes.
  2. The substituents on each terminal carbon lie in the plane perpendicular to the π bond of that carbon, so the two CH₂ (or CHCH₃) planes are at 90° to each other.
  3. In CH₃CH=C=CHCH₃ each terminal carbon carries two different groups (H and CH₃). The molecule has no plane of symmetry and no centre of symmetry, so it has a chiral axis and exists as two non-superimposable mirror images. If either terminal carbon carries two identical groups, a mirror plane exists and the allene is achiral.
