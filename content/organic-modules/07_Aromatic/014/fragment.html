12. Side-chain reactions of alkylbenzenes

The carbon attached directly to the ring is the benzylic carbon. Its C–H bonds are weak (BDE of C–H in toluene ≈ 375 kJ mol-1, versus ≈ 410 for a primary alkyl C–H and ≈ 472 for benzene C–H) because the benzyl radical, cation and anion are all stabilised by resonance over the ring. Every reaction of this section is a consequence of that single fact.

benzyl radical benzyl cation benzyl anion
Benzylic intermediates are resonance stabilised: the charge or odd electron is shared with the ortho and para ring carbons

12.1 Benzylic halogenation (free radical)

Chain mechanism: initiation (X2 → 2X· under hν/heat, or peroxide), propagation (X· + ArCH3 → HX + ArCH2·; ArCH2· + X2 → ArCH2X + X·), termination.

Cl₂, hν (or boil)⟶ Cl₂, hν⟶ Cl₂, hν⟶
Toluene → benzyl chloride → benzal chloride → benzotrichloride (stepwise side-chain chlorination)
  • Bromination with Br2/hν or, more conveniently, N-bromosuccinimide (NBS), CCl4, peroxide (or hν): NBS supplies a very low steady concentration of Br2, which prevents addition to double bonds and ring bromination. Bromine radicals are much more selective than chlorine radicals: ethylbenzene gives almost exclusively 1-bromo-1-phenylethane.
  • Isopropylbenzene (cumene) with NBS gives 2-bromo-2-phenylpropane (tertiary benzylic radical).
  • tert-Butylbenzene has no benzylic H, so no benzylic halogenation (and no benzylic oxidation).
  • Ring versus side chain: conditions decide. Lewis acid (Fe, FeX3), no light, cold → ring. Light, heat, peroxide, no Lewis acid → side chain. Boiling toluene + Cl2 (no Fe) → side chain; with Fe, in the dark at room temperature → ring.

Show solution

12.2 Benzylic oxidation

Hot alkaline KMnO4, hot acidic K2Cr2O7, hot dilute HNO3 (or Na2Cr2O7/H2SO4) oxidise any alkyl chain bearing at least one benzylic hydrogen completely to –COOH, regardless of the chain length. The rest of the chain is lost as CO2/carboxylic acids. A tertiary alkyl (no benzylic H) is untouched.

  • Mechanism (sketch). The first step is hydrogen-atom (or hydride) abstraction from the benzylic C–H by Mn(VII) or Cr(VI), giving a benzylic radical (or cation); after repeated oxidation the benzylic carbon ends up as COOH, and any carbon beyond it is cleaved off.
  • Electron-rich rings are oxidised themselves. p-Toluidine, p-cresol or anisole with strong oxidants give tars or quinones. Protect an amine as the acetamide and oxidise the side chain: p-toluidine → p-acetamidotoluene → p-acetamidobenzoic acid → hydrolysis → p-aminobenzoic acid.
  • Partial oxidation to the aldehyde: (i) Étard reaction, CrO2Cl2 in CS2 or CCl4 followed by H2O/Zn; (ii) CrO3 in acetic anhydride gives the benzylidene diacetate (ArCH(OAc)2), which is hydrolysed to ArCHO; (iii) benzal chloride hydrolysis; (iv) MnO2 or PCC on benzyl alcohols.
Trap "Alkylbenzene + KMnO4" always gives ArCOOH with one carbon only; the number of carbons in the chain is irrelevant. The answer for ethylbenzene, n-propylbenzene, isopropylbenzene, n-butylbenzene, sec-butylbenzene is benzoic acid. For tert-butylbenzene, no reaction.

12.3 The industrial cumene–phenol process (Hock)

Cumene is auto-oxidised in air (≈ 380 K, radical chain, a tertiary benzylic radical) to cumene hydroperoxide. Treatment with dilute H2SO4 causes a rearrangement in which the phenyl group migrates from carbon to oxygen, with loss of water; the resulting oxocarbenium ion hydrolyses to phenol + acetone. This route supplies most of the world's phenol and acetone.

Cumene → cumene hydroperoxide → phenol + acetone (Hock rearrangement)

Mechanism steps

Write the three mechanistic steps in the acid-catalysed decomposition of cumene hydroperoxide.

Show solution
  1. (1) Protonation of the terminal O–O oxygen (–OOH → –OOH2+).
  2. (2) Loss of water with simultaneous 1,2-migration of phenyl from C to the electron-deficient O (like Baeyer–Villiger / Criegee): an oxocarbenium ion, PhO–C+(CH3)2.
  3. (3) Water adds to the cation; the hemiketal PhO–C(OH)(CH3)2 collapses to phenol + acetone.
  4. The phenyl migrates in preference to methyl because of its better migratory aptitude (the bridged phenonium-like TS).

12.4 Acidity and carbanion chemistry

Benzylic C–H bonds are far more acidic than ordinary alkyl C–H: pKa ≈ 41 for toluene (methane ≈ 56), 33 for diphenylmethane, 31 for triphenylmethane. With strong bases (n-BuLi, NaNH2, KNH2) toluene is deprotonated to benzylic anions. Ortho and para nitro groups (–M) enormously increase the acidity: the CH3 of 2,4-dinitrotoluene (and TNT) is acidic enough to be deprotonated by mild bases (piperidine, alkoxides) and condenses with benzaldehyde; TNT also gives deep-coloured anions with alkali.

The vinyl group conjugated with the ring is an alkene first, an arene second: the C=C reacts under much milder conditions than the ring.
