5. Zaitsev versus Hofmann orientation

When two or more different β-hydrogens are available, the regiochemistry of the elimination depends on the transition state. The Zaitsev rule states that the more substituted (more stable) alkene predominates; the Hofmann product is the less substituted alkene. Three factors control which dominates: the size of the base, the size and charge of the leaving group, and the acidity and accessibility of the β-hydrogens.

  • Small unhindered base (EtO−, HO−) with a halide: mainly Zaitsev.
  • Bulky base (t-BuO−, LDA, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidide): the more accessible, less hindered hydrogens (usually on CH3) are removed faster, so the Hofmann product increases.
  • Poor or bulky leaving groups (fluoride, +NR3, +SR2): the transition state has more carbanion character (E1cB-like), the most acidic and least hindered hydrogen is removed, giving the Hofmann product.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOEt, EtOH

Product:

Small base: the trisubstituted Zaitsev alkene 2-methylbut-2-ene predominates.

Reactant:

Reagent: KOC(CH3)3, (CH3)3COH

Product:

Bulky base: the less substituted Hofmann alkene 2-methylbut-1-ene predominates.

Reactant:

Reagent: Ag₂O, H₂O, then heat (Hofmann elimination)

Product:

The quaternary ammonium group gives but-1-ene, the Hofmann alkene. The very bulky +NMe3 group makes the C–H bonds of the CH3 group the most accessible.

Key idea. Fluorides, ammonium salts and sulfonium salts give Hofmann-type alkenes; chlorides, bromides and iodides with small bases give Zaitsev alkenes; the same bromide with t-BuOK shifts toward Hofmann.
Trap. Hofmann does not mean the less stable alkene is always the exclusive product; it means it is the major one under those conditions. Mixtures are normal.
