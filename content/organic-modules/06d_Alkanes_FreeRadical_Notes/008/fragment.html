9. Allylic and benzylic halogenation; radical stability

The C–H bond adjacent to a C=C (allylic) or a benzene ring (benzylic) is unusually weak because the radical formed is delocalised over several atoms. Approximate bond dissociation enthalpies (kJ mol−1): methyl C–H 439, primary 423, secondary 413, tertiary 404, allylic 372, benzylic about 375, vinylic 465, phenyl 473. The order of radical stability is therefore allyl ≈ benzyl > 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3• > vinyl, aryl. Alkyl radicals are stabilised by hyperconjugation with the α C–H (or C–C) bonds: the tert-butyl radical has 9 α-hydrogens, isopropyl 6, ethyl 3.

N-Bromosuccinimide (NBS) in CCl4 with light or a peroxide brominates allylic and benzylic positions without adding to the double bond. NBS keeps the concentration of Br2 very low: traces of HBr react with NBS to give Br2 in situ, and a low Br2 concentration favours radical abstraction over ionic addition across the C=C.

  1. Initiation: a trace of Br• forms from Br2 by light.
  2. Propagation 1: Br• + R–H (allylic) → HBr + allylic radical.
  3. HBr + NBS → Br2 + succinimide (supplies low, steady Br2).
  4. Propagation 2: allylic radical + Br2 → allylic bromide + Br•.

Substitution occurs at the benzylic CH2; the ring is untouched. Chlorination of toluene with Cl2/hν (boiling) similarly gives C6H5CH2Cl, C6H5CHCl2 and C6H5CCl3 in turn.

Key idea. An unsymmetrical allylic radical has two reactive ends, so two constitutional products can form. For but-1-ene, CH2=CH–CH•–CH3 ↔ •CH2–CH=CH–CH3 gives 3-bromobut-1-ene and 1-bromobut-2-ene (E and Z).
Trap. Ring halogenation of toluene needs a Lewis acid (Cl2/FeCl3, dark, cold) and is ionic (o- and p-products). Light and heat give side-chain (radical) substitution. The two conditions give different products.
