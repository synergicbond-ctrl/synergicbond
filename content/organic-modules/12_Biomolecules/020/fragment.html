3.2 Denaturation, solubility and tests

Denaturation is loss of the native secondary/tertiary (and quaternary) structure with the primary structure unchanged; the protein loses its biological activity and usually becomes less soluble (coagulation). Agents: heat (boiling an egg), extremes of pH, urea and guanidinium chloride (6–8 M), detergents (SDS), organic solvents (ethanol), heavy-metal ions (Hg2+, Pb2+, Ag+: bind –SH groups, basis of their toxicity), and vigorous shaking. Some denaturations are reversible (ribonuclease, Anfinsen); boiling egg white is not.

  • Isoelectric precipitation: proteins are least soluble at their pI; casein (pI ≈ 4.6) precipitates when milk is acidified (curdling; lactic acid bacteria make curd).
  • Salting out: high concentrations of (NH4)2SO4 remove the hydration shell and precipitate proteins without denaturing them.
  • Charge: below its pI a protein is cationic (moves to cathode), above it anionic. Proteins are amphoteric colloids; dialysis separates them from small ions.

<h4>Classification of proteins</h4>
  • Simple proteins give only amino acids on hydrolysis (albumin, globulin, glutelin, histones).
  • Conjugated proteins also contain a non-protein prosthetic group: glycoproteins (carbohydrate), lipoproteins, nucleoproteins, chromoproteins (haemoglobin: haem), metalloproteins (ferritin: Fe; caeruloplasmin: Cu).
  • Derived proteins: peptones, proteoses from partial hydrolysis.
