39. Mechanism: Hofmann bromamide (degradation) reaction, step by step

Overall: RCONH2 + Br2 + 4OH− → RNH2 + CO32− + 2Br− + 2H2O.

  • Step 1 – deprotonation. OH− removes an N–H proton from the amide (pKa ≈ 15–17; the anion is stabilised by C=O). Why OH− is enough: a small equilibrium concentration of the amidate is all that is needed.
  • Step 2 – bromination of N. The amidate N lone pair attacks Br2 (σ*(Br–Br) is the LUMO); Br− leaves. Product: N-bromoamide RCONHBr. Why: a good nucleophile meets a polarisable electrophile.
  • Step 3 – second deprotonation. The N–H of RCONHBr is more acidic than that of the amide (Br is electron withdrawing); OH− gives the bromoamidate anion RCON−Br.
  • Step 4 – the rearrangement (the key step). As Br− leaves from N, the group R on the carbonyl carbon migrates to N with its bonding pair. MO lens: the R–C σ bond overlaps with the developing empty orbital on N (the σ*(N–Br)/vacant p orbital); migration is suprafacial, the migrating carbon uses the same lobe throughout, so configuration at R is retained. The product is an isocyanate R–N=C=O. Why concerted: no free nitrene is formed – migration provides the electron donation that the departing Br− demands.
  • Step 5 – addition of OH−. The isocyanate carbon is a strong electrophile (π* of N=C=O). OH− adds to give the carbamate anion RNH–COO−.
  • Step 6 – decarboxylation. The carbamic acid (after protonation) loses CO2 (very favourable, CO2 is trapped as carbonate in base), leaving RNH2.

What decides which group migrates? The better the group can stabilise positive charge in the transition state (tertiary > secondary ≈ benzyl ≈ aryl > primary > methyl). Only one group (R) is on carbon, so there is no competition in Hofmann; competition appears in Beckmann/Baeyer–Villiger. Same pattern in Curtius: R–C(=O)–N=N+=N− → loses N2 while R migrates → R–N=C=O. Loss of the extremely stable N2 is the driving force, so the Curtius rearrangement needs no base.
