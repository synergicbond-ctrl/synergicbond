15. Phenol–formaldehyde resins (Bakelite)

Phenol is trifunctional towards formaldehyde: its two ortho and one para carbons are nucleophilic. Reaction conditions decide whether the product is linear or three-dimensional.

15.1 Base-catalysed route (resol → Bakelite)

Excess HCHO, NaOH. The sequence, with the reason for every step:

+ 1 deprotonation⟶ +
Phenoxide (HOMO has large coefficients at ortho and para carbons) is a far better nucleophile than phenol.
+ 2 ortho attack on C=O (π*), then proton shift⟶
Hydroxymethylation: the o-carbon (π–HOMO of phenoxide) attacks the electrophilic carbon of HCHO (π*, LUMO), the ring re-aromatises by losing H⁺. Result: o-hydroxymethylphenol (and p- isomer).

The hydroxymethylphenols react further at the other ortho/para sites to give di- and tri-hydroxymethyl phenols, which condense with each other:

+ 3 –H₂O, link –CH₂–⟶
Benzylic –CH₂OH (after loss of OH⁻ via a quinone-methide) is attacked by another phenoxide carbon: –CH₂– bridges (also –CH₂–O–CH₂– ether bridges).

This low-molar-mass, still-fusible, soluble mixture is called a resol (A-stage), rich in –CH2OH groups. Heating the resol (with no further reagent) makes the –CH2OH groups condense with ring C–H and with each other, to produce the infusible, insoluble, cross-linked network: Bakelite (C-stage).

MOT Lens Phenoxide is a far better nucleophile than phenol: the oxygen lone-pair donation raises the π-HOMO energy and concentrates the HOMO coefficient at the ortho and para carbons. The electrophile is formaldehyde, whose LUMO is the C=O π* with a large coefficient on carbon. The ortho/para attack is therefore the HOMO(phenoxide)–LUMO(HCHO) interaction at the carbons with the largest coefficients.

15.2 Acid-catalysed route (novolac)

Excess phenol, acid catalyst (HCl, oxalic acid), so CH2O is the limiting reagent. H+ protonates HCHO to ⁺CH2OH (a stronger electrophile); phenol (a weaker nucleophile than phenoxide) attacks it at ortho/para; the benzylic alcohol is protonated, loses water to a benzylic cation which is attacked by a second phenol, linking rings through –CH2–. With phenol in excess the chain ends in free phenol rings, and no –CH2OH groups remain. This novolac is linear (or lightly branched), fusible, soluble and stable in storage. Cross-linking requires an added source of HCHO: hexamethylenetetramine (hexa) is heated with it, releasing HCHO/NH3 equivalents and bridging the chains, giving Bakelite.

Properties and uses of Bakelite: hard, rigid, brittle, heat-resistant, excellent electrical insulator, resists most solvents. **Electrical switches and sockets, handles of utensils, combs, phonograph records, fountain pens, laminates** (with paper/cloth).
Exam Trap Bakelite is not a polymer of a single monomer that “loses water at every step” in a simple chain: it is built from trifunctional phenol and bifunctional HCHO, so the net count of water lost does not follow 2n − 1. Do not apply that formula to networks.
Special Case Counting water in a network. The 2n − 1 rule is for linear chains from bifunctional monomers. A network has no definite chain length, so the quantity of water released is calculated from the mole ratio of monomers (the extent of condensation), not from 2n − 1.
