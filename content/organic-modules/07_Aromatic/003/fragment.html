1. Benzene: the structure problem

Benzene, C6H6, was isolated by Faraday (1825) and its formula fixed by Mitscherlich. Its unusual behaviour set the central puzzle of nineteenth-century organic chemistry: a molecule with the formula of a "highly unsaturated" compound (degree of unsaturation 4) that nevertheless refuses most reactions of alkenes.

Kekulé (1865) rescued the cyclic triene by proposing that two double-bond arrangements interconvert so rapidly that no 1,2-dibromobenzene with a C=C and one with a C–C between the bromines can be separated. This explains the isomer count but not the unreactivity, the equal bond lengths or the extra stability. The modern picture is resonance (valence bond language) or delocalised molecular orbitals (MO language).

Trap Benzene is not an equilibrium mixture of two cyclohexatrienes that flip back and forth. The two Kekulé structures differ only in the placement of electrons, not atoms; they are contributors to one hybrid. If the question says "oscillation" it is describing Kekulé's historical proposal, not the modern view.

1.1 The orbital picture

Each carbon is sp2 hybridised: two sp2 orbitals form C–C σ bonds, the third forms the C–H bond, and the remaining p orbital, perpendicular to the ring plane, overlaps with both neighbours. Six p orbitals give six π molecular orbitals. In Hückel theory their energies are α + 2β, α + β (twice), α − β (twice) and α − 2β, where β is negative. The three lowest orbitals (one non-degenerate bonding orbital and a degenerate pair) are filled by the six π electrons.

  • Total π energy of benzene = 2(α + 2β) + 4(α + β) = 6α + 8β.
  • Three isolated ethylene π bonds would give 3 × 2(α + β) = 6α + 6β.
  • Difference = 2β (about 150 kJ mol-1 experimentally): the delocalisation (resonance) energy — the origin of aromatic stability.
  • The lowest orbital has no node in the plane perpendicular to the ring; the degenerate pair has one node each; the degenerate antibonding pair has two; the highest has three. The number of nodes rises with energy.
Remember Closed-shell electronic configuration: all bonding MOs full, no electrons in antibonding or non-bonding orbitals. This is the same statement as the (4n + 2) rule, which you will meet in section 2.

Notice the two-stage logic: conjugation of two double bonds gives only a small gain, but adding a third double bond into the cycle gives a huge one. Further, hydrogenation of benzene to the diene is actually endothermic (about +24 kJ mol-1), so benzene hydrogenation, once started, cannot be stopped at cyclohexadiene or cyclohexene: any partially hydrogenated intermediate is hydrogenated faster than benzene itself.

Trap In "which is more stable" questions the reference is always a hypothetical localised cyclohexatriene, not cyclohexane. The resonance energy of benzene is about 150 kJ mol-1 (36 kcal mol-1); naphthalene is about 255 kJ mol-1, which is less than two benzenes (300), so each ring of naphthalene is less aromatic than benzene.

1.3 Isomers of C6H6

Benzene is the most stable of a family of C6H6 isomers whose structures are interesting because they show what benzene is not.

Benzene Dewar benzene prismane hexa-1,5-diyne fulvene
Valence isomers and other isomers of C₆H₆ (all higher in energy than benzene)

Dewar benzene and prismane are real, isolable (though strained) molecules; they were synthesised long after the Kekulé period and revert to benzene when heated. Ladenburg proposed prismane as a candidate structure; it would give 1 monosubstituted and 3 disubstituted derivatives, so it cannot be excluded by isomer count alone, but it has no C=C and its chemistry is entirely different.

1.4 Ozonolysis as a probe of Kekulé structure

Benzene ozonolysis (O3, then Zn/H2O) gives three molecules of glyoxal, OHC–CHO, exactly as expected if each C=C is cleaved once. That the product is glyoxal for either Kekulé form is consistent with the resonance picture but is also a favourite JEE trap because of the following disubstituted example.

Example 1 Main

Ozonolysis ratio for o-xylene

o-Xylene is ozonolysed followed by reductive work-up. Predict the products and the molar ratio if the two Kekulé structures contribute equally.

Show solution
  1. Kekulé form A (double bond between the two methyl-bearing carbons): that C=C gives CH3CO–COCH3 (biacetyl, 2,3-butanedione); the other two C=C bonds each give glyoxal (OHC–CHO).
  2. Kekulé form B (double bonds C1=C6, C2=C3, C4=C5, so each methyl-bearing carbon is double bonded to a CH): the two C=C involving methyl carbons each give CH3CO–CHO (methylglyoxal); the C4=C5 bond gives glyoxal.
  3. With 50 : 50 weighting: glyoxal = 0.5 × 2 + 0.5 × 1 = 1.5; methylglyoxal = 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 2 = 1.0; biacetyl = 0.5. Ratio glyoxal : methylglyoxal : biacetyl = 3 : 2 : 1.
  4. This ratio is the classic evidence that both Kekulé structures contribute equally and that the "single/double" alternation is only a formal bookkeeping device.
