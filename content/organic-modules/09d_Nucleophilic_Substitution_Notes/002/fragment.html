3. The SN1 mechanism: carbocations and racemisation

In a unimolecular substitution (SN1) the leaving group first departs without help from the nucleophile, giving a carbocation. This ionisation is slow and rate determining. The cation is then captured quickly by whatever nucleophile is present (frequently the solvent, in which case the reaction is a solvolysis). The rate depends only on the substrate: rate = k[RX]; changing the concentration or even the identity of the nucleophile leaves the rate unchanged.

  1. Slow: R–X → R+ + X– (rate determining).
  2. Fast: R+ + Nu–H → R–Nu+–H (nucleophilic capture on either face of the planar cation).
  3. Fast: loss of H+ to the solvent gives the neutral product R–Nu.

Because the cation is planar and sp2 hybridised, capture can occur from either face. A single enantiomer of a chiral tertiary halide would therefore give a racemic product. In practice many SN1 reactions give slightly more inversion than retention. This is explained by ion pairs: the leaving group has not fully diffused away when the cation is captured, and it shields the face from which it departed, so attack from the opposite face is favoured. The more stable and longer-lived the cation, the closer the product is to complete racemisation.

Key idea. SN1: two steps, carbocation intermediate, rate = k[RX], racemisation (often with a small excess of inversion owing to ion pairing). Favoured by tertiary, allylic and benzylic substrates, good leaving groups and ionising polar protic solvents.
