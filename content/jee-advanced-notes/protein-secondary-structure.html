Complete notes — protein structure

One idea behind all secondary structure. The peptide unit is planar (resonance, ω ≈ 180°). Only two bonds per residue rotate: N–Cα (φ) and Cα–C(=O) (ψ). Regular repeating (φ, ψ) values give a regular fold, which is stabilised by backbone C=O···H–N hydrogen bonds.

1 · Primary structure

  • The specific sequence of amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds (–CO–NH–), written N-terminus → C-terminus.
  • Repeating backbone: –NH–CH(R)–C(=O)–.
  • Any change in sequence gives a different primary structure, hence a different protein.
  • Only covalent bond involved: the peptide (amide) bond.
  • n residues → n − 1 peptide bonds (linear chain).

2 · The peptide bond

  • Resonance: O=C–NH ↔ ⁻O–C=NH⁺ gives the C–N bond partial double-bond character (≈ 1.33 Å, between C–N 1.47 Å and C=N 1.27 Å).
  • Rotation about C–N is restricted → six atoms (Cα, C, O, N, H, Cα) lie in one peptide plane.
  • trans (ω ≈ 180°) is strongly preferred: the two Cα atoms are on opposite sides; cis (ω ≈ 0°) is rare except before proline.
  • Free rotation: N–Cα (φ) and Cα–C (ψ) only.

3 · Secondary structure

  • Conformation of localised regions of the chain, due to regular folding of the backbone by hydrogen bonding between C=O and N–H of peptide bonds.
  • Two types: α-helix and β-pleated sheet.
  • Side-chain interactions are not what defines secondary structure.

4 · Tertiary and quaternary

  • Tertiary: overall 3-D folding of one polypeptide chain (further folding of the secondary structure). Two shapes: fibrous (keratin, myosin; insoluble, chains parallel, held by H-bonds and S–S links) and globular (insulin, albumin; soluble, chain coiled into a sphere).
  • Stabilised by H-bonds (side-chain NH2/COOH/OH), ionic (salt-bridge) COO⁻···NH3+, van der Waals / hydrophobic forces between R groups and disulphide (–S–S–) bridges between Cys residues.
  • Quaternary: arrangement of two or more polypeptide subunits into one protein complex (e.g. haemoglobin, 4 chains), held by the same weak forces.

α-Helix — Pauling–Corey–Branson (3.613 helix)

Important features

  1. The chain twists into a right-handed (clockwise) spiral; the structure is also called the 3.613 helix.
  2. 3.6 = average number of amino acid residues per turn; 13 = number of atoms in the hydrogen-bonded ring (chelate).
  3. The C=O of residue i is hydrogen-bonded to the N–H of residue i + 4 (four residues farther along the chain).
  4. All N–H bonds point along the helix axis toward the N-terminus; all C=O bonds point along the axis toward the C-terminus, so every hydrogen bond is nearly parallel to the axis.
  5. The R groups extend outward from the core of the helix (less steric crowding).
  6. All naturally occurring α-amino acids (except achiral glycine) are L; they favour the right-handed helix. Naturally occurring carbohydrates are D.

Helix dipole: all peptide dipoles point the same way, so the N-terminal end is partly δ⁺ and the C-terminal end partly δ⁻.

Counting the 13-membered ring

O(i) → C(i) → N(i+1) → Cα(i+1) → C(i+1) → N(i+2) → Cα(i+2) → C(i+2) → N(i+3) → Cα(i+3) → C(i+3) → N(i+4) → H(i+4) ⋯ back to O(i)

That is 13 atoms; the closing link is the hydrogen bond H···O. The ring is not a flat polygon, it is the real 3-D twisted loop of the helix.

β-Pleated sheet

Important features

  1. Forms when two or more peptide chains (strands) line up side by side; each strand is almost fully extended (≈ 3.3–3.5 Å per residue).
  2. C=O and N–H bonds lie in the plane of the sheet, perpendicular to the strand direction.
  3. Hydrogen bonds are inter-strand (between neighbouring chains), between N–H and C=O of nearby residues; the bonds run across the sheet.
  4. Cα atoms alternate up and down, giving the pleated (drapery-fold) appearance. Side chains R point alternately above and below the sheet.
  5. Parallel (all strands N→C in the same direction) or antiparallel (alternate strands run opposite).

Typical torsions

Antiparallel β≈ −139°≈ +135°

Antiparallel sheets have straight, strong H-bonds (alternating narrow and wide pairs); in parallel sheets the H-bonds are angled and slightly weaker. Silk fibroin (antiparallel β) is flexible but not stretchable; α-keratin (α-helix) is elastic.

JEE traps. (1) The α-helix H-bond is not between neighbouring residues: it is i → i+4. (2) Disulphide bridges and salt bridges mainly stabilise tertiary / quaternary structure; they do not define a helix. (3) The peptide C–N bond does not rotate freely, but N–Cα and Cα–C do. (4) Proline has no N–H donor and breaks helices. (5) “13” in 3.613 counts atoms in the ring, not residues. (6) Glycine is achiral. (7) Denaturation breaks secondary/tertiary structure (H-bonds, weak forces) but not the peptide bonds.

Modelling note

Every atom in the animation comes from one fixed polypeptide model built with ideal bond lengths (N–Cα 1.458 Å, Cα–C 1.525 Å, C–N 1.329 Å, C=O 1.231 Å, N–H 1.01 Å) and ideal bond angles. The scenes only change the torsions φ and ψ, the camera, the highlighting and visibility. The side chain R is drawn as one amber sphere (a CH3-like group); glycine residues have none. Hydrogen bonds are detected from real O···H distances in the model (< 2.5 Å) and are never covalent bonds.
