1.4 Ring structures: hemiacetals, pyranose and furanose

A γ- or δ-hydroxy aldehyde cyclises spontaneously because five- and six-membered rings are strong. In D-glucose the C-5 hydroxyl attacks C-1, giving a six-membered pyranose (named after pyran); attack by the C-4 hydroxyl gives a five-membered furanose (after furan). The new stereocentre (C-1) can be either configuration, giving α and β anomers.

Key idea Haworth conversion rule (D-sugars). Take the Fischer projection: every substituent on the right goes down in the Haworth ring; every substituent on the left goes up. The terminal CH2OH on the last ring carbon goes up for a D-sugar. The anomeric –OH is down in α (trans to CH2OH) and up in β (cis to CH2OH) for D-sugars.

Counting forms of a sugar

  • Open chain aldohexose: 4 stereocentres → 16 isomers.
  • Cyclic pyranose: 5 stereocentres (add C-1) → 32 cyclic isomers = 16 pairs of anomers.
  • A given D-aldohexose can exist as α/β-pyranose and α/β-furanose, so four cyclic forms plus the open chain and the hydrate.
  • At equilibrium in water (about 25 °C) D-glucose is ≈ 36 % α-pyranose, ≈ 64 % β-pyranose, with <0.5 % furanose and ≈0.002 % open chain.

<h4>Why the ring is a chair, and why β-D-glucose is so stable</h4>

In the ⁴C1 chair of β-D-glucopyranose all five non-hydrogen substituents (four OH and the CH2OH) are equatorial; in α-D-glucopyranose only the C-1 OH is axial. Mannose has an axial OH at C-2, galactose at C-4, which is why glucose is the most abundant natural sugar.

Key idea Anomeric effect. An electronegative substituent at C-1 of a pyranose prefers the axial position, contrary to simple steric reasoning, because of n(O-5) → σ*(C-1–X) hyperconjugation. This is why the α-anomer (axial OH) is still ≈ 36 % at equilibrium. In a solvent that H-bonds strongly the effect is weakened; in non-polar solvents it is stronger. It is a favourite conceptual question.
