## 1. General Properties

### 1.1 Position and electronic configuration
Group 2 = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra; general configuration [noble gas] ns² **[NCERT][Master Notes][JD Lee]**. All except Be are called "alkaline earth metals" because their oxides/hydroxides are alkaline and occur in earth's "earths" (minerals) **[NCERT]**. +2 is the dominant oxidation state because removing two ns electrons gives a noble-gas core; the third IE is enormous (removing from a closed shell), so M³⁺ is never seen **[Master Notes][JD Lee: "the third ionization energy is so high that M³⁺ ions are never formed"]**.

Electronic configurations (all sources agree):
Be [He]2s², Mg [Ne]3s², Ca [Ar]4s², Sr [Kr]5s², Ba [Xe]6s², Ra [Rn]7s².

### 1.2 Occurrence
Be: beryl Be₃Al₂Si₆O₁₈ (rare, ~2 ppm) **[Master Notes][JD Lee]**. Mg: magnesite MgCO₃, dolomite CaCO₃·MgCO₃, carnallite KCl·MgCl₂·6H₂O, seawater (~0.13% Mg²⁺) **[Master Notes][JD Lee]**. Ca: limestone/chalk/marble CaCO₃, gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O, fluorite CaF₂, fluoroapatite [3(Ca₃(PO₄)₂)·CaF₂] **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. Sr: celestite SrSO₄, strontianite SrCO₃ (~384 ppm) **[Master Notes][JD Lee]**. Ba: barytes/barite BaSO₄, witherite BaCO₃ (~390 ppm) **[Master Notes][JD Lee]**. Ra: extremely scarce, radioactive, isolated by the Curies from pitchblende, used historically in cancer radiotherapy **[JD Lee][NCERT]**.

[JD Lee] gives industrial-scale production figures (1993 data) that are good "real-world scale" flavour: Mg 303,000 t; CaO (lime) 127.9 million t; cement 1396 million t; gypsum 88.2 million t; CaF₂ 3.6 million t; MgCO₃ 10.8 million t; BaSO₄ 4.9 million t.

### 1.3 Atomic/ionic radii
Radii increase down the group (new shell added each step); Group 2 atoms/ions are **smaller** than the corresponding Group 1 element in the same period because of higher nuclear charge **[NCERT][Master Notes][JD Lee][S block.pdf]**.

Metallic radius (pm): Be 111/112, Mg 160, Ca 197, Sr 215, Ba 222 **[all sources, consistent]**.
M²⁺ ionic radius (six-coordinate, pm): Be²⁺ 31 (four-coordinate value used more commonly, 0.31 Å; six-coordinate ≈0.45 Å per **[JD Lee]** footnote), Mg²⁺ 72, Ca²⁺ 100, Sr²⁺ 118, Ba²⁺ 135, Ra²⁺ 148.

### 1.4 Ionization enthalpy
IE₁ order: Be(899) > Mg(737) > Ca(590) > Sr(549) > Ba(503); Ra(509) is slightly higher than Ba — a **minor "reversal"/irregularity** noted numerically in the data tables across sources (electron shell/relativistic effects — not explicitly explained in any source, just present in the raw table) **[Master Notes][JD Lee][S block.pdf][NCERT]**.

IE₂ values: Be 1757, Mg 1450, Ca 1145, Sr 1064, Ba 965, Ra 979 (again Ra > Ba slightly).

IE₃ (from JD Lee/aemsg table, not in NCERT): Be 14,847; Mg 7,731; Ca 4,910 — the enormous jump from IE₂→IE₃ explains why M³⁺ never forms **[JD Lee][aemsg.pdf]**.

**Why IE₁(Group 2) > IE₁(Group 1) same period, but IE₂(Group 2) < IE₂(Group 1) same period** [NCERT explicit statement, reproduced in Master Notes and S block.pdf]: Group 2 atoms are smaller (higher nuclear charge) than the corresponding Group 1 atom, so IE₁ is higher. But once Group 1 loses one electron it already has a noble-gas core, so removing a second electron from M⁺ (Group 1) is very costly; Group 2's IE₂ removes the *second* valence electron to *reach* a noble-gas configuration, which is comparatively less costly than removing an electron that would break a noble-gas core. Net effect: IE₂(AEM) < IE₂(AM) for same-period comparison. **[NCERT][S block.pdf boxed Q&A]**: "IE₁ of AM < IE₁ of AEM; IE₂ of AM > IE₂ of AEM — Reason: IE₁ of AEM is large due to increased nuclear charge in AEM as compared to AM but IE₂ of AM is large because the second electron in AM is to be removed from a cation which has already acquired noble gas configuration."

### 1.5 Hydration enthalpy
Order of magnitude: |ΔH_hyd| Be²⁺(−2494) > Mg²⁺(−1921) > Ca²⁺(−1577) > Sr²⁺(−1443) > Ba²⁺(−1305) kJ/mol **[all sources identical numbers]**. Group 2 hydration enthalpies are ~4–5× larger than the corresponding Group 1 ion **[JD Lee: "four or five times greater than for Group 1 ions"]**, due to smaller size and higher (2+) charge. In Be's case there is a further factor: formation of the very strong, well-defined complex [Be(H₂O)₄]²⁺ **[JD Lee]**. This is why Group 2 salts are far more extensively hydrated than Group 1 salts: MgCl₂·6H₂O, CaCl₂·6H₂O, BaCl₂·2H₂O exist while NaCl, KCl are anhydrous **[NCERT][JD Lee][S block.pdf]**. Number of hydrate waters *decreases* as the cation gets larger down the group (e.g., MgCl₂·6H₂O(older Master Notes gives ·8H₂O per JD Lee table 11.4 legend? — **note**: [JD Lee] text elsewhere in Group 1 chapter comparing to Mg gives MgCl₂·8H₂O in one place; the Group 2 chapter and [NCERT] both consistently state MgCl₂·6H₂O, CaCl₂·6H₂O, SrCl₂·6H₂O, BaCl₂·2H₂O — treat MgCl₂·6H₂O as the standard JEE answer).

### 1.6 Melting/boiling points, density, hardness
Group 2 metals are silvery white, harder and higher melting than Group 1 (two valence electrons participate in metallic bonding vs one) **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**. Be, Mg are somewhat greyish **[NCERT]**. Trend is **not monotonic/regular** — explicitly flagged in every source as a JEE trap: "the trend is, however, not systematic" **[NCERT][S block.pdf]**; "melting points do not vary regularly, mainly because the metals adopt different crystal structures" **[JD Lee]**.

m.p./K: Be 1560, Mg 924, Ca 1124, Sr 1062, Ba 1002, Ra 973.
b.p./K: Be 2745, Mg 1363, Ca 1767, Sr 1655, Ba 2078, Ra ~1973.
Density (g/cm³): Be 1.84, Mg 1.74, Ca 1.55, Sr 2.63, Ba 3.59, Ra ~5.5.
(All consistent across [NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes][S block.pdf].)

Group 2 metals adopt a body-centred-cubic-type or other lattice depending on element (JD Lee notes Group 1 is bcc uniformly with coordination 8; for Group 2 the varying crystal structures cause irregular m.p./b.p. trend — **[JD Lee]** general statement, no single lattice type given for Group 2 explicitly).

Cohesive energy / hardness reasoning [JD Lee, general — applies by extension]: hardness depends on number of bonding electrons; Group 2's two valence electrons per atom give roughly double the cohesive energy of Group 1, hence harder metals with higher m.p./b.p.

### 1.7 Standard reduction potentials (electropositive character / reducing power)
E°(M²⁺/M) in V: Be −1.85/−1.97 (two slightly different values appear: JD Lee text gives −1.85, but the standard data table in JD Lee/NCERT/Master Notes gives −1.97 — **both values appear in the literature; treat −1.97 as the standard tabulated value, −1.85 as the value JD Lee uses in the "Diagonal Relationship" discussion comparing to Al's −1.66 V**), Mg −2.36/−2.37, Ca −2.84/−2.87, Sr −2.89, Ba −2.92/−2.91, Ra −2.92.

Key qualitative points, consistent everywhere:
- Reducing power increases Be→Ba broadly, but Be is anomalous: **less negative** than the rest (least reducing) **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes][S block.pdf]**.
- Ca, Sr, Ba have E° values "similar to and quite high in the electrochemical series" compared to alkali metals **[JD Lee]**.
- Group 2 reducing power overall < Group 1 (corresponding period) **[NCERT: "their reducing power is less than the corresponding alkali metals"]**.
- Be's less-negative E° is explained as arising from **large hydration energy of small Be²⁺ + large atomization enthalpy of the metal working against ionization** — i.e., Be's poor reducing power is not due to weak thermodynamic driving force at the ion level but a combination of high atomization enthalpy (metallic bonding is strong/hard metal) **[NCERT explicit: "Beryllium has less negative value compared to other alkaline earth metals. However, its reducing nature is due to large hydration energy associated with the small size of Be²⁺ ion and relatively large value of the atomization enthalpy of the metal."]** — this NCERT sentence is important, precise JEE-quotable reasoning.
- E° is a net thermodynamic sum: atomisation/sublimation (ΔH_s) + IE₁ + IE₂ + hydration enthalpy, so **cannot be predicted from IE alone** **[Master Notes][JD Lee Born-Haber-type reasoning]**. This mirrors Group 1's classic Li vs Na/K puzzle (Li has most negative E° despite modest IE, due to huge hydration enthalpy from small size; but Li reacts *slowly* with water — kinetics not thermodynamics — **[NCERT][JD Lee]** — directly transferable reasoning pattern to Be/Mg passivation logic).

### 1.8 Flame colours (electron promotion energy reasoning)
Ca brick red, Sr crimson, Ba apple green; **Be and Mg show no characteristic flame colour** because their electrons are too strongly (tightly) bound to be excited by ordinary flame temperature **[NCERT][Master Notes][JD Lee][S block.pdf identical wording]**. Mechanism: flame heat excites outer electron to higher level; on relaxation, energy emitted as visible light (E = hν); used for qualitative flame test and quantitative flame photometry / atomic absorption spectroscopy **[NCERT][JD Lee]**.

[JD Lee Group 1 chapter, transferable reasoning]: colour can arise from short-lived species formed momentarily in the flame (not necessarily the ground-state ion) — e.g. Li's red line is attributed to a short-lived LiOH species. This same logic likely applies to Ca/Sr/Ba flame colours (excited atomic/molecular species), though sources don't detail the Group-2-specific species.

**JEE trap explicitly flagged [Master Notes]**: "Mg burns with dazzling white light" (combustion emission, i.e., MgO incandescence) must NOT be confused with "Mg²⁺ gives a characteristic flame-test colour" — Mg (like Be) shows no flame-test colour.

## 2. Reactivity toward Air / O₂ / N₂

- Be, Mg are **kinetically inert** toward O₂/water at room temperature due to a protective adherent oxide film **[NCERT: "Beryllium and magnesium are kinetically inert to oxygen and water because of the formation of an oxide film on their surface"][JD Lee][S block.pdf]**. Powdered Be burns brilliantly on ignition to give BeO + Be₃N₂ **[NCERT]**.
- Mg burns with dazzling brilliance in air giving MgO + Mg₃N₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee: "Mg burns with dazzling brilliance in air and evolves a lot of heat. This is used to start a thermite reaction with aluminium, and also to provide light in flash photography using light bulbs, not electronics"]**.
- Ca, Sr, Ba are readily attacked by air, forming oxide + nitride even at ordinary temperatures; peroxide tendency increases for larger cations, Ba especially (BaO₂ most stable/classical peroxide) **[NCERT][Master Notes][JD Lee]**.
- Normal oxide MO formed by all on burning in excess O₂; Ba also forms peroxide BaO₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee]**: "2M + O₂ → 2MO (normal oxide); Ba + O₂ → BaO₂ (peroxide)" **[JD Lee table 11.9]**.
- Mg reduces CO₂ when burning: 2Mg + CO₂ → 2MgO + C — **classic trap: CO₂ does NOT extinguish burning Mg** **[Master Notes][JD Lee general reducing-agent logic]**.

## 3. Reactivity toward Water

Table (all sources agree):
- Be: essentially inert to water/steam at ordinary conditions — protected by BeO; **JD Lee explicitly notes uncertainty**: "There is some doubt whether it reacts with steam to form the oxide BeO, or fails to react at all."
- Mg: intermediate — does not react with cold water; decomposes hot water slowly forming Mg(OH)₂+H₂; with steam gives MgO+H₂ (protective oxide layer normally prevents reaction unless removed, e.g. by Hg amalgamation — resembling Al's behaviour) **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**.
- Ca, Sr, Ba: react readily/vigorously with cold water, liberating H₂, forming M(OH)₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee]**: Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂.

**JEE trap [Master Notes]**: "Mg + steam → Mg(OH)₂" is wrong; correct steam product is MgO + H₂ (dehydration favoured at high T); Mg + hot liquid water → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂ is the liquid-water product. Condition (liquid vs steam) changes the isolated solid product.

## 4. Reactivity toward Dihydrogen (Hydrides)

All Group 2 elements except Be react directly with H₂ on heating to form MH₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee][S block.pdf]**. BeH₂ made indirectly: 2BeCl₂ + LiAlH₄ → 2BeH₂ + LiCl + AlCl₃ **[all sources, identical equation]**. Pure BeH₂ can also be made via BeCl₂ + 2Li[BH₄] → BeB₂H₈ + 2LiCl, then BeB₂H₈ + 2PPh₃ → BeH₂ + 2Ph₃PBH₃ (thermal decomposition in sealed tube with triphenylphosphine) **[JD Lee/aemsg.pdf — this two-step pure-sample route is not in NCERT or Master Notes; unique deep-source detail]**.

**Bonding of BeH₂ — full MOT/VB reasoning [JD Lee/aemsg.pdf, detailed; matches request for "MOT-based explanations to preserve"]:**
- Be has only 2 valence electrons (2s²) but must bond to 2 H atoms → not enough electrons for two normal 2-electron Be–H bonds if each Be–H needs its own electron pair from Be. Monomeric BeH₂ (normal 2c-2e bonds) would leave only 4 electrons in Be's outer shell (electron deficient).
- Real BeH₂ is **polymeric/electron-deficient**, with each Be bonded to **4 H atoms**, forming **three-centre two-electron (3c–2e) bonds** — described in JD Lee as "banana-shaped molecular orbital... covers three atoms Be···H···Be, and contains two electrons." This is explicitly called a **cluster compound**, and the phenomenon is termed "electron deficient" bonding, where each Be shares electron pairs with several neighbours **[JD Lee: "clustering is important in metals... and in the halides of the second and third row transition metals"]**.
- Solid BeH₂ exists in both amorphous and crystalline forms; gas-phase species may include polymeric chains and rings **[JD Lee]**.
- The diagram given: Be–H–Be bridge with a "banana bond" drawn as two overlapping lobes spanning Be···H···Be (Figure 11.2 in JD Lee).

**MOT/VB reasoning for gaseous linear BeX₂/BeH₂ (VB picture, from JD Lee 9.22 style anomalous-behaviour section):**
- Ground state Be: 1s² 2s² (2p empty) — no unpaired electrons, cannot form 2 covalent bonds directly.
- **Excited state**: one 2s electron promoted to 2p → 1s² 2s¹ 2p¹ → **two unpaired electrons available for sp hybridization**, allowing formation of two collinear (180°) bonds → **linear BeX₂ molecule in the gas phase** (sp hybridisation) **[JD Lee: "two unpaired electrons can form bonds with two X atoms — linear molecule (sp hybridization)"]**.
- This linear/monomeric form exists **only in the gas phase**; in the solid, four-fold coordination is always achieved via polymerisation/complex formation (see BeCl₂ structures below) because the gas-phase sp arrangement leaves the outer shell unfilled (only 4 electrons around Be, i.e., electron-deficient) **[JD Lee]**.
- Hydrated Be²⁺ ion [Be(H₂O)₄]²⁺: Be²⁺ (1s²2s⁰2p⁰, no electrons in valence shell) accepts 4 electron pairs from 4 O atoms of water → **sp³ hybridisation, tetrahedral, coordination number 4** **[JD Lee, explicit orbital-box diagram]**. This is the standard MOT/VB picture for why Be²⁺(aq) and most Be complexes are tetrahedral (never 6-coordinate like the other Group 2 ions, because Be has only 4 valence orbitals total: one 2s + three 2p — no d-orbitals available) **[JD Lee][NCERT: "The remaining members of the group can have a coordination number of six by using d-orbitals"]**.

Reactivity of hydrides: CaH₂, SrH₂, BaH₂ are **ionic** (contain H⁻); BeH₂ and MgH₂ are **covalent/polymeric** **[NCERT][JD Lee: "CaH₂, SrH₂ and BaH₂ are ionic, and contain the hydride ion H⁻. Beryllium and magnesium hydrides are covalent and polymeric"]**. All hydrides are reducing agents, hydrolysed by water/dilute acids liberating H₂: CaH₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + 2H₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. CaH₂ nicknamed "hydrolith" **[Master Notes]**.

## 5. Reactivity toward Halogens — Halide Chemistry

General: M + X₂ → MX₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. Except beryllium halides, all Group 2 halides are ionic **[NCERT][S block.pdf]**.

### 5.1 BeCl₂/BeF₂ preparation
- BeF₂: thermal decomposition of (NH₄)₂BeF₄ **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**: BeO + 2NH₃ + 4HF → (NH₄)₂BeF₄ →(heat) BeF₂ + 2NH₄F.
- BeCl₂: BeO + C + Cl₂ →(600–800 K) BeCl₂ + CO **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes — all cite same equation and temperature range]**; also 2BeO + CCl₄ →(800°C) 2BeCl₂ + CO₂ **[JD Lee, additional route not in NCERT]**.
- Anhydrous Be halides **cannot** be obtained by dehydrating the hydrate [Be(H₂O)₄]Cl₂ — attempts cause hydrolysis: [Be(H₂O)₄]Cl₂ →(heat) Be(OH)₂ + 2HCl + 2H₂O **[JD Lee — precise mechanism/equation, valuable JEE-reasoning detail matching Master Notes' general statement about MgCl₂ hydrolysis on heating]**.

### 5.2 BeCl₂ structure — solid/dimer/monomer (full detail)
- **Solid state**: infinite **chain polymer** with **chlorine bridges**; each Cl forms one normal covalent bond to one Be and donates a lone pair (coordinate bond) to the neighbouring Be **[NCERT diagram][JD Lee][Master Notes][S block.pdf — all draw the same zig-zag Be–Cl–Be–Cl chain]**.
- **Vapour phase, moderate T**: chloro-bridged **dimer** (Be₂Cl₄), which **dissociates into the linear monomer at high temperatures ~1200 K** **[NCERT: "In the vapour phase BeCl₂ tends to form a chloro-bridged dimer which dissociates into the linear monomer at high temperatures of the order of 1200 K"][JD Lee — same figure with monomer/dimer/polymer three structures explicitly drawn, Figure 11.3]**.
- Electron deficiency of 2-coordinate Be in the monomer is compensated by bridging in the solid/dimer (same electron-deficient/cluster-bonding logic as BeH₂) **[JD Lee]**.
- BeCl₂ (and BeMe₂) are Lewis acidic: accept extra ligand lone pairs to form 4-coordinate species, e.g. [BeCl₄]²⁻, [BeF₄]²⁻, or BeCl₂·D₂ adducts with ethers/ketones/aldehydes (D = O-donor solvent) **[JD Lee][NCERT: BeCl₂ + 2Cl⁻ → [BeCl₄]²⁻ type logic generalized in Master Notes]**.
- **Be(Me)₂ polymer** — same chain structure as (BeCl₂)ₙ, but bonding is different: **three-centre two-electron bonds** covering one Me (CH₃) group and two Be atoms (cf. BeH₂ bonding, NOT simple halogen-bridge lone-pair donation) **[JD Lee: "The bonding in Be—Me—Be is best described as two electrons forming a three-centre bridge bond involving both Be atoms and the CH₃ group... This is different from the halogen bridging in (BeCl₂)ₙ where the bridging chlorine atom forms two normal two-electron bonds"]** — this is a key nuanced MOT-type distinction: halide bridges = lone-pair donor (dative) bonds vs alkyl bridges = genuine 3c-2e electron-deficient bonds, analogous to diborane.

### 5.3 Fajans'-rule / covalency reasoning
Covalent character: BeX₂ > MgX₂ > CaX₂ > SrX₂ > BaX₂ (small, highly polarising Be²⁺ distorts anion electron cloud) **[Master Notes][JD Lee: Fajans' rules explicitly invoked in the "Anomalous Behaviour of Beryllium" section: "small highly charged ions tend to form covalent compounds"]**. For fixed cation, covalent character increases with anion polarisability: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ **[Master Notes]**. Electronegativity difference reasoning **[JD Lee explicit]**: BeF₂ has the *largest* electronegativity difference of any Be compound (2.5) and so is the **most ionic** Be compound, yet even BeF₂ is regarded as covalent when fused (very low conductivity) — showing how "small but high charge density" wins over "large electronegativity difference" in determining bond character for Be specifically.

### 5.4 Hydrated halides / hydration trend
Tendency to form halide hydrates decreases down the group: MgCl₂·6H₂O(some sources ·8H₂O in Group-1-chapter cross-reference), CaCl₂·6H�2O, SrCl₂·6H₂O, BaCl₂·2H₂O **[NCERT][JD Lee — near-identical wording]**. Dehydration of Ca/Sr/Ba hydrated halides achievable by simple heating; **hydrated halides of Be and Mg undergo hydrolysis on heating instead** (cannot get anhydrous salt by simply heating the hydrate) **[NCERT explicit][JD Lee gives explicit MgCl₂·6H₂O →Δ→ Mg(OH)Cl + HCl + 5H₂O then Mg(OH)Cl → MgO + HCl, from S block.pdf "Few Important Points" — valuable extra detail not in NCERT/Master Notes]**. Anhydrous MgCl₂ industrially prepared instead by heating a double salt MgCl₂·NH₄Cl·6H₂O (loses H₂O first, then NH₄Cl driven off at higher T, avoiding hydrolysis) **[S block.pdf — unique practical detail]**.

### 5.5 Fluoride solubility
Fluorides of the heavier metals are relatively insoluble/have high lattice energy (small F⁻); **BeF₂ is exceptional — very soluble in water** owing to the very high solvation energy of Be in forming [Be(H₂O)₄]²⁺ **[JD Lee explicit][Master Notes flags "handle fluoride solubility with care"]**. CaF₂ is the industrially crucial exception (main source of F₂ and HF): CaF₂ + H₂SO₄ → 2HF + CaSO₄; then HF + KF → electrolysis → F₂ **[JD Lee — the classic industrial preparation route, absent from NCERT/Master Notes]**.

## 6. Reactivity toward Acids and Alkalis

M + 2HCl → MCl₂ + H₂ for Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. Be reacts slowly/only after oxide film removed; Be, like Al, is **rendered passive by concentrated HNO₃** (forms a thin protective oxide layer) **[JD Lee explicit: "Be is rendered passive by concentrated HNO₃... it forms a very thin layer of oxide on the surface of the metal, which protects it from further attack by the acid"][Master Notes notes "very dilute HNO₃, Mg can evolve H₂" as an often-tested exception]**.

Be is **amphoteric**, dissolving in NaOH: Be + 2NaOH + 2H₂O → Na₂[Be(OH)₄] + H₂ (sodium beryllate) **[NCERT][JD Lee][S block.pdf — identical equation everywhere]**. Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba do **not** react with NaOH — "purely basic" **[JD Lee][S block.pdf]**.

## 7. Reducing Nature and Solutions in Liquid Ammonia

Ca, Sr, Ba dissolve in liquid NH₃ to give **deep blue** solutions (bright blue at dilute concentration) due to **solvated/ammoniated electrons** — same phenomenon as Group 1 **[NCERT][JD Lee][S block.pdf]**. Mg is much less soluble under ordinary conditions; **Be does not show this behaviour typically** **[Master Notes]**. Equation: M + (x+y)NH₃ → [M(NH₃)ₓ]²⁺ + 2[e(NH₃)ᵧ]⁻ **[NCERT][JD Lee — identical, except NCERT excludes Be, Mg explicitly in the general Group 2 equation]**.

- **Concentrated solutions**: bronze-coloured, due to formation of metal clusters **[JD Lee]**.
- Solutions decompose slowly, evolving H₂ and forming **amides** (not simply "amide" generically — for Group 2, evaporation gives **hexammoniates** first: M(NH₃)₆ → M(NH₂)₂ + 4NH₃ + H₂ **[JD Lee — precise, distinguishes Group 2 behaviour: "evaporation of ammonia from solutions of Group 1 metals yields the metal, but with Group 2 metals evaporation of ammonia gives hexammoniates of the metals. These slowly decompose to give amides"]**. This is a genuinely useful nuance beyond the Master Notes' simpler M+2NH₃→M(NH₂)₂+H₂ overall equation.
- Reaction is accelerated by transition metals/their compounds (catalytic decomposition) **[JD Lee]**.
- Overall MOT-adjacent reasoning: 2NH₃ + 2e⁻ → 2NH₂⁻ + H₂ (ammoniated electron acts as reducing agent/strong base) **[JD Lee]**.

## 8. Nitrides

All Group 2 elements burn in N₂ to form ionic nitrides M₃N₂ (in contrast to Group 1, where only Li forms a nitride, Li₃N) **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**. Reason lattice energy is large enough to drive N₂ → N³⁻ conversion despite N≡N bond strength, because M²⁺ and N³⁻ both carry high charge → very high lattice energy **[JD Lee: "the large amount of energy required comes from the very large amount of lattice energy evolved when the crystalline solid is formed. The lattice energy is particularly high because of the high charges on the ions M²⁺ and N³⁻"]** — directly parallels why Li (uniquely small among Group 1) forms Li₃N.

Be₃N₂ is comparatively **volatile** (greater covalent tendency of Be) — other nitrides are not volatile **[JD Lee]**. All nitrides decompose on heating and hydrolyse in water liberating NH₃, forming the metal hydroxide: Ca₃N₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2NH₃ **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**.

## 9. Carbides

- **Be₂C**: made by heating BeO with C at 1900–2000°C; brick-red, ionic, has an antifluorite structure with C⁴⁻ ("methanide")-type anions; hydrolyses to give **methane**: Be₂C + 4H₂O → 2Be(OH)₂ + CH₄ **[JD Lee — precise prep conditions and structure type][NCERT/Master Notes give only the hydrolysis equation]**.
- **CaC₂ (and SrC₂, BaC₂)**: "acetylide," contains C₂²⁻; MC₂ adopts a **distorted sodium chloride-type structure** (M²⁺ replaces Na⁺, C≡C²⁻ replaces Cl⁻; the C₂²⁻ ion is not spherical like Cl⁻, so the lattice is distorted along the axis where the ion is aligned) **[JD Lee — a genuinely deep structural point absent elsewhere]**. Above 450°C the anion adopts random orientations and the cell becomes tetragonal. Made by Ca + 2C →(1100°C) CaC₂, or CaO + 3C →(2000°C) CaC₂ + CO **[JD Lee, both routes; NCERT/Master Notes give the electric-furnace CaO+3C route only]**. Hydrolysis: CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂ **[all sources]**.
- **Mg₂C₃**: contains C₃⁴⁻; hydrolyses to **propyne** (methylacetylene) CH₃C≡CH **[NCERT does not mention this; JD Lee/Master Notes both give it — good cross-check]**. On heating, MgC₂ (calcium-carbide-analogue) converts to Mg₂C₃ **[JD Lee]**.
- CaC₂ + N₂ →(1100°C, electric furnace) CaNCN (calcium cyanamide) + C — **an important industrial N₂-fixation reaction** (alternative to Haber process) **[JD Lee — extensive coverage: cyanamide ion [N=C=N]²⁻ is isoelectronic with CO₂ and linear; CaNCN used as slow-acting nitrogenous fertilizer, advantage over NH₄NO₃/urea being it's not washed away by rain; further reactions: CaNCN + H₂SO₄ → H₂NCN + CaSO₄ (cyanamide); CaNCN + CO₂ + H₂O → H₂NCN + CaCO₃; H₂NCN + H₂O →(pH<2 or >12) urea H₂N·CO·NH₂; H₂NCN + H₂S → thiourea H₂N·CS·NH₂; H₂NCN →(pH 7-9) dicyanamide →(pyrolysis) melamine]** — this whole industrial nitrogen-fixation/fertilizer/plastics thread is unique to JD Lee/aemsg.pdf and not present in NCERT or Master Notes; interesting enrichment for a "beyond NCERT" JEE Advanced answer.
- Note: BaC₂ reacting with N₂ gives a **cyanide** Ba(CN)₂, NOT a cyanamide — an explicitly flagged exception **[JD Lee: "It is interesting that BaC₂ also reacts with N₂, but it forms a cyanide Ba(CN)₂, not a cyanamide (NCN)²⁻"]**.

## 10. Complexes

Group 2 metals are "not noted for their ability to form complexes" generally, but are **better at forming complexes than Group 1** because their divalent ions are smaller (higher charge density) **[JD Lee]**. Be is appreciably smaller than the rest, so Be forms many complexes; only Mg and Ca show much tendency to complex in solution, usually with O-donor ligands **[JD Lee][NCERT does not cover this section]**.

- **BeF₂ + 2F⁻ → [BeF₄]²⁻** (tetrafluoroberyllate); M₂[BeF₄] salts resemble sulphates in properties **[JD Lee]**.
- **BeCl₂·D₂** adducts (D = ether/aldehyde/ketone, O-donor) — tetrahedral **[JD Lee]**.
- Chelates: **beryllium oxalate [Be(ox)₂]²⁻**, and complexes with β-diketones (acetylacetone) and catechol — all tetrahedral Be²⁺ **[JD Lee]**.
- **Basic beryllium acetate [Be₄O(CH₃COO)₆]**: unusual structure — central O²⁻ surrounded tetrahedrally by 4 Be atoms at the corners of a tetrahedron, 6 acetate groups bridge along the 6 edges. Covalent, low m.p. (285°C)/b.p. (330°C), soluble in organic solvents, **can be distilled — useful for purification of beryllium** **[JD Lee — detailed, valuable industrial/structural point, absent from other sources]**.
- **Basic beryllium nitrate [Be₄O(NO₃)₆]**: same tetrahedral Be₄O core, but NO₃⁻ groups act as **bidentate bridging ligands** between pairs of Be atoms (chelate-type bridging) **[JD Lee/aemsg.pdf — precise structural description with figure references]**. Prepared: BeCl₂ →(N₂O₄) Be(NO₃)₂·2N₂O₄ →(warm to 50°C under vacuum) Be(NO₃)₂ →(125°C) [Be₄O(NO₃)₆].
- Be salts said to "taste sweet" but this must **never be tested** — Be compounds are highly toxic; toxicity arises partly from Be's strong complexing ability, which lets it **displace Mg from enzymes** in the body; contact with skin causes dermatitis; inhaling Be dust/smoke causes **berylliosis** (a disease resembling silicosis) **[JD Lee — explicit safety/toxicity note, worth flagging as JEE-adjacent general knowledge, and matches "Be and Ba compounds are all very toxic" from the JD Lee chapter introduction]**.
- Mg forms a few halide complexes like [NEt₄]₂[MgCl₄], but Ca, Sr, Ba do not form comparable halide complexes **[JD Lee]**.
- **Chlorophyll**: Mg at centre of a flat heterocyclic porphyrin ring, bonded to 4 N atoms; absorbs light in red region; drives photosynthesis 6CO₂ + 6H₂O →(chlorophyll, sunlight)→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ **[JD Lee — gives the full photosynthesis equation, richer than NCERT's brief mention][NCERT/Master Notes/S block.pdf mention Mg-chlorophyll link but without the explicit glucose equation]**.
- **EDTA complexation** of Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺: Ca²⁺ + [H₂EDTA]²⁻ → [Ca(EDTA)]²⁻ + 2H⁺; EDTA gives **six-coordinate** complexes with most metal ions (octahedral), but **Be is invariably four-coordinate and does not complex appreciably with EDTA** **[JD Lee — a nice contrast point: Be's small size that normally drives strong complexation actually excludes it from EDTA chemistry because EDTA needs 6 coordination sites]**. EDTA titrations of Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ (water hardness estimation) performed at **higher pH** than for other metals (e.g. Zn²⁺, Cd²⁺, Pb²⁺) because Ca/Mg-EDTA complexes are less stable — at low pH, EDTA gets protonated instead of complexing **[JD Lee]**.

## 11. Oxides

- All burn in O₂ to give MO (rock-salt structure, except BeO) **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. BeO covalent (4:4 zinc-blende/wurtzite structure); MgO, CaO, SrO, BaO ionic (6:6 NaCl structure) **[JD Lee explicit structure types, Table 11.10 radius-ratio predictions vs found — predicted CN for SrO/BaO is 8 by simple radius-ratio rules but the actual found CN is 6 in both cases, illustrating the **limits of the radius-ratio rule** — genuinely advanced JEE-relevant caveat not found in NCERT/Master Notes]**.
- Basicity: BeO amphoteric < MgO weakly basic < CaO < SrO < BaO increasingly strongly basic **[all sources]**.
- BeO amphoteric: BeO + 2H⁺ → Be²⁺ + H₂O; BeO + 2OH⁻ + H₂O → [Be(OH)₄]²⁻ **[NCERT][Master Notes]**.
- BeO/MgO used as **refractories** for furnace linings due to: high m.p. (BeO ≈2500°C, MgO ≈2800°C), very low vapour pressure, good thermal conductor, chemical inertness, electrical insulator **[JD Lee — this exact 5-point list, valuable as a ready-made JEE answer]**.
- Basic strength order confirmed quantitatively by carbonate-decomposition temperatures (see §12 below), a nice cross-link JD Lee draws explicitly between oxide basicity and carbonate thermal stability.

### Peroxides
- Stability/ease of formation of peroxides **increases with cation size** (larger, less polarising cation better stabilises the large, diffuse O₂²⁻ ion) **[Master Notes][JD Lee]**.
- BaO₂: 2BaO + O₂ ⇌ 2BaO₂ (passing air over BaO at 500°C) **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. SrO₂ similarly but needs high pressure+temperature. CaO₂ **not** formed this way — instead made as the hydrate by treating Ca(OH)₂ with H₂O₂, then dehydrating **[JD Lee — this preparative nuance for CaO₂ is not in NCERT/Master Notes]**. Crude MgO₂ made using H₂O₂; **no peroxide of beryllium is known** **[JD Lee explicit — "no peroxide of beryllium is known"]**.
- Peroxides = white ionic solids containing [O–O]²⁻, salts of the very weak acid H₂O₂; acid liberates H₂O₂: BaO₂ + 2HCl → BaCl₂ + H₂O₂ (also with dilute H₂SO₄ giving BaSO₄↓ + H₂O₂, precipitation-driven) **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**.
- **MOT bond-order treatment of peroxide/superoxide ions [JD Lee, Group 1 chapter — directly transferable/reusable for Group 2 peroxide discussion]**:
  - Peroxide ion [O–O]²⁻ has 18 electrons: σ1s², σ*1s², σ2s², σ*2s², σ2pₓ², π2p_y² π2p_z², π*2p_y² π*2p_z² → **bond order = 1** (single bond), consistent with the O–O single bond drawn in structural formulas, diamagnetic.
  - Superoxide ion [O₂]⁻ has 17 electrons: same filling except π*2p_y² π*2p_z¹ (one unpaired electron in π*) → **bond order = 1.5**, **paramagnetic** (explains why superoxides like KO₂ are paramagnetic — direct answer to the classic "why is KO₂ paramagnetic" JEE question, reproduced also in NCERT Problem 10.3 and S block.pdf).
  - This MOT bond-order approach can be extended by analogy for Group 2 peroxides (BaO₂ etc., same peroxide ion, same bond order = 1, diamagnetic) even though the Group 2 chapters don't restate the MOT diagram explicitly (it's given once in the Group 1 chapter, cf. "The bonding in peroxides and superoxides is described in the examples of molecular orbital treatment in Chapter 4" — JD Lee cross-reference).

## 12. Hydroxides

Basicity/solubility both increase down the group; Be(OH)₂ amphoteric, Mg(OH)₂ weakly basic (used to treat acid indigestion — "milk of magnesia"), Ca(OH)₂/Sr(OH)₂/Ba(OH)₂ strong bases **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**.

Quantitative solubility data **[JD Lee — precise numbers not in other sources]**: Mg(OH)₂ ≈1×10⁻⁴ g/L (extremely insoluble) at 20°C; Ca(OH)₂ ≈2 g/L; Sr(OH)₂ ≈8 g/L; Ba(OH)₂ ≈39 g/L.

Be(OH)₂ amphoteric: Be(OH)₂ + 2OH⁻ → [Be(OH)₄]²⁻ (beryllate ion); Be(OH)₂ + 2HCl + 2H₂O → [Be(H₂O)₄]Cl₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee — slightly different but equivalent equation forms]**.

**Why hydroxide solubility increases down the group** — the canonical JEE-Advanced reasoning, stated identically as a "Problem" in NCERT and S block.pdf: "the anion (OH⁻) is common; cationic radius influences the lattice enthalpy; since lattice enthalpy decreases much more than hydration enthalpy with increasing ionic size, solubility increases down the group" **[NCERT Problem 10.4/S block.pdf Problem 10.4, verbatim reasoning]**. JD Lee frames this as a **general competing-factors principle** applicable to all Group 2 salts: "Solubility of most salts decreases with increased atomic weight though the usual trend is reversed with the fluorides and hydroxides in this group... With most compounds, on descending the group, the hydration energy decreases more rapidly than the lattice energy: hence the compounds become less soluble as the metal gets larger. However, with fluorides and hydroxides the lattice energy decreases more rapidly than the hydration energy, and so their solubility increases on descending the group." This single paragraph is the master key explaining ALL Group 2 solubility trends (carbonate/sulphate decreasing vs fluoride/hydroxide increasing) — an extremely high-value synthesis sentence for the final notes.

Solutions of Ca(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂ called **lime water** and **baryta water** respectively; both used to test/detect CO₂ (turn milky/turbid due to CaCO₃/BaCO₃; excess CO₂ redissolves via bicarbonate) **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**. **Baryta water is more sensitive** — even exhaled breath on it gives a positive test, whereas with lime water, breath must be *blown through* the solution as bubbles **[JD Lee — a nice practical/qualitative-analysis nuance, absent elsewhere]**.

Thermal stability of hydroxides increases down the group (M(OH)₂ → MO + H₂O on heating); small cations polarise OH⁻ more strongly, favouring dehydration **[Master Notes][NCERT — implicit in "Mg(OH)₂ decomposes more readily than Ba(OH)₂"]**.

## 13. Carbonates and Bicarbonates

- Normal carbonates: insoluble/sparingly soluble, precipitated by adding Na₂CO₃/(NH₄)₂CO₃ to a soluble Group 2 salt solution **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. Solubility **decreases** down the group as atomic number of M increases **[NCERT][JD Lee]**.
- **All carbonates decompose on heating** to CO₂ + oxide: MCO₃ → MO + CO₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**.
- **BeCO₃ is unstable and can only be kept in an atmosphere of CO₂**; unusual because it contains the hydrated ion [Be(H₂O)₄]²⁺ rather than simple Be²⁺ **[NCERT][JD Lee — both state this unusual hydrated-ion feature of BeCO₃, valuable JEE-Advanced-level detail]**.
- **Quantitative decomposition temperatures [JD Lee — precise numbers, unique deep-source data]**: BeCO₃ <100°C, MgCO₃ 540°C, CaCO₃ 900°C, SrCO₃ 1290°C, BaCO₃ 1360°C. These numbers make the qualitative "thermal stability increases down the group" trend concrete and are excellent for a numerical/graded-answer JEE Advanced question.
- Thermal stability reasoning: small cation polarises CO₃²⁻ strongly, promoting decomposition into more compact O²⁻ + CO₂; polarising power falls down the group so stability rises **[Master Notes][JD Lee general polarisation logic]**.
- **CaCO₃ crystal chemistry — calcite vs aragonite [JD Lee — genuinely advanced solid-state detail, absent from NCERT/Master Notes]**: two crystalline forms occur naturally. Calcite is the more stable form; each Ca²⁺ is surrounded by 6 O atoms from CO₃²⁻ ions (i.e., CN=6, matches the CaO-type radius-ratio prediction). Aragonite is a **metastable** form, standard enthalpy of formation only ~5 kJ/mol higher than calcite, but a high activation energy barrier prevents spontaneous conversion to calcite at room T. In aragonite, Ca²⁺ is surrounded by **9 oxygen atoms** — an unusually high coordination number. Aragonite can be made in the lab by precipitating from a hot solution.
- **Hydrogencarbonates (bicarbonates)**: exist only in solution (not isolable solids for Group 2, similar to Group 1's Mg/Li exception) **[Master Notes][JD Lee]**. CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq); reverse on boiling/heating: Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + CO₂ + H₂O **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. This underlies **stalactite/stalagmite formation** in limestone caves — water percolating through limestone carries dissolved Ca(HCO₃)₂; slow decomposition to insoluble CaCO₃ deposits the mineral, growing stalactites down from the roof and stalagmites up from the floor **[JD Lee — vivid, exam-friendly geological application, not covered in NCERT/Master Notes in this much narrative detail]**.
- **Hardness of water [JD Lee — comprehensive treatment beyond NCERT]**:
  - *Temporary hardness*: due to Mg(HCO₃)₂ and Ca(HCO₃)₂; removed by boiling (drives off CO₂, decomposing bicarbonate to insoluble carbonate) — 2HCO₃⁻ ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + CO₂ + H₂O. Also removable by adding slaked lime ("lime softening"): Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Ca(OH)₂ ⇌ 2CaCO₃ + 2H�2O; operating at pH 10.5 nearly completely removes HCO₃⁻-caused hardness.
  - *Permanent hardness*: due mainly to MgSO₄/CaSO₄ in solution; **not** removed by boiling. Removed by distillation, ion-exchange resins, or adding phosphates (trisodium phosphate Na₃PO₄, sodium pyrophosphate Na₄P₂O₇, sodium tripolyphosphate Na₅P₃O₁₀, or Graham's salt/calgon (NaPO₃)ₙ) which sequester/complex Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺, keeping them in solution without scale formation. Also removable industrially by adding Na₂CO₃ (lime-soda process): CaSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄.
  - Scum formation: hard-water Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ react with soap's stearate ions to form an insoluble scum of calcium/magnesium stearate before lather can form.

## 14. Sulphates

Solubility decreases sharply: BeSO₄ > MgSO₄ ≫ CaSO₄ > SrSO₄ > BaSO₄ (virtually insoluble) **[NCERT][JD Lee — JD Lee explicitly uses "≫" between MgSO₄ and CaSO₄, capturing the sharp break in the trend][Master Notes]**. Reason: high hydration enthalpy of small Be²⁺/Mg²⁺ overcomes lattice enthalpy, favouring dissolution; for larger cations hydration falls off while lattice energy for the large SO₄²⁻ ion stays comparatively high, so solubility collapses **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**.

Epsom salt MgSO₄·7H₂O used as a mild laxative **[JD Lee][NCERT — "Epsom salt" phrase appears in JD Lee only, but the medicinal use is general knowledge]**. Alabaster: fine-grained CaSO₄·2H₂O, shiny like marble, used for ornaments — cannot be kept outdoors because CaSO₄ is slightly soluble (2 g/L) **[JD Lee — unique detail]**. BaSO₄ insoluble AND opaque to X-rays — used as a "barium meal" for diagnostic imaging of stomach/duodenum **[NCERT][JD Lee — identical medical application, good exam-ready fact]**.

Thermal decomposition: MSO₄ → MO + SO₃ on strong heating; **more basic metal → more thermally stable sulphate** (same logic as carbonates) **[JD Lee]**. **Quantitative decomposition temperatures [JD Lee — unique numbers]**: BeSO₄ 500°C, MgSO₄ 895°C, CaSO₄ 1149°C, SrSO₄ 1374°C (BaSO₄ presumably even higher, not given numerically but implied most stable). Heating sulphates with carbon reduces them to sulphides: BaSO₄ + 4C → BaS + 4CO — **most barium compounds industrially are made starting from BaS** **[JD Lee]**.

Calcium sulphate hemihydrate = Plaster of Paris, CaSO₄·½H₂O (see §16).

## 15. Nitrates

Made by dissolving carbonate/oxide/hydroxide in dilute HNO₃; MgCO₃ + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + CO₂ + H₂O-type reaction (general) **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. Anhydrous nitrates cannot be obtained by simply heating the hydrated crystal (decomposes to oxide instead); must be prepared using **liquid dinitrogen tetroxide (N₂O₄) and ethyl acetate** **[JD Lee — unique preparative method, absent from NCERT/Master Notes]**, exemplified for Be: BeCl₂ →(N₂O₄)→ Be(NO₃)₂·2N₂O₄ →(warm to 50°C, vacuum)→ Be(NO₃)₂ →(125°C)→ basic beryllium nitrate [Be₄O(NO₃)₆] (this last step shows Be is **unique** in the group in forming a *basic* nitrate in addition to the normal salt) **[JD Lee explicit: "Beryllium is unusual in that it forms a basic nitrate in addition to the normal salt"]**.

Hydration tendency of the crystalline nitrate decreases down the group: Mg(NO₃)₂ crystallises with 6 H₂O; Ba(NO₃)₂ crystallises anhydrous **[NCERT][JD Lee — identical wording/example]**.

All decompose on heating to the oxide (like LiNO₃, unlike the nitrite-forming pattern of heavier Group 1 nitrates): 2M(NO₃)₂ → 2MO + 4NO₂ + O₂ **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes — flagged explicitly as a JEE trap: "Group 2 nitrates do not follow the common NaNO₃/KNO₃ nitrite-forming pattern"]**.

## 16. Calcium Compound System (NCERT primary scope)

### 16.1 CaO — Quicklime
- Prep: CaCO₃ →(1070–1270 K, rotary kiln, commercial) CaO + CO₂; CO₂ removed as formed to drive completion (Le Chatelier) **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes][S block.pdf — identical temperature range everywhere]**.
- White amorphous solid, m.p. 2870 K, absorbs atmospheric moisture and CO₂ on exposure **[NCERT]**.
- Slaking: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (+heat) — "addition of limited amount of water breaks the lump of lime" **[NCERT][JD Lee]**. Quicklime slaked with soda gives solid **soda lime** (mixture of NaOH and Ca(OH)₂, made from quicklime and aqueous NaOH; easier to handle than pure NaOH) **[NCERT][JD Lee — JD Lee gives the same definition]**.
- Basic oxide, combines with acidic oxides at high T: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃; 6CaO + P₄O₁₀ → 2Ca₃(PO₄)₂ **[NCERT][Master Notes — identical equations]**.
- CaO + 2NH₄Cl → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃ + H₂O (heat) **[Master Notes]**.
- Uses (**NCERT-listed, learn first**): (i) important primary material for manufacturing cement, cheapest form of alkali; (ii) manufacture of sodium carbonate from caustic soda; (iii) purification of sugar and manufacture of dyestuffs **[NCERT verbatim][S block.pdf identical]**.
- Additional uses [JD Lee]: (1) steelmaking — removes phosphates and silicates as slag; (2) mixed with SiO₂/alumina to make cement; (3) glassmaking; (4) water softening; (5) making CaC₂; (6) making slaked lime.

### 16.2 Ca(OH)₂ — Slaked lime
- Prep: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ **[NCERT]**. White amorphous powder, sparingly soluble in water; **aqueous solution = lime water; suspension of excess solid = milk of lime** **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes — all three define these terms identically, crucial "do not confuse" table]**.
- CO₂ two-stage reaction: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O (milky); excess CO₂: CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → Ca(HCO₃)₂ (soluble, milkiness clears) **[NCERT][JD Lee]**.
- Reaction with Cl₂ → bleaching powder: 2Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → CaCl₂ + Ca(OCl)₂ + 2H₂O (simplified school representation) or written as 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → Ca(OCl)Cl·Ca(OH)₂·CaCl₂·2H₂O (the more realistic mixed formula per JD Lee: "bleaching powder is made by passing Cl₂ into slaked lime, and is often written as Ca(OCl)₂, but it is really a mixture") **[NCERT gives Ca(OCl)₂; JD Lee explicitly notes this is a simplification and gives the fuller mixed-salt formula — good nuance for JEE Advanced to note "bleaching powder is not a single pure compound"][Master Notes echoes this caution]**.
- Whitewashing: applied as suspension; hardens via **chemical carbonation** to CaCO₃, not merely drying **[Master Notes — detailed mechanism][NCERT: "used in white wash due to its disinfectant nature"]**.
- Mortar: Ca(OH)₂ + sand + water; curing = water loss (initial stiffness) + slow carbonation of Ca(OH)₂ to CaCO₃ (durable binder); sand reduces shrinkage **[Master Notes][JD Lee mortar composition: 1 part slaked lime : 3 parts sand, per S block.pdf "Few Important Points"]**.
- Uses (NCERT): mortar (building material); whitewash (disinfectant nature); glass making, tanning industry, preparation of bleaching powder, purification of sugar **[NCERT verbatim][S block.pdf identical]**.

### 16.3 CaCO₃
- Natural forms: limestone, chalk, marble; calcite/aragonite crystal forms **[NCERT][JD Lee — see §13 above for detailed calcite/aragonite structural chemistry]**.
- Prep: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O; or CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaCl **[NCERT]**. Excess CO₂ must be avoided (converts to soluble bicarbonate) **[NCERT][Master Notes]**.
- White fluffy powder, almost insoluble in water; decomposes at 1200 K to CaO + CO₂ **[NCERT]**.
- Reacts with dilute acid liberating CO₂: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂; CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂ (but note: with H₂SO₄, insoluble CaSO₄ can coat the surface and slow the reaction — passivation effect) **[NCERT gives the equation; Master Notes adds the passivation/kinetic nuance]**.
- Uses (NCERT): building material as marble; manufacture of quicklime; flux (with MgCO₃) in extraction of metals such as iron; specially precipitated CaCO₃ used extensively in high-quality paper manufacture; antacid; mild abrasive in toothpaste; constituent of chewing gum; filler in cosmetics **[NCERT verbatim, reproduced identically in S block.pdf]**.

### 16.4 Calcium Sulphate System — Gypsum / Plaster of Paris / Anhydrite
- Gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O; Plaster of Paris (hemihydrate) CaSO₄·½H₂O; anhydrous/"dead-burnt" CaSO₄ **[all sources]**.
- Prep of PoP: 2(CaSO₄·2H₂O) →(393 K)→ 2(CaSO₄·½H₂O) + 3H₂O — controlled partial dehydration **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes — same 393 K value]**. JD Lee gives a fuller three-stage sequence with temperatures: CaSO₄·2H₂O →(150°C)→ CaSO₄·½H₂O (plaster of Paris) →(200°C)→ CaSO₄ (anhydrite) →(1100°C)→ CaO + SO₃ — **the most granular temperature staging found across all sources**, useful for precise JEE Advanced numerical/graph questions.
- Setting: CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (sets into hard solid in 5–15 minutes on mixing with correct water amount) **[NCERT][Master Notes gives the detailed dissolution–supersaturation–recrystallisation mechanism: hemihydrate dissolves slightly, solution becomes supersaturated w.r.t. gypsum, gypsum nuclei form and grow as interlocking needle/plate crystals, converting plastic paste to rigid mass; excess water evaporates later leaving pores that weaken the set mass — this detailed mechanism is Master Notes' own enrichment, not explicitly spelled out step-by-step in NCERT/JD Lee/S block.pdf, though JD Lee's general lattice/hydration-energy framework is consistent with it]**.
- "Dead burnt" = fully anhydrous CaSO₄ from strong heating above 393 K — loses the ability to set normally because rehydration becomes too slow **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes]**.
- Alabaster = fine-grained CaSO₄·2H₂O, shiny, used for ornaments — cannot be kept outdoors (CaSO₄ solubility 2 g/L) **[JD Lee]**.
- Gypsum added to Portland cement (2–3% by weight) to **regulate/slow down setting** (specifically of the aluminate phase) — this is a *different* chemical role from PoP's own setting-by-rehydration **[NCERT][JD Lee][Master Notes flags this "do not confuse" distinction explicitly as a trap]**.
- Uses (NCERT): largest use in building industry as plaster; immobilising fractures/sprains; dentistry; ornamental work, casts of statues/busts **[NCERT verbatim][S block.pdf identical]**.

### 16.5 Cement / Portland cement
- First introduced in England 1824 by **Joseph Aspdin**; called "Portland cement" because it resembles natural limestone quarried in the Isle of Portland, England **[NCERT][JD Lee — identical history]**.
- Made by combining lime-rich material (CaO) with clay (SiO₂ + oxides of Al, Fe, Mg) **[NCERT]**.
- Average Portland cement composition (NCERT, identical in S block.pdf): CaO 50–60%; SiO₂ 20–25%; Al₂O₃ 5–10%; MgO 2–3%; Fe₂O₃ 1–2%; SO₃ 1–2%. Good-quality cement: silica/alumina ratio between 2.5–4; lime/(silica+alumina+iron oxide) ratio as close to 2 as possible **[NCERT — precise ratio criteria, good for numerical JEE questions]**.
- Manufacture: limestone + clay strongly heated together → fuse/react → "cement clinker"; clinker mixed with 2–3% gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) to form cement **[NCERT][JD Lee]**.
- Important ingredients in Portland cement (NCERT, precise %): dicalcium silicate Ca₂SiO₄ 26%; tricalcium silicate Ca₃SiO₅ 51%; tricalcium aluminate Ca₃Al₂O₆ 11%.
- Setting of cement: mixing with water → hydration of the constituent molecules + their rearrangement → hard mass **[NCERT]**. Purpose of gypsum: slow the setting so it hardens sufficiently rather than "flash setting" **[NCERT][JD Lee]**.
- Uses: concrete/reinforced concrete, plastering, bridges/dams/buildings — "commodity of national necessity next to iron and steel" **[NCERT — near-verbatim phrase]**.
- **Sorel cement** (extra, not NCERT) — a mixture of MgO and MgCl₂ (paste-like) that sets to a hard mass on standing; used in dental filling, flooring **[S block.pdf — unique detail]**.

## 17. Biological Importance of Mg and Ca

**[NCERT core facts, verbatim/near-verbatim across NCERT, Master Notes, S block.pdf, and JD Lee]**:
- Adult body: ~25 g Mg, ~1200 g Ca (vs only 5 g Fe, 0.06 g Cu). Daily requirement 200–300 mg.
- **Mg**: cofactor for **all enzymes that utilise ATP in phosphate transfer**; central metal in **chlorophyll** (light absorption for photosynthesis). JD Lee elaborates: Mg²⁺ forms a complex with ATP, and is a constituent of **phosphohydrolases and phosphotransferases** (enzymes for reactions involving ATP and energy release); also essential for transmission of impulses along nerve fibres.
- **Ca**: ~99% of body calcium in bones/teeth (as apatite Ca₃(PO₄)₂-type mineral; teeth enamel specifically as **fluoroapatite** [3(Ca₃(PO₄)₂)·CaF₂] — **[JD Lee explicit mineral formula]**); roles in neuromuscular function, interneuronal transmission, cell membrane integrity, blood coagulation. Plasma Ca regulated ~100 mg/L by **calcitonin and parathyroid hormone**. Bone is not inert — continuously solubilised/redeposited (~400 mg/day passes through plasma in man) **[NCERT — this exact "bone dynamics" fact reproduced in Master Notes/S block.pdf]**.
- JD Lee adds intracellular-vs-extracellular distribution context (paralleling the Group 1 Na⁺/K⁺ pump discussion): Mg²⁺ ions concentrated in animal cells (like K⁺), while Ca²⁺ concentrated in body fluids outside the cell (like Na⁺) — Ca²⁺ required to **trigger muscle contraction** and to **maintain the regular beating of the heart**.

## 18. Diagonal Relationship: Be and Al

Ionic radius Be²⁺ ≈31 pm; charge/radius ratio nearly matches Al³⁺ (JD Lee gives a formula: charge per unit area = ionic charge / [(4/3)π·(ionic radius)²]; Be²⁺ = 2.36, Al³⁺ = 2.50 — very close, even though absolute radii (Be²⁺ 0.45 Å six-coordinate, Al³⁺ 0.535 Å) are not that close — **[JD Lee — this is the precise quantitative justification for diagonal relationships in general, applicable equally to Li/Mg]**.

Complete similarity list (merged from NCERT + JD Lee + Master Notes, all consistent):
1. Both not readily attacked by acids (protective oxide film) — passivated by conc. HNO₃.
2. Both amphoteric oxides (BeO, Al₂O₃) and amphoteric hydroxides (Be(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃); dissolve in excess alkali to give beryllate [Be(OH)₄]²⁻ / aluminate [Al(OH)₄]⁻.
3. Both chlorides have Cl-bridged structures in the vapour phase (BeCl₂ dimer/polymer; Al₂Cl₆ dimer); both soluble in organic solvents; both strong Lewis acids; both used as Friedel-Crafts catalysts.
4. Both form complex fluorides with strong tendency: [BeF₄]²⁻, [AlF₆]³⁻ (note: Be tetrahedral CN=4 max; Al can reach CN=6 — an important **point of difference within the diagonal similarity**, explicitly flagged as a JEE trap **[Master Notes: "Be–Al similarity is diagonal, not complete identity. Al commonly reaches coordination number 6; Be is commonly treated as coordination number ≤4"]**).
5. Both carbides hydrolyse to give methane: Be₂C → CH₄; Al₄C₃ → CH₄ (contrast with CaC₂ → C₂H₂).
6. Both nitrides hydrolyse to NH₃: Be₃N₂ → NH₃; AlN → NH₃.
7. Both hydrides electron-deficient/polymeric with multicentre (3c-2e) bonding — BeH₂ vs AlH₃ **[JD Lee: "Beryllium hydride is electron deficient and polymeric, with multicentre bonding, like aluminium hydride"]**.
8. Standard electrode potentials close: Be −1.85 V, Al −1.66 V (much closer to each other than Be is to Ca/Sr/Ba, −2.87/−2.89/−2.90 V) **[JD Lee — explicit numeric comparison]**.
9. Be salts extensively hydrolysed (acidic in solution due to H₃O⁺ generation, see §Anomalous behaviour below) — Al salts behave similarly.
10. Be salts are among the most soluble known **[JD Lee]**.
11. Both Be and Al rendered passive by (concentrated) nitric acid.
12. Be forms many complexes (atypical of Groups 1–2 generally), just as Al is a strong complex-former for Group 13.

## 19. Anomalous Behaviour of Beryllium — Full Reasoning Chain

Three root causes, stated most crisply in JD Lee (matches Master Notes' "reasoning engine" framing):
1. **Extremely small size** → Fajans' rules: small, highly charged ions favour covalent bonding.
2. **Comparatively high electronegativity** (1.5, vs 1.2 Mg, 1.0 Ca, 1.0 Sr, 0.9 Ba) — the electronegativity difference with other elements is seldom very large, again favouring covalency (except with F, where BeF₂ still ends up regarded as covalent when fused despite the largest ΔEN in the group) **[JD Lee]**.
3. **Only 2s/2p valence orbitals** (outer shell max 8 electrons; only one 2s + three 2p orbitals available for bonding) → maximum conventional coordination number of **4**; anhydrous Be compounds predominantly covalent, and BeX₂ molecules "should be linear" (gas phase, sp-hybridised) — later members can exceed 8 outer electrons and reach CN=6 using d-orbitals **[NCERT][JD Lee — both state this explicitly]**.

Consequences (full merged list):
- Be does not exhibit CN > 4 (no d-orbitals in valence shell); other Group 2 members can reach CN=6 using d orbitals **[NCERT][JD Lee]**.
- Oxide/hydroxide amphoteric (unique in the group) **[NCERT][JD Lee]**.
- Be salts extensively hydrolysed in water — **acidic solutions**, precise mechanism **[JD Lee — detailed, valuable]**: the Be–O bond in [Be(H₂O)₄]²⁺ is very strong, which *weakens* the O–H bonds of the coordinated water, favouring proton loss: H₂O + [Be(H₂O)₄]²⁺ ⇌ [Be(H₂O)₃(OH)]⁺ + H₃O⁺, possibly followed by further polymerisation to hydroxo-bridged species [Be₂OH]³⁺, [Be₃(OH)₄]²⁺. In alkaline solution, [Be(OH)₄]²⁻ forms. **Other Group 2 salts do not hydrolyse appreciably** — this hydrolysis is uniquely pronounced for Be.
- Be salts rarely have more than 4 waters of crystallisation (only 4 orbitals available in the valence shell), whereas Mg can reach CN=6 using 3d orbitals in addition to 3s/3p **[JD Lee]**.
- Be forms compounds which are largely covalent and easily hydrolysed (high IE + small size) **[NCERT]**.
- Be halides covalent, polymeric/molecular, soluble in organic media, fume in air due to hydrolysis, sublime, do not conduct electricity when fused **[JD Lee]**.
- BeH₂ covalent/polymeric (not saline like CaH₂/SrH₂/BaH₂).
- Be₂C methanide-type, gives CH₄ on hydrolysis (unlike acetylide-type MC₂ of Ca/Sr/Ba giving C₂H₂).
- Be carbonate unusually unstable, only stable under CO₂ atmosphere, and structurally unusual (retains hydrated ion).
- Be shows no characteristic flame colour.
- Be forms complexes readily — atypical tendency for the group (e.g., [BeF₄]²⁻, [Be(OH)₄]²⁻, beryllium acetate/oxalate/nitrate chelates).
- Be is amphoteric as a **metal** too (dissolves in NaOH, liberating H₂, unlike Mg/Ca/Sr/Ba which are purely basic/do not react with alkali).
- Be, like Al, is rendered passive by concentrated HNO₃.
- Toxicity: Be and Ba compounds "all very toxic" **[JD Lee, chapter intro]**; Be specifically causes berylliosis and displaces Mg from enzymes.

## 20. JEE-Specific Traps, Common Misconceptions, and Exam-Ready Reasoning (compiled across sources)

1. All Group 2 oxides basic — **False**, BeO amphoteric.
2. All Group 2 hydroxides strong/highly soluble bases — **False**, Be(OH)₂ amphoteric, Mg(OH)₂ weakly basic/sparingly soluble.
3. Hydroxide solubility decreases down group — **False**, it increases (lattice energy falls faster than hydration energy for OH⁻ and F⁻ specifically).
4. Sulphate solubility increases down group — **False**, decreases sharply; BaSO₄ virtually insoluble.
5. Carbonate solubility increases down group — **False**, decreases overall (opposite mechanism to hydroxide: here hydration energy falls faster than lattice energy).
6. Mg + steam → Mg(OH)₂ — **False**, standard product is MgO + H₂; Mg(OH)₂ forms with liquid (esp. hot) water, not steam.
7. Be reacts vigorously with water because E° is very negative — **False**; kinetic passivation by BeO film dominates over thermodynamics (parallel to why Li reacts gently despite most negative E° in Group 1 — rate/kinetics ≠ thermodynamics).
8. Burning Mg extinguished by CO₂ — **False**; Mg reduces CO₂ (2Mg+CO₂→2MgO+C) and continues burning; CO₂ extinguishers must never be used on Mg fires.
9. BeH₂ ionic/saline — **False**; covalent, polymeric, electron-deficient (3c-2e bonded).
10. CaH₂ covalent like BeH₂ — **False**; CaH₂/SrH₂/BaH₂ are ionic, containing H⁻.
11. BeCl₂ simple ionic solid — **False**; strongly covalent, chain-polymeric in solid, dimeric in vapour at moderate T, monomeric linear only above ~1200 K.
12. All Group 2 carbides give ethyne (acetylene) on hydrolysis — **False**; Be₂C → CH₄ (methanide); Mg₂C₃ → CH₃C≡CH propyne; CaC₂/SrC₂/BaC₂ → C₂H₂ (acetylide). BaC₂ + N₂ gives cyanide Ba(CN)₂, not cyanamide (unlike CaC₂ + N₂ → CaNCN).
13. Group 2 nitrates decompose to nitrites on heating (like some Group 1 nitrates) — **False**; all give MO + NO₂ + O₂ (like LiNO₃).
14. Blue liquid-ammonia solutions coloured due to M²⁺ — **False**; due to solvated/ammoniated electrons.
15. Be and Mg show characteristic flame colours — **False** in the standard flame test (their electrons are too strongly bound).
16. Lime water is a suspension — **False**; it's a clear (saturated) solution; milk of lime is the suspension.
17. Excess CO₂ increases permanent CaCO₃ precipitate — **False**; excess CO₂ converts insoluble CaCO₃ to soluble Ca(HCO₃)₂, clearing the milkiness (a solubility-equilibrium sequence, not a contradiction).
18. Whitewash hardens purely by water evaporating — **False**; chemical carbonation of Ca(OH)₂ to CaCO₃ is the key hardening step.
19. Gypsum and PoP are the same hydration state — **False**; dihydrate (gypsum) vs hemihydrate (PoP).
20. PoP sets to anhydrous CaSO₄ — **False**; it rehydrates back to gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O).
21. Dead-burnt plaster = CaSO₄·½H₂O — **False**; dead-burnt plaster is fully anhydrous CaSO₄ (from overheating beyond the PoP stage).
22. More mixing water always gives a stronger PoP set — **False**; excess water leaves pores after evaporation, generally lowering strength.
23. Be resembles only Mg, has no special relation to Al — **False**; Be–Al diagonal relationship is extensive and well-documented.
24. Be²⁺ should have a high coordination number because of its high +2 charge — **False** in the standard model; its very small valence shell (only 2s+2p orbitals, no d) commonly limits CN to 4.
25. BaSO₄ dissolves readily because Ba²⁺ is a large, "easy" ion — **False**; the *reduced* hydration enthalpy of the large Ba²⁺ ion is precisely why BaSO₄ is extremely insoluble.
26. The exact E° order can be derived from ionization enthalpy alone — **False**; it is a thermodynamic sum of atomisation, IE₁+IE₂, and hydration enthalpy terms.
27. CaO and Ca(OH)₂ have identical names/uses ("lime" used loosely) — **False**; quicklime (CaO) ≠ slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂) ≠ lime water (solution) ≠ milk of lime (suspension) — four genuinely distinct terms.
28. Radius-ratio rules always correctly predict the coordination number of ionic oxides — **False** (a subtler, JD-Lee-specific trap): predicted CN=8 for SrO/BaO from radius ratio, but the actually found structure is 6:6 NaCl-type for both — illustrating known limitations of the simple radius-ratio approach (ionic radii not perfectly known/spherical/rigid).
29. Fluoride and hydroxide solubility trends can be explained the same way as carbonate/sulphate — **False**; F⁻ and OH⁻ are the two group anions where **lattice energy falls faster than hydration energy** down the group (opposite to CO₃²⁻/SO₄²⁻ where hydration falls faster), giving the *reversed* (increasing) solubility trend. This single "which energy falls faster" framework (JD Lee's core insight) is the master explanatory tool for every Group 2 solubility trend and is worth stating explicitly as the unifying principle in the final notes.
30. Anhydrous MgCl₂ can be obtained by simply heating MgCl₂·6H₂O in air — **False**; this causes hydrolysis (→ Mg(OH)Cl + HCl → MgO + HCl); anhydrous MgCl₂ industrially made via double salt MgCl₂·NH₄Cl·6H₂O route, or by suppressing hydrolysis (e.g., heating in HCl atmosphere).

## 22. MOT / Bonding Explanations to Preserve (Consolidated)

For the final notes, the following bonding explanations are the "reuse as-is" candidates, all from **[JD Lee]/[aemsg.pdf]**:

1. **BeH₂ / BeMe₂ electron-deficient bonding**: 3-centre-2-electron ("banana") bonds bridging Be···H···Be (or Be···CH₃···Be); Be forms 4 bonds to 4 H atoms total in the polymer despite having only 2 valence electrons and 2 valence orbitals to "start" with in the naive picture — resolved by clustering/multicentre bonding, directly analogous to boron hydride (diborane-type) bonding.
2. **BeX₂ gas-phase linearity**: ground-state Be (1s²2s², no unpaired e⁻) cannot bond; promotion of one 2s electron to 2p gives 1s²2s¹2p¹ (2 unpaired electrons) → **sp hybridisation** → linear, 180°, 2-coordinate BeX₂ molecule, stable only in the gas phase.
3. **[Be(H₂O)₄]²⁺ tetrahedral hydration**: Be²⁺ (bare ion, empty 2s/2p) accepts 4 lone pairs from 4 H₂O O atoms → **sp³ hybridisation**, CN=4, tetrahedral — this is the general reason Be is always 4-coordinate (never 6), because only 4 valence orbitals (1×2s + 3×2p) exist with no accessible d-orbitals.
4. **BeCl₂ solid/dimer/monomer structural progression** with explicit distinction between **halide bridging** (lone-pair donation, 2 separate 2c-2e-type dative bonds per bridging Cl) and **alkyl bridging** in Be(Me)₂ (genuine 3c-2e electron-deficient bond, one shared bonding pair covering 2 Be + 1 C).
5. **Peroxide/superoxide MOT bond order** (from the Group 1 chapter but directly reusable for BaO₂ etc.): peroxide O₂²⁻, 18 e⁻, bond order 1, diamagnetic; superoxide O₂⁻, 17 e⁻ (one unpaired e⁻ in π*2p), bond order 1.5, paramagnetic — this explains KO₂/paramagnetism questions and can be extended by direct analogy to Group 2 peroxides (all bond order 1, diamagnetic, since only Ba/Sr/Ca(with H2O2) peroxides are known — no Group 2 superoxides exist).
6. **Fajans' rules stated explicitly** as the umbrella principle for Be's covalency: "small highly charged ions tend to form covalent compounds" — this is the formal citation to use whenever justifying "Be²⁺ is small and highly polarising, therefore covalent" claims.
