1. The carbon–halogen bond: structure, polarity and physical properties

1.1 Classes of organic halides

A halogen can be attached to carbon in five structurally distinct ways. The reactivity of the C–X bond changes completely from one class to another, and most exam questions are really tests of whether you can put a given halide into the right class.

Alkyl (1°) Alkyl (2°) Alkyl (3°) Allylic Benzylic Vinylic Aryl
Seven structural types. Only the first five (sp3 C–X, including allylic and benzylic) undergo ordinary SN1/SN2; vinylic and aryl halides do not.
  • Alkyl halide (haloalkane): X on an sp3 carbon; classed 1°, 2° or 3° by the number of carbons attached to the carbon bearing X.
  • Allylic halide: X on an sp3 carbon next to C=C. Benzylic halide: X on an sp3 carbon next to a benzene ring. Both ionise unusually easily because the cation is resonance-stabilised, and both undergo SN2 unusually fast because the transition state is conjugated.
  • Vinylic halide: X on an sp2 carbon of C=C. Aryl halide: X directly on an aromatic ring carbon. Both have a shorter, stronger C–X bond (sp2 carbon plus lone-pair delocalisation).
  • Polyhalogen compounds: more than one halogen. gem-dihalide: both X on the same carbon (CH3CHCl2, ethylidene chloride). vic-dihalide: X on adjacent carbons (BrCH2CH2Br, ethylene dibromide). α,ω-dihalide: X on the two ends of a chain.
Trap Do not confuse allylic (X on the carbon next to C=C, CH2=CH–CH2–X) with vinylic (X on the C=C itself, CH2=CH–X). Allyl chloride is very reactive; vinyl chloride is nearly inert to SN. Likewise benzyl chloride (C6H5CH2Cl) is reactive, chlorobenzene (C6H5Cl) is inert to ordinary nucleophiles.

1.2 Nature of the C–X bond

Halogens are more electronegative than carbon, so the bond is polarised Cδ+–Xδ−. Going down the group the bond becomes longer and weaker because the overlap between the compact carbon 2p/sp3 orbital and a larger halogen orbital worsens.

Key idea The dipole moment is the product of charge separation and bond length: μ = q × d. Fluorine has the largest charge separation but the shortest bond; chlorine has a smaller charge but a longer bond, so CH3Cl has a larger dipole moment than CH3F. So the order of μ is CH3Cl > CH3F > CH3Br > CH3I, not the order of electronegativity. This is a favourite exam trap.
Trap Bond strength and bond polarity point in opposite directions for reactivity. C–F is the most polar yet the strongest, so alkyl fluorides are the least reactive in substitution; C–I is the least polar yet the weakest, so alkyl iodides are the most reactive. In both SN1 and SN2 reactions the rate order is R–I > R–Br > R–Cl ≫ R–F. (This is for sp3 carbon. The reverse order for aryl halides in SNAr is discussed in section 17.)

1.3 Physical properties

Boiling point. Alkyl halides boil much higher than alkanes of similar carbon number because of dipole–dipole forces and much larger polarisability. For a given alkyl group the boiling point rises R–F < R–Cl < R–Br < R–I because the molar mass and the polarisability (London forces) increase. For isomeric halides, branching lowers the boiling point (smaller surface area).

Density. Alkyl chlorides (monochloro, short chain) are lighter than water; alkyl bromides and iodides, and all polyhalo compounds such as CHCl3 (1.49 g mL−1) and CCl4 (1.59), are heavier than water and sink to the bottom in a separating funnel. The density rises with the number of heavy halogen atoms and falls as the alkyl group lengthens. Solubility. Slightly soluble in water: breaking the hydrogen-bond network of water costs more energy than the weak halide–water attractions return. Freely soluble in organic solvents. Many (CH2Cl2, CHCl3, CCl4) are excellent solvents for non-polar organic compounds. Isomeric dihalobenzenes. The para isomer has the highest melting point because it packs best in the crystal lattice (symmetry), while ortho (largest dipole) has the highest boiling point among the three.

Example 1 Main

Boiling-point ranking

Arrange in increasing order of boiling point: (i) 1-chlorobutane (ii) 1-bromobutane (iii) 2-bromo-2-methylpropane (iv) 1-iodobutane.

Show solution
  1. Same carbon skeleton in (i), (ii), (iv): bp increases with the size and polarisability of X: Cl < Br < I.
  2. (iii) is a branched isomer of (ii), more compact, so it boils lower than (ii).
  3. Values: 1-chlorobutane 78 °C, tert-butyl bromide 73 °C, 1-bromobutane 102 °C, 1-iodobutane 131 °C.
  4. Order: (iii) 73 < (i) 78 < (ii) 102 < (iv) 131 °C. Note that the branched bromide even boils below the straight-chain chloride because the effect of the extra mass of Br is partly cancelled by the compact shape.
Example 2 Main

Dipole moment

Arrange CH3F, CH3Cl, CH3Br, CH3I in decreasing order of dipole moment and explain.

Show solution
  1. μ = charge × distance. F is the most electronegative, but its C–F bond is the shortest (1.39 Å).
  2. From Cl onwards the bond length increases faster than the charge separation falls.
  3. Experimental values: CH3Cl 1.87 D > CH3F 1.85 D > CH3Br 1.81 D > CH3I 1.62 D.
