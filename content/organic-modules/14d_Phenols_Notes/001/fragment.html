2. Acidity of phenol: comparison with alcohols, water, carbonic acid and carboxylic acids

Phenol is a weak acid, but it is far stronger than saturated alcohols. Two factors account for this. First, the phenoxide ion is stabilised by delocalisation of the negative charge into the ring, which an alkoxide cannot do. Second, the oxygen is attached to an sp2 carbon, which is more electronegative than an sp3 carbon, so the inductive effect also favours the anion. In an alkoxide the alkyl group is electron-releasing and destabilises the anion.

Approximate pKa values in water, which are well established, are collected below. A smaller pKa means a stronger acid.

  • Ethanol ≈ 16 and water ≈ 15.7 (comparable; both far weaker than phenol)
  • Phenol ≈ 10
  • Carbonic acid, first dissociation ≈ 6.4
  • Benzoic acid ≈ 4.2 and acetic acid ≈ 4.8

Carboxylic acids are stronger than phenol because the carboxylate ion shares its charge equally between two oxygen atoms (two equivalent resonance forms in which the charge sits on the more electronegative atom), whereas in phenoxide the charge is spread partly on carbon atoms, which are poor at bearing negative charge. So the acidity order is: carboxylic acid > carbonic acid > phenol > water ≈ alcohol.

Phenol is neutralised completely by hydroxide, since pKa of water (15.7) is well above that of phenol.

Key idea. Aqueous sodium hydrogencarbonate (conjugate acid pKa ≈ 6.4) is not basic enough to deprotonate phenol (pKa ≈ 10), so phenol does not dissolve in it and no CO2 is evolved. Carboxylic acids do dissolve with effervescence. This is the basis for separating phenols from carboxylic acids.

The converse is also useful: carbon dioxide passed through aqueous sodium phenoxide regenerates phenol (the solution turns turbid), because carbonic acid is the stronger acid and phenoxide is the stronger base.

Formula. For an acid HA of pKa in a solution of concentration c (weak acid, small ionisation): pH ≈ ½(pKa − log c). For the reaction HA + B– ⇌ A– + HB, log K = pKa(HB) − pKa(HA).
