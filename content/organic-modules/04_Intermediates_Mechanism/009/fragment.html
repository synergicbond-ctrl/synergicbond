8. Carbenes

8.1 Structure: singlet and triplet carbenes

A carbene is a neutral species with a carbon atom that has only two bonds and six valence electrons (R2C:). The two non-bonding electrons can be arranged in two ways, and the two arrangements are different spin states.

Singlet: lone pair and empty p orbital (angle 100–110°). Triplet: two unpaired electrons (angle 130–150°)
  • Singlet carbene: the carbon is sp2. The two non-bonding electrons are paired in one sp2 orbital, and a p orbital is empty. The carbene is therefore both a nucleophile (filled sp2 orbital, the HOMO) and an electrophile (empty p orbital, the LUMO). The bond angle is small, 100 to 110°, because the lone pair repels the bonding pairs. The stable N-heterocyclic carbene studied by X-ray crystallography has an angle of 102° and a 13C NMR shift of 211 ppm at the carbene carbon.
  • Triplet carbene: one electron in an sp2 (or sp) orbital and one in the p orbital, with parallel spins. It is a diradical and cannot be seen by NMR but is observed by ESR. The bond angle is larger, 130 to 150°.

Substituents with lone pairs (halogens, oxygen, nitrogen) donate into the empty p orbital and lower the energy of the singlet, which makes it the ground state; all stable carbenes are of this type, and strongly donating diamino carbenes are nucleophilic rather than electrophilic. For most carbenes the triplet is the ground state, with the singlet about 40 kJ mol−1 higher: it costs that much energy to pair the electrons in one orbital (Hund's rule). Which state reacts? A carbene formed from a precursor in which all electrons are paired is formed as the singlet, whatever the ground state. Dichlorocarbene from chloroform and base is formed as a singlet, which is also its ground state. Methylene from diazomethane is formed as the singlet and, if it reacts rapidly, reacts as the singlet. If the reaction is diluted with an inert gas (perfluoropropane) the carbene undergoes many collisions before it reacts, spin flips to the triplet, and addition to a Z-alkene becomes less stereospecific. Photolysis of a diazoalkane in solution gives a mixture of cis and trans cyclopropanes (65 % cis in one example) because the triplet reacts stepwise. Lack of stereospecificity almost certainly indicates a triplet, but stereospecific addition does not by itself prove that a singlet was involved. Reactive carbenes can be observed in frozen argon below 77 K by irradiating precursors such as diazomethane, and examined by infrared and ESR spectroscopy.

MOT Lens A singlet carbene has both a filled sp2 orbital (HOMO, nucleophilic) and an empty p orbital (LUMO, electrophilic). Donor substituents (lone pairs of Cl, O, N) mix into the empty p orbital, which raises its energy, lowers the energy of the singlet and reduces the electrophilicity; the extreme case is the N-heterocyclic carbene, which is nucleophilic and can be isolated. A triplet carbene has one electron in each of two orbitals and behaves as a diradical.
Special Case Spin state at formation. A carbene formed from an all-paired precursor (CHCl3 and base, a diazoalkane heated) is formed as a singlet whatever its ground state; if it reacts faster than it can undergo collisional spin flip, it reacts as a singlet. Photolysis permits formation of the triplet.

Diazocarbonyl compounds are much more stable than diazoalkanes because the carbonyl group stabilises the diazo dipole, and they are made from an acyl chloride and diazomethane (the second equivalent of CH2N2 removes the HCl as CH3Cl and N2) or from the parent carbonyl compound and tosyl azide. A carbenoid is a carbene bound to a metal that transfers CR2 without ever being a free carbene; carbenoids are milder and more selective.

Compare & Distinguish Singlet carbene: one concerted step, stereospecific, a [1+2] cycloaddition (cheletropic). Triplet carbene: stepwise radical addition, not stereospecific. Stereospecific addition does not prove a singlet (rotation may be slow and spin flip fast), but non-stereospecific addition almost certainly indicates a triplet.

8.3 Reactions of carbenes

Carbenes are electrophilic and look for any pair of electrons. Unlike carbocations, which choose charged or very electron-rich nucleophiles, a carbene reacts with any filled orbital: a lone pair, a C=C double bond (electron-rich or electron-poor) or even a C–H bond. Many of the reactions are therefore overall insertions: into O–H, C=C and C–H bonds. The word "insertion" describes the overall result, not always the mechanism.

8.3.1 Cyclopropanation of alkenes

A singlet carbene adds to a C=C bond in a single concerted step, and the geometry of the alkene is retained in the cyclopropane. The reaction is a [1+2] cycloaddition (a cheletropic reaction). In the orbital picture the carbene HOMO (filled sp2) interacts with the alkene LUMO (π*) and the alkene HOMO (π) with the carbene LUMO (empty p). A head-on approach has an antibonding interaction, so the carbene approaches sideways and then swings round as the two new bonds form.

Z-but-2-ene with dibromocarbene gives the cis-1,1-dibromo-2,3-dimethylcyclopropane (the two methyl groups remain cis; less than 1 % trans)

The retention of the less stable cis relationship from the Z-alkene is the key evidence for a concerted reaction. The Simmons–Smith reagent also adds stereospecifically (syn) to the alkene and is a standard method for making cyclopropanes. Many natural products and insecticides (pyrethrins) contain cyclopropane rings.

Relative reactivity towards dichlorocarbene

Rank by rate of reaction with dichlorocarbene: ethene, cyclohexene, 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene. Give the reason.

Show solution
  1. Dichlorocarbene is an electrophile, and its LUMO (the empty p orbital) is raised in energy only slightly by the chlorine lone pairs. It reacts faster with alkenes whose HOMO (the π orbital) is higher in energy, which means alkenes with more alkyl substituents.
  2. 2,3-Dimethylbut-2-ene (tetrasubstituted) > cyclohexene (disubstituted) > ethene.
Experimental Connection The mild methylation of a carboxylic acid with diazomethane involves no carbene: the acid protonates CH2N2 and the carboxylate displaces N2 from CH3N2+. Photolysis of the same reagent, in contrast, gives methylene, which also inserts into O–H and C–H bonds but non-selectively.
8.3.2 Insertion into X–H bonds

Carbenes insert into O–H, N–H and even C–H bonds. Diazomethane is therefore a reagent for the methylation of carboxylic acids to methyl esters: the acid protonates the diazo carbon, and the resulting methyldiazonium ion is attacked by the carboxylate in an SN2 reaction with loss of N2. Diazomethane is toxic and explosive, which limits the method to small scales. Rhodium carbenoids insert into C–H bonds intramolecularly.

8.3.3 Reimer–Tiemann reaction

Phenol with chloroform and aqueous sodium hydroxide gives mainly salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde), with a little 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, after acidification.

CHCl₃, NaOH(aq), then H⁺⟶
Reimer–Tiemann reaction: ortho-formylation of phenol
  • Hydroxide removes the proton of CHCl3, and CCl3− loses chloride (α-elimination) to give :CCl2.
  • Phenol is deprotonated to phenoxide, whose HOMO has large coefficients at the ortho and para carbons.
  • The ortho carbon of the phenoxide attacks the electrophilic :CCl2 to give a cyclohexadienone anion, which tautomerises to ArCHCl2 (as the phenoxide).
  • Loss of chloride with help from the O− gives a quinone-methide-like species; hydroxide adds and a further loss of chloride gives the aldehyde.
8.3.4 Carbylamine reaction

A primary amine heated with chloroform and alcoholic potassium hydroxide gives an isocyanide R–N+≡C− (a foul-smelling liquid); secondary and tertiary amines do not react in this way, so it is a test for the –NH2 group of a primary amine. The nitrogen lone pair attacks :CCl2, and successive loss of two molecules of HCl by base gives the isocyanide.

8.3.5 Wolff rearrangement and ring expansion

An α-diazoketone R–CO–CHN2 loses N2 with heat, light or silver(I) to give an α-oxocarbene, in which the substituent R then migrates from the carbonyl carbon to the carbene carbon to give a ketene, R–CH=C=O. Water, alcohols or amines add to the ketene to give an acid, ester or amide. With diazomethane, an acid chloride is thereby converted into the next higher homologue of the acid (Arndt–Eistert synthesis).

Compare & Distinguish Free carbene, carbenoid and ylide are different things. Dichlorocarbene is a free carbene. The Simmons–Smith reagent ICH2ZnI and rhodium or copper carbenes from diazo compounds are carbenoids. Ph3P=CH2 is an ylide, a carbanion next to a phosphonium centre: it reacts with C=O in the Wittig reaction and does not cyclopropanate alkenes.
