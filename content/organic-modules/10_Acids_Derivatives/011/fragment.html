11.2.1 Base hydrolysis (saponification), BAC2

OH⁻ attacks C=O⟶ − EtO⁻⟶ EtO⁻ deprotonates (irreversible)⟶
B_AC2: addition, elimination, then fast acid–base step
  • Step 1 (slow): OH− adds to C=O giving the tetrahedral intermediate. Step 2: the alkoxide is expelled. Step 3: RO− (strong base) removes the proton from RCOOH: carboxylate + ROH. This last step is essentially irreversible (pKa 4.8 vs 16): saponification goes to completion, which is why one full equivalent of base is consumed, unlike an acid-catalysed hydrolysis.
  • Rate = k[ester][OH−]: second order overall.
  • Substituent effects: electron-withdrawing groups in the acyl part (ClCH2COOEt) speed the reaction (they stabilise the negative tetrahedral intermediate; large positive ρ); bulky groups near C=O slow it (steric hindrance): HCOOEt > CH3COOEt > CH3CH2COOEt > (CH3)3CCOOEt. Phenyl esters hydrolyse faster than alkyl esters because phenoxide is the better leaving group.
  • Labelling: hydrolysis of an ester in H218O/OH− gives the carboxylate with 18O; the alcohol has no label. Hydrolysis of unreacted ester recovered midway contains 18O in the C=O: exchange via the reversible tetrahedral intermediate.

11.2.2 Acid hydrolysis, AAC2

H⁺⟶ + H₂O⟶ − H⁺ (transfer)⟶ − EtOH⟶ − H⁺⟶
A_AC2 is the exact reverse of Fischer esterification (principle of microscopic reversibility)
  • Rate = k[ester][H+]; because water is in huge excess the kinetics appear first order in ester (pseudo first order). Reversible, so excess water is used.
  • For Fischer esterification and acid hydrolysis, forward and reverse follow the same path.

11.2.3 Pseudo-first-order kinetics: the classic numerical

For ester + H2O in HCl, titrating equal aliquots with alkali: V0, Vt, V∞ are volumes of NaOH at times 0, t and complete reaction; k = (2.303/t) log[(V∞ − V0)/(V∞ − Vt)].

Example 23 Main

Kinetics of ester hydrolysis

In the HCl-catalysed hydrolysis of ethyl acetate, 5 mL aliquots required 20.0, 22.4 and 40.0 mL of alkali at t = 0, 30 min and ∞. Find the rate constant and half-life.

Show solution
  1. V∞ − V0 = 20.0 mL; V∞ − Vt = 40.0 − 22.4 = 17.6 mL.
  2. k = (2.303/30 min) log(20.0/17.6) = (2.303/30)(0.0555) = 4.26 × 10−3 min−1.
  3. t½ = 0.693/k = 163 min. (Vt − V0 reflects the acetic acid formed; the HCl contribution is constant and cancels in the differences.)
Example 24 Adv

Saponification, second order

Ethyl acetate and NaOH, each 0.050 M, react with k = 0.11 L mol−1 s−1. How long to reach 50 %? How long if [NaOH] is 0.050 M but the ester is 0.100 M?

Show solution
  1. Equal initial concentrations: t½ = 1/(k a) = 1/(0.11 × 0.050) = 182 s.
  2. Unequal: with a = 0.100 M (ester), b = 0.050 M (NaOH, limiting), k t = [1/(a − b)] ln[b(a − x)/(a(b − x))]. For 50 % of the NaOH consumed, x = 0.025 M: k t = (1/0.050) ln[0.050 × 0.075/(0.100 × 0.025)] = 20 ln 1.5 = 8.11, so t = 8.11/0.11 = 74 s. The excess ester speeds the consumption of the limiting reagent.

11.2.4 Tertiary and benzylic esters: AAL1

t-Butyl acetate in dilute acid: protonation, then C–O cleavage gives (CH3)3C+ + CH3COOH, and the cation takes up water. With a chiral 2° or 3° alkyl group, racemisation (and partial retention/inversion mixtures) results; with 18O in the water the label ends in the alcohol, not the acid, which is the diagnostic of alkyl–oxygen fission.

Example 25 Adv

Label tracking

tert-Butyl acetate is hydrolysed in dilute H2SO4 in water enriched in 18O (H218O). Where is the label? Contrast with methyl acetate.

Show solution
  1. AAL1 for the t-butyl ester: the t-butyl cation is captured by H218O, giving (CH3)3C–18OH and unlabelled CH3COOH.

Which esters are hydrolysed (in dilute acid) mainly with alkyl–oxygen fission? (A) CH3COOC(CH3)3 (B) CH3COOCH3 (C) CH3COOCH(C6H5)2 (D) C6H5COOC2H5

11.3 Transesterification

RCOOR′ + R″OH ⇌ RCOOR″ + R′OH, catalysed by acid or alkoxide, driven by a large excess of R″OH or by removal of R′OH (low-boiling alcohols distil off). It is the basis of biodiesel: triglyceride + 3 CH3OH (NaOH or KOH) → 3 fatty acid methyl esters + glycerol; and of PET manufacture (dimethyl terephthalate + ethylene glycol). To avoid scrambling, use the alkoxide that matches the ester’s alkyl group (NaOEt with ethyl esters).

Example 27 Main

Grignard on esters

What is the product when ethyl formate reacts with excess CH3MgBr followed by H3O+? Compare with ethyl acetate.

Show solution
  1. HCOOEt + 2 CH3MgBr: first addition-elimination gives CH3CHO (acetaldehyde), which reacts at once with the second equivalent: after work-up propan-2-ol ((CH3)2CHOH), a secondary alcohol.
  2. Ethyl acetate with excess CH3MgBr gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol ((CH3)3COH). Rule: formate esters give secondary alcohols; all other esters give tertiary alcohols (with two identical R groups from the Grignard).

11.5 Ester pyrolysis

Esters containing a β-hydrogen on the alkyl (alcohol) side eliminate a carboxylic acid at 400–500 °C through a six-membered cyclic transition state (Ei, syn elimination). Products: an alkene + RCOOH, with no carbocation, hence no rearrangement; the elimination is syn and gives mainly the Hofmann (less substituted) alkene when a choice exists with statistical weighting.

500 °C, Ei⟶ +
Ethyl acetate → ethene + acetic acid
500 °C⟶ + +
sec-Butyl acetate: but-1-ene (statistical, kinetic) with but-2-enes
Trap Pyrolysis of tert-butyl esters gives isobutene at lower temperatures (and also by acid). Esters whose alkyl group has no β-H (methyl, benzyl, neopentyl) cannot undergo this elimination.
<h4>11.6 Lactones</h4>

Intramolecular esterification of γ- and δ-hydroxy acids to five- and six-membered lactones is very easy (often spontaneous): 4-hydroxybutanoic acid → γ-butyrolactone. β-Lactones (four-membered, strained) and larger rings (seven or more) form much less readily; β-hydroxy acids prefer to dehydrate to α,β-unsaturated acids. α-Hydroxy acids dimerise to six-membered cyclic lactides (2 molecules).

Example 28 Main

Ester or lactone

A compound C4H6O2 dissolves slowly in NaOH (consuming 1 equivalent) and on acidification gives a hydroxy acid that recyclises on warming. What is it, and what other isomer shows the same C4H6O2 formula as an acyclic unsaturated ester?

Show solution
  1. Cyclic ester of 4-hydroxybutanoic acid: γ-butyrolactone (oxolan-2-one). NaOH opens the ring to sodium 4-hydroxybutanoate; acidification gives 4-hydroxybutanoic acid, which relactonises. Degree of unsaturation = 2 (ring + C=O).
  2. An acyclic isomer: methyl acrylate CH2=CHCOOCH3 (also C4H6O2); vinyl acetate CH3COOCH=CH2 is another.
