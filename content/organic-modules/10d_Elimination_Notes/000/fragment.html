1. E2: mechanism and kinetics

A bimolecular elimination (E2) removes a proton and a leaving group in a single concerted step. The base attacks a hydrogen on the carbon next to the carbon bearing the leaving group (the β-hydrogen), the C–H bond electrons become the new π bond, and the leaving group departs at the same time. No intermediate forms, so there is only one transition state and the rate depends on both partners.

Rate law. Rate = k[R–X][B−]. Second order overall: first order in substrate and first order in base. Doubling either concentration doubles the rate; doubling both quadruples it.
  1. Step 1 (single concerted step): the base B− approaches a β-hydrogen while the C–X bond lengthens.
  2. Step 2 (same transition state): the C–H bond breaks, the C=C π bond forms, X− leaves, and BH forms.
  3. Evidence for the picture: a second-order rate law, a primary hydrogen/deuterium isotope effect, no rearranged carbon skeleton, and strict stereochemical requirements.

Reactant:

Reagent: NaOEt in EtOH, warm

Product:

2-Bromobutane gives mainly (E)-but-2-ene (Zaitsev product, the more stable trans disubstituted alkene), less (Z)-but-2-ene and a smaller amount of but-1-ene.

Reactivity of the substrate toward E2 with a strong base follows the order tertiary > secondary > primary, because more alkyl groups on the α-carbon make the developing double bond more stable in the transition state. Strong bases are required: the common ones are alkoxides (EtO−, t-BuO−), hydroxide in alcohol, and amide ion.

Trap. Do not confuse the first-order E1 with the second-order E2. If the problem states that the rate is independent of base concentration, or that a rearranged product forms, think carbocation (E1), not E2.
