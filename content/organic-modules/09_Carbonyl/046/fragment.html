39. Structure and the two electrophilic sites

In C=C–C=O the carbonyl group conjugates with the C=C. Resonance shows that the β-carbon carries partial positive charge as well as the carbonyl carbon:

↔ resonance⟶
Second contributor: positive charge on the β-carbon, negative charge on oxygen. Hence both C=O carbon and β-carbon are electrophilic

Two sites of nucleophilic attack:

  • C=O carbon (1,2-addition): fast, kinetically preferred by hard, charge-controlled nucleophiles; gives allylic alkoxide/alcohol. Often reversible.
  • β-Carbon (1,4-addition, conjugate addition): gives an enolate that is protonated to the saturated carbonyl compound (overall addition of Nu–H across C=C); thermodynamically preferred because it retains the strong C=O and forms a new σ bond at the cost of the weaker π bond of C=C.

Key idea Hard/soft rule. Hard nucleophiles (high charge density, small, strongly basic: RLi, RMgX, H−, OH−) attack the hard electrophile (the carbonyl carbon). Soft nucleophiles (polarisable, stabilised: cuprates, enolates of 1,3-dicarbonyls, thiolates, amines, I−) attack the soft electrophile (the β-carbon). Reversibility also pushes towards 1,4.
Hydrogen halides and water. HBr adds to acrolein as a conjugate (Michael-type) addition: CH2=CH–CHO + HBr → BrCH2CH2CHO, the opposite regiochemistry to the Markovnikov expectation for propene, because attack by Br− occurs at the β-carbon after protonation on oxygen. Water adds (hydration) to give the β-hydroxy compound (the retro-aldol/dehydration equilibrium).

Electrophilic additions to the C=C. The conjugated C=C is deactivated towards Br2 (the carbonyl withdraws electrons) but still adds it: PhCH=CHCHO + Br2 gives 2,3-dibromo-3-phenylpropanal (slower than for an isolated alkene). Diels–Alder. The C=C of acrolein, MVK and maleic anhydride is an electron-poor dienophile, reacting with 1,3-butadiene or cyclopentadiene.

39.1 Allylic (γ) acidity: vinylogy A γ-hydrogen (on the carbon next to the C=C of an enone) is acidic: the resulting dienolate is delocalised over five atoms (O–C=C–C=C). Alkylation or protonation of the dienolate can occur at α or γ. 39.2 Spectroscopic fingerprints Conjugation lowers the IR C=O stretch (about 1685 cm-1 for an enone against 1715 for a saturated ketone) and moves the π→π* UV absorption to longer wavelength. The Woodward–Fieser rules estimate λmax: enone or six-ring enone base 215 nm; five-ring enone 202 nm; enal 207 nm; add 10 (α-alkyl), 12 (β-alkyl), 18 (γ or higher alkyl), 30 per additional conjugated C=C, 5 per exocyclic C=C. For mesityl oxide: 215 + 2 × 12 (two β-methyls) = 239 nm (observed about 237 nm).

Example 43 Adv

Mechanism-based

Explain why the reaction of CH3MgBr with cyclohexenone gives mainly the tertiary allylic alcohol, while with CH3MgBr/CuI the saturated ketone is obtained.

Show solution
  1. Grignard reagents are hard, charge-controlled, irreversible nucleophiles: they attack the carbonyl carbon (large partial positive charge, fastest). With Cu(I), an organocopper species (R2CuMgBr) forms in situ; it is a soft, polarisable nucleophile that transfers R to the soft β-carbon through a Cu(III) intermediate, giving the enolate and then the saturated ketone after work-up.
