3. Physical properties and structure

Hydrogen bonding and dimers

A carboxylic acid is both a strong H-bond donor (O–H) and a good acceptor (C=O). Two molecules pair into a cyclic dimer with two H-bonds. Consequences, each of which has been asked:

  • Carboxylic acids boil much higher than alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and esters of similar molar mass.
  • In non-polar solvents (benzene) and in the vapour near the boiling point, acids exist as dimers, so the apparent molar mass by colligative methods is about double (van’t Hoff factor i ≈ 0.5). In water the dimers are broken up by solvent H-bonds, so ordinary behaviour (or ionisation, i > 1) is seen.
  • Lower acids (up to C4) are miscible with water; solubility falls steadily with chain length, since the hydrophobic chain grows while the one polar head is constant. Benzoic acid dissolves only about 3 g per litre of water at 25 °C but dissolves readily in aqueous NaOH or NaHCO3 by forming the sodium salt.

Draw the dimer yourself: a planar eight-membered ring R–C(=O···H–O)–… in which each O–H of one acid donates to the C=O oxygen of the other (two H-bonds, about 7 kcal/mol each in the gas phase).

<h4>Melting-point alternation of dicarboxylic acids</h4>

In the series HOOC–(CH2)n–COOH, even-carbon acids (oxalic 189 °C, succinic 188, adipic 153, suberic 142, sebacic 134) melt higher than the odd-carbon acids on either side (malonic 135, glutaric 98, pimelic 106, azelaic 106). Even members pack better in the crystal because their two COOH groups point in opposite directions (anti), so the molecule is more symmetric and the lattice is stronger.

Trap The same even-odd effect does not apply to solubility or acidity trends in a simple way. Do not use melting point to infer acidity.
