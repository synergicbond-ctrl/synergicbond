14. Basicity

14.1 Measuring base strength

A base B takes up a proton: B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻, K_b = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B], pK_b = −log K_b. For comparison the pKₐ of the conjugate acid BH⁺ (written pKₐH) is used: pKₐH + pK_b = 14 in water at 25 °C. A higher pKₐH (lower pK_b) means a stronger base. Basicity is the exact reverse of the acidity of the conjugate acid: the more stable BH⁺ (the less willing to give up H⁺), the stronger the base.

  • What matters: the availability of the lone pair (or π-pair) for bonding to H⁺. Anything that lowers the electron density on the donor atom (−I, −M, delocalisation into a π-system or into a carbonyl, high s-character in the orbital holding the lone pair) weakens the base; anything that raises it (+I, +M of a substituent) or stabilises the conjugate acid (H-bonding to solvent, resonance in BH⁺) strengthens it.

14.3 Aliphatic amines

  • Gas phase (no solvent): basicity increases with the number of alkyl groups, because alkyl groups stabilise the cation by +I and polarisability: R₃N > R₂NH > RNH₂ > NH₃. (In the gas phase proton affinities: NH₃ 854, CH₃NH₂ 899, (CH₃)₂NH 930, (CH₃)₃N 949 kJ mol⁻¹.)
  • Aqueous solution: the cation R₃NH⁺ is stabilised by hydrogen bonding to water: each N–H can donate a hydrogen bond. RNH₃⁺ has three N–H bonds (best solvated), R₂NH₂⁺ has two, R₃NH⁺ one. The loss of solvation with each added alkyl group opposes the +I effect and also bulk hinders solvation. The result is that the secondary amine is the strongest base for the methyl and ethyl series, and the tertiary amine is in the middle.

Illustration 14.3 Adv

Gas phase vs solution

In the gas phase the basicity order of the methylamines is NH₃ < CH₃NH₂ < (CH₃)₂NH < (CH₃)₃N, but in water the order of (CH₃)₃N and CH₃NH₂ is reversed. Explain.

  1. In the gas phase there is no solvent: each methyl group stabilises the ammonium cation by its +I effect and by polarisability, so basicity increases with the number of methyl groups.
  2. In water the ammonium ions are solvated by hydrogen bonds to N–H protons: RNH₃⁺ (3 N–H) > R₂NH₂⁺ (2 N–H) > R₃NH⁺ (1 N–H). The loss of stabilising solvation with each methyl group offsets the +I gain, so in water Me₃N is less basic than MeNH₂ and Me₂NH.

14.4 Aromatic amines: anilines

In aniline the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the ring (+M of NH₂); it is therefore less available than in methylamine and also its conjugate acid cannot enjoy the same delocalisation: aniline (pKₐH 4.60) is a much weaker base than aliphatic amines (about 10⁶ times) and weaker than NH₃.

  • EDG at o/p (+M, +I: CH₃, OCH₃) increases the basicity: p-toluidine (5.08) > aniline; p-anisidine (5.34).
  • EWG (NO₂, Cl, CN) decreases it: p-nitroaniline (1.00), m-nitroaniline (2.47), p-chloroaniline (3.98); the para −M group is much more effective than meta (NO₂ at para delocalises the lone pair through the ring onto the nitro group). m-Anisidine (4.20) is weaker than aniline because at meta OCH₃ is only −I.
  • Ortho substituent: o-toluidine 4.44, o-chloroaniline 2.65, o-nitroaniline −0.26 — in general every ortho substituent lowers the basicity of aniline (steric hindrance to solvation of the NH₃⁺ and to protonation; electrostatic effects), the 'ortho effect' of Section 15.
  • N-alkylation: N-methylaniline 4.85, N,N-dimethylaniline 5.07: modest increase (+I); but diphenylamine (0.78) and triphenylamine (≈ −5) show that a second and third phenyl drastically reduce the basicity because the lone pair is shared among several rings.
Exam Trap In N,N-dimethylaniline the NMe₂ group conjugates with the ring only if it can be coplanar. With a methyl group at the ortho position, steric inhibition of resonance twists NMe₂ out of the plane, the lone pair is no longer delocalised, and the base becomes stronger: 2-methyl-N,N-dimethylaniline is a stronger base than N,N-dimethylaniline (pKₐH about 5.9 against 5.1). Similarly, the ortho effect usually makes o-substituted anilines weaker, but SIR can reverse the trend: always check the geometry.
Illustration 14.4 Main

Aniline vs methylamine

Why is methylamine (pKₐH 10.64) a much stronger base than aniline (4.60)?

  1. In methylamine the lone pair is localised on N, enhanced by the +I effect of CH₃, and the ammonium ion is stabilised by solvation.
  2. In aniline the lone pair overlaps with the ring π-system (+M of NH₂) and is partly delocalised on the ortho/para carbons; it is less available for protonation. The anilinium ion has no such delocalisation (the lone pair is used for N–H), so protonation costs the resonance energy.
  3. Hence CH₃NH₂ ≫ C₆H₅NH₂ in basic strength.

14.5 Heterocyclic and special bases

pyridine (5.2) piperidine (11.1) pyrrole (−3.8) imidazole (7.0) DMAP (9.6) quinuclidine (11.0)
Nitrogen heterocycles
  • Hybridisation: pyridine (N is sp², 33 % s) pKₐH 5.2 vs piperidine (sp³, 25 % s) 11.1. The nitrogen lone pair in a more s-rich orbital is held more tightly.
  • Pyrrole (−3.8) is not basic: the N lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet. Protonation occurs on C-2 (not N) and destroys the aromaticity of the ring.
  • Imidazole (6.95): protonation at the pyridine-type N (N-3) gives the imidazolium ion in which the charge is shared equally by both nitrogens (two equivalent contributors); the pyrrole-type N–H lone pair is part of the sextet. Imidazole is much more basic than pyridine and the active-site histidine of enzymes. Pyrazole (2.5) is weaker because the two nitrogens are adjacent (−I of the other N, and the cation has less resonance stabilisation).
  • DMAP (9.6): the 4-dimethylamino group donates strongly (+M) through the ring to the pyridine nitrogen. DMAP is about 10⁴ times more basic than pyridine and a powerful acylation catalyst.
  • Quinuclidine (11.0) vs triethylamine (10.75): in quinuclidine the three alkyl arms are tied back in a bicyclic cage, so the lone pair is exposed and solvation is easy; no steric hindrance. DABCO (8.8) is less basic because of the second N.
  • 2,6-Di-tert-butylpyridine (3.58) is weaker than 2,6-dimethylpyridine (6.7) and pyridine (5.2): the bulky groups prevent solvation of the pyridinium N–H (hindrance to solvation).
  • Amidines and guanidines are the strongest neutral organic bases (acetamidine 12.4, guanidine 13.6) because the conjugate acid (amidinium, guanidinium) is resonance-stabilised: guanidinium has three equivalent contributors, the charge shared over three N atoms; the planar, symmetrical Y-shaped cation is exceptionally stable.

  • Amides (acetamide −0.5) and urea (0.1) are very weakly basic: the lone pair on N is delocalised into C=O. They protonate at oxygen, not nitrogen, because the O-protonated cation is stabilised by resonance (the positive charge is shared with N).
  • Nitriles (acetonitrile ≈ −10): sp N, 50 % s-character: lone pair held very tightly.
  • Hydrazine (8.1), hydroxylamine (6.0): the α-effect: adjacent atom with a lone pair; their basicity is less than NH₃ though (inductive −I of the neighbour).
Illustration 14.8 Main

Pyridine and piperidine

Why is piperidine (pKₐH 11.1) 10⁶ times more basic than pyridine (5.2)?

  1. In piperidine the nitrogen is sp³ hybridised (25 % s-character) with a localised lone pair and the ring carbons are +I donors. In pyridine the nitrogen is sp² (33 % s): the lone pair is held closer to the nucleus and is less available; the N also experiences the −I effect of the ring sp² carbons.
  2. The difference of about 6 pKₐ units is 10⁶ in basicity.
Illustration 14.9 Adv

Which nitrogen of imidazole is protonated?

Explain which N of imidazole is protonated and why the cation is particularly stable.

  1. N-3 (pyridine-type, lone pair in plane) is protonated; its lone pair is not part of the aromatic sextet. The product has two N–H groups and the positive charge is delocalised between the two nitrogens (amidinium-type resonance: N–C=N⁺ ↔ N⁺=C–N). The two contributors are equivalent, which gives especially high stability (pKₐH 6.95).
  2. Protonating the pyrrole-type nitrogen N-1 would remove its lone pair from the 6π system, which is why this is not done.

The strongest base

Which is the strongest base among: pyridine, aniline, guanidine, piperidine, acetamide? Why?

  1. pKₐH: guanidine 13.6, piperidine 11.1, pyridine 5.2, aniline 4.6, acetamide −0.5.

14.6 Arranging bases: a checklist

  • Step 1: Atom. Compare N, O, S, C bases by the atom carrying the lone pair. N > O.
  • Step 2: Delocalisation. A base whose lone pair is conjugated (amide, aniline, pyrrole) is weaker than one in which it is not (amine, pyridine). If the conjugate acid is stabilised by resonance (amidines), the base is stronger.
  • Step 3: Hybridisation. sp³ > sp² > sp.
  • Step 4: Inductive/steric/solvation effects in the same class.
HYF Anions as bases: the order of basicity is the reverse of the acidity of conjugate acids: NH₂⁻ > OH⁻ > F⁻; CH₃⁻ > NH₂⁻ > OR⁻ ≈ OH⁻ > CH₃COO⁻ > Cl⁻ > I⁻; R⁻ (alkyl) > R₂C=CH⁻ > RC≡C⁻ (carbanions: sp³ > sp² > sp). Bases that are very strong: R⁻ (alkyl-lithiums), NH₂⁻, H⁻, and RO⁻ among the oxygen bases.

Illustration 14.12 Adv

Basic strength of oxygen compounds

Why is dimethyl ether (pKₐH ≈ −3.8) a much weaker base than trimethylamine (9.8)?

  1. Oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen, so its lone pairs are held more tightly and the O–H⁺ bond formed is weaker; the oxonium ion is much less stable than the ammonium ion.
  2. The pKₐH of 10 and −3.8 reflect about 10¹³-fold difference in basicity.
