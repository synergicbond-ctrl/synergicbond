4. Physical properties

State and odour. Methylamine, dimethylamine, trimethylamine and ethylamine are gases at room temperature; higher amines are liquids; the arylamines are liquids or solids. Lower amines have a fishy ammoniacal smell (trimethylamine is the smell of stale fish); aniline is a colourless oil that darkens in air by oxidation.

Boiling points. Boiling points are governed by hydrogen bonding. N–H···N hydrogen bonds are weaker than O–H···O (N is less electronegative than O), so the order for compounds of similar mass is alkane < tertiary amine < secondary amine < primary amine < alcohol < carboxylic acid. Within isomeric amines: 1° > 2° > 3° because a 1° amine has two N–H bonds available for hydrogen bonding (strong association), a 2° has one, and a 3° has none (it can only accept). Branching lowers b.p. (less surface contact).

Trap Isomeric 3° amines boil roughly as low as an alkane of similar mass; do not expect them to have b.p. comparable to a 1° amine. Also note that primary amines have lower b.p. than alcohols of similar mass because the N–H···N bond is weaker than O–H···O.

Solubility in water. Lower amines (up to about 6 carbons for 1°) are soluble because they form hydrogen bonds with water (N···H–O and N–H···O); solubility falls as the hydrophobic alkyl part grows. Aniline is only sparingly soluble (≈3.6 g per 100 mL) because the large hydrocarbon part outweighs hydrogen bonding. Amines are soluble in organic solvents (benzene, ether, alcohol).

Salts. All amines (except very weak ones) dissolve in dilute mineral acids to give soluble ammonium salts, and are regenerated by NaOH. This is the basis of separating amines from neutral and acidic organic compounds. Amine hydrochlorides are crystalline ionic solids, very soluble in water and insoluble in ether.
