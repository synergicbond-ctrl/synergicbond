Chirality checker: must be, can be or cannot be?

Pick a class of compound, set its features, and the checker walks through the four checks and gives the verdict.

    Which term is essential: chiral carbon, asymmetry, dissymmetry, chirality, helicity, chiral axis?

    Only chirality is essential. Every other term is a special case of it or a way of producing it.

    1. Chirality = dissymmetry. ESSENTIAL (necessary). The molecule must be non-superimposable on its mirror image, that is, have no σ, no i and no Sₙ. It may keep rotation axes (C₂, D₂ …). Without it optical activity cannot occur. With it, activity is possible; it is observed only if the form is stable and the sample is not racemic.
    2. Asymmetry (no symmetry at all, point group C₁). Sufficient but not necessary. Every asymmetric molecule is chiral, but many chiral molecules are only dissymmetric: the chiral spirane, biphenyl, allene, helicene and twisted terphenyl models in this studio all keep a C₂ axis. So the correct requirement is dissymmetry (Pasteur’s word), not asymmetry.
    3. Chiral carbon (asymmetric carbon, stereocentre). Neither necessary nor sufficient. Not necessary: allenes, spiranes, hemispiranes, biphenyls, terphenyls and helicenes are active with none. Not sufficient: a meso compound has chiral carbons and a mirror plane.
    4. Chiral axis, helicity, chiral plane, pyramidal atom. Ways of being chiral. Each is sufficient when it is present alone and stable, and none is necessary. Two chiral elements can cancel (meso with two centres, meso terphenyl with two axes), so judge the molecule as a whole.
    Practical order: (1) no σ, i, Sₙ → chiral; (2) find the chiral element; (3) check the form is stable; (4) check the sample is not racemic. Use the checker above to try it for every class.

    What is optical isomerism?

    Two compounds with the same constitution that differ only in 3-D arrangement, and that rotate plane-polarised light, are optical isomers. A molecule is optically active when it is chiral: it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image. The test is symmetry:

    1. A molecule with a mirror plane (σ), a centre of inversion (i) or an alternating axis (Sₙ) is achiral (superimposable on its mirror image).
    2. A molecule with none of these is chiral. It may still have ordinary rotation axes (C₂, C₃ …); these do not stop it being chiral.
    3. The mirror image of a chiral molecule is its enantiomer: identical m.p., b.p. and solubility, equal and opposite optical rotation.
    4. Optical isomers that are not mirror images are diastereomers; they differ in all physical properties.

    CHFClBr and its mirror image. The mirror (dashed line) turns the left molecule into the right one; no rotation can make them identical.

    Criteria for optical activity: must be, can be, cannot be

    Use these words exactly as defined here, and the questions become routine.

    • Chiral = no mirror plane (σ), no centre of inversion (i), no alternating axis (Sₙ). C₂ and other rotation axes do not matter.
    • MUST BE optically active: the molecule is chiral, it is configurationally stable, and the sample is a single enantiomer.
    • CAN BE optically active: it is chiral on paper, but stability or the type of sample is not settled by the question (bulky or small groups, racemic or pure).
    • CANNOT BE optically active: a σ, i or Sₙ is present (achiral, meso), or the two mirror-image forms swap too fast to isolate.
    Absence of symmetry does not guarantee activity. It makes the molecule capable of activity. It still shows no rotation if (1) the forms interconvert fast (amine inversion, [4]helicene, small-group biphenyl), (2) the sample is racemic, (3) the rotation passes through zero at the wavelength used, or (4) the rotation is too small to measure (isotopic chirality).

    The five checks, in this order

    1. Symmetry. σ, i or Sₙ present → cannot be active. Stop.
    2. Chiral element. No σ, i, Sₙ → chiral. Find the element: stereocentre, chiral axis, helix, plane, or pyramidal N/P/S.
    3. Stability. Can the two forms swap quickly? If yes → cannot be resolved.
    4. Sample. Racemic mixture or meso compound → zero rotation. Only a single enantiomer or an enriched sample rotates light.
    5. Counting. n unlike stereogenic units → 2ⁿ stereoisomers. If the molecule has symmetrical halves, some become meso.

    1. Stereocentre (C, Si, Ge, N⁺, P⁺, S⁺)

    • MUST BE chiral: Cabcd, all four groups different, as a single enantiomer.
    • CANNOT BE: Caabc, Caabb, Caaaa (a mirror plane exists).
    • CAN BE: a centre that racemises under the conditions (for example the α-carbon of a ketone with acid or base).
    • A pseudoasymmetric centre (two groups that are an R and an S form of the same thing) occurs only in achiral or specific forms. A compound containing one is often meso.

    2. Two or more stereocentres

    • Unlike ends (A–X–Y–B): 2ⁿ stereoisomers, all chiral. For n = 2 there are 4: two enantiomeric pairs, each pair a set of diastereomers of the other. Each pure isomer MUST BE active.
    • Like ends (A–X–X–A, symmetrical): n even gives 2ⁿ⁻¹ + 2^(n/2 − 1); n odd gives 2ⁿ⁻¹. For n = 2: 3 isomers (a pair plus one meso). For n = 3: 4 (a pair plus two meso). For n = 4: 10.
    • Meso: σ or i present, CANNOT BE active, however many stereocentres it has (internal compensation).
    • Racemic (±): every molecule is chiral, but the mixture gives zero rotation (external compensation). It CAN BE resolved.

    3. Cyclic compounds

    Judge each ring by its average flat shape unless the ring flipping is frozen.

    • 1,2- or 1,3-disubstituted, same groups: cis is meso (CANNOT BE active); trans is a pair of enantiomers (MUST BE chiral, resolvable).
    • 1,4-disubstituted: cis and trans both have a mirror plane (CANNOT BE active). They are only geometric isomers.
    • Different groups: chiral if no plane exists; count centres as in section 2.
    • Ring flipping (cyclohexane) averages the structure, so the flipping conformers are not counted as separate isomers.

    4. Allenes and cumulenes (chiral axis)

    • MUST BE: abC=C=Cab, with a ≠ b on each end carbon.
    • CANNOT BE: either end has two identical groups.
    • Cumulenes: an even number of C=C bonds (allene = 2) gives a chiral axis. An odd number (alkene = 1, butatriene = 3) puts the groups in one plane, so the isomerism is cis/trans, not optical.

    5. Spiranes (chiral axis)

    • MUST BE: each ring is unsymmetrical about the axis through the spiro atom (for example COOH and H on the axis carbon of both rings), as one enantiomer.
    • CANNOT BE: either ring is symmetrical (two identical groups on its axis carbon), because a mirror plane appears.
    • CAN BE chiral in addition if the rings themselves carry stereocentres. Check those separately.

    6. Hemispiranes (alkylidene-cycloalkanes)

    • MUST BE: the exocyclic carbon has two different groups and the ring carbon opposite the C=C has two different groups (for example 4-methylcyclohexylideneacetic acid).
    • CANNOT BE: the exocyclic carbon has two identical groups, or the ring carbon has two identical groups (cyclohexylideneacetic acid, which has H and H on C4).

    7. Biphenyls (atropisomerism)

    All four conditions must hold.

    1. Bulky ortho groups, usually three or four (NO₂, COOH, I, Br, CH₃, OCH₃). F, H and OH are too small.
    2. Each ring unsymmetrical: its two ortho groups must differ. A symmetrical ring (2,6 identical) creates a mirror plane.
    3. High barrier: about 90–100 kJ/mol or more, so the isomers last hours at room temperature.
    4. Not bridged flat: a ring locked planar is achiral.
    • MUST BE: all four hold and the sample is one enantiomer (6,6′-dinitrodiphenic acid).
    • CAN BE: three ortho groups, or two very large ones (binaphthyl compounds are an example). It depends on the size.
    • CANNOT BE: a ring is symmetrical, or the groups are small and the form racemises.

    8. Terphenyls

    • Check every biaryl bond with the biphenyl rules above.
    • p-Terphenyl (X at 2 and 5 of the central ring, Y on one ortho position of each end ring): two stereogenic axes give 2² = 4 stereoisomers if the end rings are different, as two pairs of enantiomers.
    • If the ends are identical and the central ring is symmetrical, there are 3: a pair (MUST BE active) plus one meso form with a centre of symmetry (CANNOT BE active).
    • o-Terphenyl is a twisted helix that flips quickly: CANNOT BE isolated, unless bulky groups or a long ortho-linked chain lock it (CAN BE).

    9. Helicenes and other helices

    • MUST BE: [6]helicene or larger, as one helix (P or M). The barrier is about 150 kJ/mol.
    • CAN BE: [5]helicene. It is chiral, but the barrier of about 100 kJ/mol lets it racemise slowly at room temperature.
    • CANNOT BE isolated: [4]helicene (barrier about 17 kJ/mol).
    • Octahedral tris-chelate complexes such as [Co(en)₃]³⁺ are helical (Δ/Λ) and chiral. cis-[M(AA)₂X₂] is chiral. trans isomers are achiral.

    10. Nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur

    • Amines NR¹R²R³: the nitrogen flips about 10¹⁰ times per second (barrier about 25–35 kJ/mol). CANNOT BE resolved, even with three different groups.
    • CAN BE resolved: quaternary ammonium (four different groups), amine oxides, a nitrogen locked in a rigid frame (Tröger's base), and N in a three-membered ring with a high barrier (aziridines, at low temperature).
    • Phosphines PR¹R²R³ and sulfoxides R–S(=O)–R′ (R ≠ R′): barrier about 100 kJ/mol or more, so they MUST BE chiral and resolvable. The same holds for sulfonium salts.
    • Sulfides, sulfones, and phosphates with identical groups are achiral.

    11. Planar chirality

    • CAN BE: paracyclophanes with the right substituent pattern, ansa compounds, trans-cyclooctene. The ring must be small enough that the chain cannot swing through.
    • CANNOT BE: the ring is large enough for the flipping to occur.

    12. Conformational chirality

    • Conformers that interconvert quickly (gauche butane, chair and boat forms) CANNOT BE counted as isolable isomers.
    • Use the time-averaged shape. It is achiral unless the interconversion is frozen, as in a hindered biphenyl.

    13. Isotopic chirality

    • CAN BE chiral by isotopes alone (CHDTX, or a CH₂D–R pattern). It is chiral in principle (cryptochirality), but the rotation is usually too small to measure.

    Common traps

    • "No symmetry, so active": wrong when the sample is racemic or the form flips fast.
    • "Has stereocentres, so active": wrong for meso compounds.
    • "No stereocentre, so inactive": wrong for allenes, spiranes, hemispiranes, biphenyls and helicenes.
    • A C₂ axis does not make a molecule achiral.

    Quick revision

    1. Chiral = no σ, no i, no Sₙ = non-superimposable on its mirror image = optically active. C₂ axes are allowed.
    2. PPL: E vibrates in one plane. It is the sum of equal clockwise and anticlockwise circular beams. A chiral medium has nL ≠ nR, which turns the plane by α = π l (nL − nR) / λ. Malus: I = I₀cos²β.
    3. Clockwise looking at the source = (+) dextro. Enantiomers: equal angle, opposite sign. Racemic: external compensation. Meso: internal compensation.
    4. Polarimeter: crossed analyser = dark; sample turns the plane = bright; turn the analyser by α = dark again. Half-shade: set equal halves. [α] = α / (l·c).
    5. Spirane: different groups on the axis atom of both rings; rings perpendicular; chiral axis.
    6. Hemispirane: different groups on the exocyclic carbon and on the ring carbon opposite the C=C.
    7. Biphenyl: 3–4 bulky ortho groups (blocked rotation) and each ring unsymmetrical (ortho groups differ).
    8. Terphenyl: each stereogenic axis obeys the biphenyl rule; two axes give up to 4 stereoisomers (3, including a meso form with a centre of symmetry, if the molecule is symmetrical).
    9. Helicity: a coiled backbone (helicene, twisted o-terphenyl) is chiral as P or M; it is stable only if the helix cannot unwind (barrier ≳ 100 kJ/mol, as in [6]helicene).
    10. Amines: the pyramid flips through a planar, achiral transition state (barrier about 25–35 kJ mol⁻¹), so chiral amines racemise at once, unless N is quaternary, locked in a frame (Tröger's base) or strained in a ring.

    The rules, step by step

    1. Rank the four groups at each stereocentre with the CIP rules. Keep each priority attached to its atom, whatever the drawing.
    2. Fischer: horizontal bonds point toward you, vertical bonds away. Turning the formula 180° in the page gives the same molecule. Turning it 90° does not. Swapping any two groups at a centre inverts it.
    3. Lowest priority toward you (a horizontal group in Fischer, a solid wedge): trace 1 → 2 → 3, then reverse the answer.
    4. Lowest priority away (hashed wedge, vertical in a one-centre Fischer): read 1 → 2 → 3 directly, clockwise = R.
    5. Newman and sawhorse: one of the four groups points along the line of sight (the other carbon). Trace the other three in priority order. If that group has priority 2 and points away, read directly; if it points toward you, reverse. If its priority is 3 (or 1), reverse once more. The reading box above applies exactly this.
    6. Converting between drawings is only a change of viewpoint: put the same atom on the same carbon, keep each group on the same side relative to the bond, and recheck R/S afterwards.

    The naming trap

    (3R,2R) is the same as (2R,3R): the locants refer to the same numbered atoms and have simply been written in reverse order. Always put the locants in ascending order before comparing two names, and never compare just the printed order of the letters.

    1. (2R,3R) and (2S,3S): both centres inverted, so enantiomers.
    2. (2R,3R) and (2R,3S): one centre changed, diastereomers.
    3. (2R,3S) and (2S,3R) in a symmetrical molecule: identical (one meso compound). In an unsymmetrical one (2-bromo-3-chlorobutane) they are enantiomers.

    Plane-polarised light (PPL)

    Light is a transverse electromagnetic wave. The electric vector E and magnetic vector B are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. In ordinary (unpolarised) light E vibrates in all planes around the beam, changing randomly. In plane-polarised light E vibrates in one plane only.

    1. Ordinary light from a lamp is a mixture of many waves, each vibrating in its own plane (animation: the coloured ribbons on the left).
    2. A polariser contains a preferred direction. It lets through only the component of E along its transmission axis and removes the rest.
    3. After the polariser, one ribbon remains: all vibrations lie in a single plane, the plane of vibration. (Historically the "plane of polarisation" is the plane perpendicular to this, the one containing B.)
    4. An unpolarised beam loses half its intensity at an ideal polariser: I = I₀/2.

    Side view, drawn in perspective. Light travels to the right. Each translucent ribbon is the plane in which E vibrates. The slotted plate is the polariser (the slots show its transmission axis). The circles underneath show the same light end-on, as the eye sees it.

    Ways of producing plane-polarised light

    1. Nicol prism (calcite): calcite splits a ray into an ordinary (o) and an extraordinary (e) ray. The prism is cut and cemented with Canada balsam (n = 1.55). The o-ray (no = 1.658) meets the balsam beyond the critical angle (about 69°) and is totally reflected onto a blackened face. The e-ray (ne = 1.486) goes straight through, polarised in one plane.
    2. Polaroid sheet: a stretched polymer (poly-vinyl alcohol) dyed with iodine. The aligned chains absorb E parallel to them and transmit E perpendicular to them.
    3. Reflection at the Brewster angle, tan θB = n (about 56° for glass): the reflected beam is completely polarised, with E perpendicular to the plane of incidence.
    4. Double refraction (calcite, quartz) and scattering also give polarised light.

    Schematic Nicol prism (not to scale).

    Malus's law: the analyser

    A second polariser, the analyser, turned by an angle β from the first, passes only the component of E along its own axis: amplitude E₀ cos β, so the intensity is

    I = I₀ cos²β

    β = 0° gives full transmission, β = 45° gives half, β = 90° (crossed) gives no light. A polarimeter uses this dark, crossed setting as its reference.

    Turn the analyser. After it, the wave vibrates in the analyser's plane with amplitude cos β. The graph shows I/I₀ = cos²β and the moving dot is your setting.

    Optical rotation

    When PPL passes through an optically active substance (a pure liquid or a solution), its plane of vibration is rotated by an angle α.

    1. Look toward the light source (light coming at your eye).
    2. If the plane turns clockwise, the substance is dextrorotatory, labelled (+) or d.
    3. If it turns anticlockwise, it is laevorotatory, labelled (−) or l.
    4. The two enantiomers rotate by the same angle in opposite directions under identical conditions.

    The ribbon (the plane of vibration) twists gradually while the light crosses the tube, and leaves turned by α. The dials show the result as the observer sees it.

    Biot's laws and specific rotation

    • α is proportional to the path length l and to the concentration c of the active substance.
    • α depends on wavelength (roughly ∝ 1/λ²; this is optical rotatory dispersion), on temperature and on solvent. So the sodium D line (589 nm) is used.
    [α]λt = αobs / ( l × c )   (l in dm, c in g mL⁻¹)
    • If c is in g per 100 mL, use [α] = 100·α / (l·c).
    • Written like [α]D20 = +66.5° (sucrose in water). Others: D-glucose +52.7° (equilibrium), D-fructose −92°.
    • Mutarotation: fresh α-D-glucose (+112°) or β-D-glucose (+19°) drifts to +52.7° as the two forms interconvert in water.

    Cancelling cases (no net rotation)

    • Racemic mixture (±): 1 : 1 mixture of enantiomers; rotations cancel (external compensation). It can be resolved into (+) and (−).
    • Meso compound: a single molecule with a mirror plane or centre of symmetry; one half cancels the other (internal compensation). Cannot be resolved.
    • Optical purity (enantiomeric excess) = [α]observed / [α]pure × 100 %.
    (+) / (−) is measured by experiment. It has no fixed relation to D / L (relative configuration) or to R / S (CIP rules).

    How is the plane of PPL rotated?

    The key idea: plane-polarised light is the sum of two circularly polarised beams of equal amplitude, one whose E vector spirals clockwise and one anticlockwise (two helices of opposite handedness). Their vector sum always lies in one fixed plane.

    1. Split the PPL into a clockwise (R, pink) and an anticlockwise (L, blue) circular component. In the animation the two helices add to the dark planar wave.
    2. In an achiral medium both travel at the same speed (nL = nR), stay in step, and the resultant stays in its original plane. No rotation.
    3. A chiral molecule is "handed" (like a screw thread), so it interacts differently with the two helices: nL ≠ nR.
    4. One component therefore travels slower; its phase lags more and more as it goes through the tube (the lag grows with path length l).
    5. On leaving the sample the two components recombine into a plane-polarised wave whose plane is turned by half the phase difference.
    6. In formula form: α = π l (nL − nR) / λ (radians). If nL > nR the rotation is clockwise, (+).
    7. The enantiomer swaps the roles of L and R, so it gives exactly −α.
    8. An achiral molecule, in every orientation, has a mirror-image orientation that cancels its effect, so liquids of achiral molecules do not rotate. A chiral molecule's mirror image is a different molecule, so there is no cancellation.

    Blue and pink helices are the two circular components; the dark ribbon is their sum. Inside the tube the pink helix is delayed, so the dark plane swings.

    The same thing end-on. Set α = 0: the resultant just oscillates along the vertical dashed line. Move α: one component is delayed by 2α, and the line of vibration swings by α, half of that.

    Measuring the angle: the polarimeter

    Parts, in the order the light meets them

    1. Monochromatic source: sodium lamp (589 nm).
    2. Polariser (fixed Nicol) makes the PPL.
    3. Sample tube of known length l (1 or 2 dm) filled with solution of known concentration c.
    4. Analyser: a second Nicol that can be rotated, fixed to a graduated circular scale with a vernier.
    5. Eyepiece / telescope, usually with a half-shade (Laurent) plate covering half the field.

    Why is the field dark or bright?

    1. The polariser passes only light vibrating in one plane (take it as vertical, 0°).
    2. Dark (zero reading). The analyser is turned to 90° to the polariser ("crossed"). Its axis is horizontal, but the light vibrates vertically, so the component along the analyser axis is cos 90° = 0. No light passes: the field is dark.
    3. Bright (sample inserted). The optically active sample turns the vibration plane by α. Now the light is tilted by α from vertical, so it has a component sin α along the analyser axis. The analyser passes I₀ sin²α. Light gets through: the field turns bright, and the larger α is, the brighter it is (maximum at 90°).
    4. Dark again (measurement). Turn the analyser by the same angle α so that its axis is again at 90° to the new vibration plane. The component along it is zero again, the field goes dark, and the angle you turned it through is the rotation α.
    5. Why a half-shade? Near the dark setting the brightness changes only slowly (I ∝ (α − reading)²), so the eye cannot find the darkest point to better than a few degrees. In a half-shade instrument a small plate tilts the vibration plane in the two halves of the field by +ε and −ε. They pass sin²(δ + ε) and sin²(δ − ε), where δ = α − reading. They are equal only at δ = 0, and the eye is extremely sensitive to a brightness step between neighbouring halves, so the end-point can be set to a fraction of a degree. At the end-point both halves are equally, faintly lit (not black).

    Procedure

    1. With the empty (or solvent-filled) tube, rotate the analyser to the crossed position: dark field, or equal halves in a half-shade instrument. Note the scale reading: the zero reading.
    2. Fill the tube with the solution. The plain field brightens and the half-shade field shows unequal halves.
    3. Rotate the analyser until the original dark / equal-halves appearance returns.
    4. The angle through which the analyser was turned is the observed rotation α: clockwise = (+), anticlockwise = (−).
    5. Repeat several times, take the mean and compute [α] = α / (l·c).
    6. Ambiguity of 180°: a reading of +30° could also be −150°. Halve the concentration: a true +30° gives +15°, a true −150° gives −75°.

    The analyser starts crossed (reading 0°, empty tube: dark). Turn it and read the live explanation above. (Brightness of the eyepiece circles is exaggerated for visibility.)

    Optical isomerism in spiranes

    A spirane has two rings joined at a single common atom (the spiro atom, sp³). The two rings lie in planes at right angles. Replace both C=C bonds of an allene by rings and you get a spirane; the chirality has the same origin: a chiral axis.

    Condition for optical activity

    1. The chiral axis runs through the spiro atom and the atom of each ring that lies opposite it (C2, C4, C6 in spiro[3.3]heptane).
    2. On each ring, the two groups on the axis atom must be different (here COOH and H on C2, and COOH and H on C6).
    3. Because the rings are perpendicular, no mirror plane survives. The molecule is chiral.
    4. If either ring carries two identical groups (COOH, COOH), a mirror plane exists and the compound is achiral.

    Classic example: spiro[3.3]heptane-2,6-dicarboxylic acid. C2 is not a stereocentre (its two ring arms are the same), yet the molecule is resolvable.

    Hemispiranes (alkylidene-cycloalkanes)

    A hemispirane is the half-way molecule between an allene and a spirane: one end is a ring, the other is an exocyclic C=C–(a,b). The chiral axis runs along the C=C and through the ring (C1 to C4).

    allene (a,b at both ends) → hemispirane (one end = ring) → spirane (both ends = rings)

    Condition for optical activity

    1. The exocyclic carbon of the C=C must carry two different groups (COOH and H).
    2. The ring atom opposite the double bond (C4) must carry two different groups (CH₃ and H).
    3. The groups on the exocyclic carbon lie in the plane of the ring (all the sp² atoms are coplanar), while those on C4 lie above and below the ring. The two end planes are perpendicular, as in allenes.
    4. No mirror plane is left, so the compound is chiral. If the exocyclic carbon has two identical groups, or C4 has two identical groups, a mirror plane appears and it is achiral.

    Example: 4-methylcyclohexylideneacetic acid. It has no stereocentre (C4's two ring arms are identical) yet it is chiral.

    The ring is drawn flat in the paper. The exocyclic carbon's COOH and H lie in the paper (blue); the CH₃ and H on C4 stick out of the paper. Right: mirror image.

    Allenes: the parent, and the symmetry rules

    Allenes (C=C=C) are the model for all the axial cases. The two C=C bonds are at 90°, so the groups at one end lie in a plane perpendicular to the groups at the other end. Spiranes, hemispiranes and biphenyls are allene-like in exactly this sense.

    1. Each end carbon needs two different groups (here CH₃ and H) for the molecule to be chiral.
    2. If either end carries two identical groups there is a mirror plane and the allene is achiral.
    3. The page finds all the symmetry elements of the model by testing every operation that swaps or flips the x, y, z axes (48 operations), then lists them under the animation.
    4. Choose "Symmetry operation" in the test menu to watch a mirror, an inversion or a C₂ rotation being carried out on the molecule, and see whether it reproduces itself.

    Rule to remember: a molecule is chiral when it has no σ, no i and no Sₙ. It is allowed to have C₂ axes. The chiral models here (allene, spirane, biphenyl) keep one C₂ axis (point group C₂) and are still chiral.

    Optical isomerism in biphenyls (atropisomerism)

    Biphenyl has no stereocentre, but when rotation about the C1–C1′ single bond is blocked, the molecule is frozen in a twisted shape that can be chiral. Stereoisomers produced by hindered rotation are called atropisomers (a = not, tropos = turning).

    Conditions

    1. Bulky ortho groups: usually three, and best four, large groups in the 2, 2′, 6, 6′ positions (such as –NO₂, –COOH, –OCH₃, –I, –Br, –CH₃). They collide if the rings try to become coplanar. Small groups (F, H, OH) are not enough.
    2. The rings are therefore twisted, close to perpendicular, and cannot rotate past each other at room temperature.
    3. Each ring must be unsymmetrical: its two ortho groups must differ (2 ≠ 6 and 2′ ≠ 6′).
    4. Why? If one ring has identical ortho groups (e.g. 2,6-dinitro), the plane of the other ring is a mirror plane, so the molecule is achiral (tick "ring A ortho groups identical" below and watch it).
    5. Barrier: roughly 90–100 kJ mol⁻¹ or more is needed so that isomers survive for hours (t½ ≥ about 1000 s at room temperature).

    Classic example: 6,6′-dinitrodiphenic acid (2,2′-dicarboxy-6,6′-dinitrobiphenyl), the first biphenyl to be resolved (Christie and Kenner, 1922). Each ring carries NO₂ and COOH at its ortho positions.

    Optical isomerism in terphenyls

    A terphenyl has three benzene rings in a chain (o-, m- or p-), so it has two biaryl axes. Each axis follows the biphenyl rules, and the number of stereoisomers depends on how many axes are stereogenic.

    1. The two biaryl bonds of p-terphenyl are collinear: they lie on one common chiral axis running through all three rings (green dashed line in the drawings and in the 3-D model). Each biaryl bond is its own stereogenic element (axis 1 and axis 2, orange).
    2. For an axis to be stereogenic, rotation about it must be blocked by bulky ortho groups.
    3. The two rings flanking that axis must each be unsymmetrical (their ortho groups must differ).
    4. In p-terphenyl the central ring sits between both axes. A central ring with bulky groups X at 2 and 5 is unsymmetrical at both axes. Each end ring carries one bulky ortho group Y.
    5. Each axis can be set one of two ways (Y up or Y down relative to the central ring), so with two axes there are up to 2² = 4 combinations.
    6. If the two end rings are different, all four are distinct: two pairs of enantiomers (the pairs are diastereomers, "syn" and "anti").
    7. If the end rings are identical (and the central ring is 2,5-symmetric), the molecule behaves like tartaric acid: 3 stereoisomers, a pair of enantiomers plus one meso form.

    Optical isomerism due to helicity (helicenes and twisted terphenyls)

    A molecule can be chiral simply because its whole backbone is coiled like a screw or a spring. This is helical chirality: no stereocentre, and no single chiral axis, only a helix. A right-handed helix is called P (plus), a left-handed one M (minus). The two are mirror images, cannot be superimposed, and are enantiomers.

    Helicenes

    1. A [n]helicene is made of n benzene rings fused ortho to each other, always turning the same way (like phenanthrene, benzo[c]phenanthrene …).
    2. With four or more rings the two ends crowd each other: the inner hydrogens would collide if the molecule were flat.
    3. To relieve the strain the rings stack above each other and the molecule coils into a helix, right-handed (P) or left-handed (M).
    4. The helix is rigid: to turn P into M the molecule would have to pass through a strained, nearly planar form. The barrier grows with n: about 17 kJ/mol for [4]helicene (cannot be resolved), about 100 kJ/mol for [5]helicene (racemises slowly) and about 150 kJ/mol for [6]helicene (stable).
    5. Because the molecule is itself a screw, it interacts very differently with the clockwise and anticlockwise circular components of light (see “How is the plane of PPL rotated?” in the Light tab). The specific rotation of [6]helicene is huge, of the order of ±3700°, thousands of times larger than for a typical chiral carbon compound.
    6. The helix has a C₂ axis (perpendicular to the helix axis) but no mirror plane and no centre, so it is chiral. Look for this in the model: the symmetry search finds only C₂.

    Twisted terphenyls (o-terphenyl)

    1. In o-terphenyl both outer rings sit on neighbouring carbons of the central ring and cannot all lie flat.
    2. Both outer rings therefore twist in the same sense about their bonds to the central ring. The result is a propeller or short helix with a C₂ axis, P or M.
    3. For plain o-terphenyl this twist reverses very easily, so it is a racemic, rapidly flipping mixture and shows no optical activity.
    4. It becomes resolvable only if bulky groups (or a long chain of ortho-linked rings, an ortho-phenylene oligomer) lock the helix, exactly as bulky ortho groups lock a biphenyl.

    Amine inversion (the "umbrella flip")

    A tertiary or secondary amine NR¹R²R³ is pyramidal: the nitrogen is sp³ and its fourth "substituent" is a lone pair. With three different groups the molecule has no mirror plane, so it looks chiral. Yet such amines cannot be resolved, because the molecule turns inside out like an umbrella in a gale.

    1. Start with the pyramid: N at the top, three groups as the ribs, lone pair pointing up.
    2. The nitrogen moves toward the plane of the three groups while the ribs flatten.
    3. At the top of the barrier N is planar and sp²; the lone pair sits in a p orbital. This planar form has a mirror plane (σh, the plane of N and its three substituents): it is achiral.
    4. N then emerges on the other side: the lone pair now points down. That is the mirror image of the first pyramid (the flip is a reflection in σh; the dashed ghost shows it).
    5. No bond is broken. The barrier is only about 25–35 kJ mol⁻¹ for simple amines (ammonia ≈ 24 kJ mol⁻¹), so the flip happens roughly 10¹⁰–10¹¹ times per second at room temperature.
    6. The two enantiomers are in rapid equilibrium, so the amine is always racemic and shows no optical activity.

    Left: the real molecule flipping (N dark blue, C grey, H white; the translucent lobes are the lone pair), with the amber σh plane and a dashed mirror-image ghost. Right: energy against the position of N. Change the amine to see which symmetry elements the pyramid has (amber planes, C₃ axis). Drag to turn the model.

    When can a nitrogen stereocentre be resolved?

    • Quaternary ammonium salts (four different groups on N⁺) and amine oxides: no lone pair, so nothing to flip.
    • Tröger's base: the nitrogens sit at bridgeheads of a rigid frame, so they cannot invert. It was resolved by Prelog and Wieland (1944).
    • Aziridines (N in a three-membered ring): the planar transition state is strained, so the barrier is much higher and invertomers can be separated.
    • For comparison, phosphines and sulfoxides invert far more slowly (barriers of the order of 100 kJ mol⁻¹ or more), so chiral ones are resolvable.
