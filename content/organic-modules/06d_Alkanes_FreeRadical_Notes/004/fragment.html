5. Free-radical chain halogenation: the mechanism

Alkanes react with chlorine or bromine in diffuse light, UV light or at about 520–670 K to give alkyl halides and hydrogen halide: R–H + X2 → R–X + HX. The reaction is a radical chain substitution. Light (or heat) supplies the energy to cleave the weakest bond, the X–X bond, homolytically; each unpaired electron is carried by one fragment.

  1. Initiation: Cl–Cl → 2 Cl• (homolysis by hν; ΔH about +243 kJ mol−1). This step makes radicals but consumes none.
  2. Propagation 1: CH4 + Cl• → CH3• + HCl. The chlorine atom abstracts a hydrogen atom.
  3. Propagation 2: CH3• + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•. The product radical attacks Cl2 and regenerates Cl•, which restarts the cycle.
  4. Termination: Cl• + Cl• → Cl2; CH3• + Cl• → CH3Cl; CH3• + CH3• → C2H6. Radicals disappear in pairs; ethane found in the product mixture is evidence for the radical mechanism.

Reactant:

Reagent: Cl2, hν (excess CH4)

Product:

With excess chlorine the substitution continues: CH3Cl → CH2Cl2 → CHCl3 → CCl4. Excess alkane favours the monohalide.

Key idea. The propagation steps are the two that repeat; they are 2 per cycle. One photon can create thousands of product molecules (chain length). A small amount of O2 or an inhibitor scavenges radicals and causes an induction period.
Trap. Each substitution consumes exactly one X2 and releases one HX. Replacing all six hydrogens of ethane needs 6 mol of Cl2 and gives 6 mol of HCl, not 3.
