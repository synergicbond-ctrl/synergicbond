14.1 Empirical and molecular formula

  • Divide each percentage by the atomic mass to get relative moles (or take the masses directly if the data are given in grams).
  • Divide all by the smallest number.
  • If the ratios are not integers multiply by 2, 3, 4 …: 1.5 → ×2, 1.33 → ×3, 1.25 → ×4, 1.67 → ×3 (the usual fractions; do not round 1.33 to 1).
  • This gives the empirical formula (simplest whole-number ratio). Its mass is the empirical formula mass.
  • Molecular formula = (empirical formula) × n, where n = molecular mass / empirical formula mass. The molecular mass comes from vapour density (M = 2 × V.D., with V.D. relative to hydrogen), a colligative property, a Victor Meyer experiment, mass spectrometry, or the titration methods of Section 19.
Example 22 Main

Empirical and molecular formula

A compound contains C 40.0 %, H 6.7 %, O 53.3 %. Its vapour density is 90. Find the molecular formula.

Show solution
  1. Relative moles: C 40.0/12 = 3.33; H 6.7/1 = 6.7; O 53.3/16 = 3.33. Divide by 3.33: C 1 : H 2.0 : O 1. Empirical formula CH2O, mass 30.
  2. Molecular mass = 2 × 90 = 180; n = 180/30 = 6. Molecular formula C6H12O6 (e.g. glucose).
Example 23 Adv

Four elements

Paracetamol contains C 63.6 %, H 6.0 %, N 9.3 %, O 21.1 % (by mass). Find the empirical formula, and the molecular formula if M = 151.

Show solution
  1. C 63.6/12 = 5.30; H 6.0/1 = 6.0; N 9.3/14 = 0.664; O 21.1/16 = 1.32.
  2. Divide by 0.664: C 7.98, H 9.0, N 1.00, O 1.99 ⇒ C : H : N : O = 8 : 9 : 1 : 2. Empirical formula C8H9NO2 (mass 151).
  3. n = 151/151 = 1, so the molecular formula is also C8H9NO2.
Exam Trap Never divide percentages by atomic mass and then stop at "1.33 : 1": always clear fractions. A ratio such as C 1 : H 1.33 : O 0.67 means C3H4O2 (×3 and then ×1.5 would be wrong); when two non-integral values appear (1.33 and 0.67) divide by the smaller first and then multiply as needed.
