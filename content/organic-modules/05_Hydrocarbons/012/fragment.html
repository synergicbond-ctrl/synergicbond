11. Ring strain: Baeyer theory and its failure

11.1 Baeyer strain theory (1885)

Baeyer assumed (i) rings are planar, (ii) the normal tetrahedral angle 109°28′ is the ideal, and (iii) the strain in a ring arises from the deviation of each bond from this value, shared equally by the two bonds at an atom:

Formula Angle deviation d = ½ (109°28′ − internal angle of the planar regular polygon)
Baeyer angle strain for planar rings
RingInternal angled (deviation per bond)Predicted stability
Cyclopropane60°24°44′most strained
Cyclobutane90°9°44′strained
Cyclopentane108°0°44′nearly strain-free (predicted most stable)
Cyclohexane120°−5°16′predicted strained (opposite sign)
Cycloheptane128°34′−9°33′predicted more strained

Baeyer's theory is correct for 3, 4 and (roughly) 5-membered rings, but wrong for six-membered and larger rings because those rings are not planar: they pucker to keep all angles near 109.5°. Cyclohexane has essentially zero angle strain in the chair; the rings of 7 or more carbons also pucker, with only small strain.

Trap Do not apply Baeyer's formula to rings larger than 5 and expect real strain energies. In JEE the question "which cycloalkane is most stable by Baeyer theory" has the answer cyclopentane (smallest |d|), while "which is actually the most stable" has the answer cyclohexane (chair, zero strain).

11.2 Measured strain: heats of combustion

Strain is measured by comparing the heat of combustion per CH2 with that of a strain-free reference (658.6 kJ mol-1 per CH2, as in the long-chain alkane or cyclohexane).

Total strain = (value per CH2 − 658.6) × n. Check: cyclopropane (697 − 658.6) × 3 = 115 kJ; cyclobutane 27.4 × 4 = 110; cyclopentane 5.4 × 5 = 27; consistent with the table. Ring strain is the sum of: angle strain (bond angles deviating from 109.5°), torsional strain (eclipsed bonds) and steric (transannular) strain (non-bonded repulsion across a ring, which is large in 8–11 membered rings).

Example 15 Foundation

Strain energy from combustion data

The heat of combustion of cyclopentane is 3320 kJ mol-1. Calculate its total strain energy using 658.6 kJ mol-1 per CH2 for a strain-free unit, and decide whether cyclohexane (3951 kJ mol-1) is strained.

Show solution
  1. Strain-free value for five CH2: 5 × 658.6 = 3293 kJ.
  2. Cyclopentane strain = 3320 − 3293 = 27 kJ mol-1.
  3. Cyclohexane: 6 × 658.6 = 3951.6 ≈ 3951 → strain ≈ 0; it is the strain-free reference.

11.3 Cyclopropane: bent bonds and chemical consequences

  • C–C–C = 60°, but the best overlap lines of the sp3-like orbitals subtend about 104°: the electron density lies outside the internuclear axis: bent ("banana") bonds. The C–C bond (151 pm) is weak (≈ 255 kJ mol-1) and has partial π character.
  • The six C–H bonds are eclipsed (torsional strain); H–C–H ≈ 115°. The C–H bonds have extra s-character (≈ sp2), so they are shorter and stronger and the C–H is more acidic than in alkanes.
  • Hence cyclopropane behaves like an alkene in some reactions: it undergoes ring-opening addition with H2/Ni (80 °C), HBr, Br2/FeBr3, conc. H2SO4 (but does not decolourise Br2/CCl4 in the dark readily, nor KMnO4).

Example 16 Advanced

Product of ring opening

Predict the product(s) of (a) 1,1-dimethylcyclopropane + HBr (b) cyclobutane + H2/Ni at 200 °C (c) cyclopentane + HBr.

Show solution
  1. (a) Protonation at a CH2; cleavage at the bond to the quaternary carbon, which becomes a tertiary cation: CH3–C+(CH3)–CH2CH3 → 2-bromo-2-methylbutane.
  2. (b) n-Butane.
  3. (c) No reaction (the five-membered ring is essentially strain-free).
