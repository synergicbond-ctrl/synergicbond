2. Anti-periplanar geometry and stereospecific E2

In the E2 transition state the four atoms H–C–C–X lie in one plane. Two arrangements are possible: anti-periplanar (H and X on opposite sides, dihedral angle 180°) and syn-periplanar (same side, 0°). Anti is strongly preferred because it is staggered, avoids electrostatic repulsion between the base and the leaving group, and gives the best overlap of the departing σ orbitals with the developing p orbitals.

The consequence is stereospecificity: when the β-carbon has only one hydrogen, the configuration of the substrate fixes the geometry of the alkene. Two diastereomers of the same constitution give alkenes that are E/Z isomers of each other.

  1. Draw the Newman projection along the Cα–Cβ bond and rotate until the leaving group and the chosen β-hydrogen are 180° apart.
  2. Read off which substituents on the two carbons are now on the same side of the plane; those become cis in the alkene.
  3. Assign E or Z with the CIP priority rules at each alkene carbon.

The classic meso versus racemic contrast follows the same logic. meso-2,3-Dibromobutane and its racemic (dl) diastereomer, treated with alcoholic base, remove HBr to give the two stereoisomeric 2-bromobut-2-enes: the meso compound gives (E)-2-bromobut-2-ene and the dl pair gives (Z)-2-bromobut-2-ene, because in each case the anti-periplanar H/Br conformer places the two methyl groups on a different side.

Key idea. Stereospecific means different stereoisomers of the reactant give different stereoisomers of the product through a mechanism with a fixed geometry. Always check whether the β-carbon has one H (outcome fixed) or two H (both alkene geometries accessible, the more stable one predominates).
