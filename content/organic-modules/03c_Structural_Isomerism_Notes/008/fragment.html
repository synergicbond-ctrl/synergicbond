9. A systematic method for enumerating isomers

Random drawing misses structures and repeats others. A reliable procedure has four stages: (1) compute the degrees of unsaturation and list which feature(s) will carry them; (2) build the carbon skeletons, longest chain first, then each shorter parent chain with branches placed at distinct positions; (3) place the functional group, double bond or ring on every non-equivalent position of each skeleton; (4) rename or canonicalise each structure and cross out duplicates and mirror-image drawings of the same compound. Finally check the count against the formula, and apply any restriction in the question (aromatic only, acyclic only, optically active only).

Worked example: alcohols of C4H10O. The DoU is zero, and a single OH group replaces one hydrogen of butane or 2-methylpropane. The skeletons are butane and 2-methylpropane. Butane has two non-equivalent carbons (end and inner) giving two alcohols; 2-methylpropane has two non-equivalent carbons (CH3 and CH) giving two alcohols. There are four alcohols in all; one of them (butan-2-ol) is chiral.

Butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol.

  1. Step 1: C4H10O has DoU = 0, so only single bonds and no rings.
  2. Step 2: skeletons of four carbons: butane and 2-methylpropane.
  3. Step 3: add OH on each different carbon: butane gives C1 or C2; 2-methylpropane gives the CH3 or the CH carbon.
  4. Step 4: no duplicates, so the alcohol count is 4. Adding the three ethers (CH3OC3H7, CH3OCH(CH3)2, C2H5OC2H5) gives 7 structural isomers of C4H10O in total.

For C4H8 the single DoU is placed as a C=C (but-1-ene, but-2-ene, 2-methylpropene) or as a ring (cyclobutane, methylcyclopropane): 5 structural isomers.

Key idea. Skeleton first, group second, duplicates last. Always separate the questions 'how many different structures?' and 'how many stereoisomers?': a constitutional count first, stereo second.
Trap. Drawing the same chain bent differently is not a new skeleton. A chain drawn with a kink is still butane. Use the longest-chain rule and number from the end that gives the lower locants to confirm two drawings differ.
